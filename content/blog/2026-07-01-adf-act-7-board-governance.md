## Part 1: Why Governance Is Not a Bureaucratic Afterthought

There is a tendency in discussions of economic institutions to treat governance as the boring administrative wrapper around the "real" work — the investment decisions, the financial instruments, the project pipelines. This tendency is wrong and dangerous.

Governance — who makes decisions, on what basis, with what accountability, under what constraints — is the most important determinant of whether an institution succeeds or fails. The history of development finance institutions worldwide is a history of institutions that had excellent mandates, adequate capital, and sophisticated financial instruments, but failed because their governance was captured, corrupted, or simply incompetent.

The Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 devotes four complete sections (Sections 10 through 17) to the governance of the fund. This is not excessive; it reflects an appropriate recognition that the board's design is as important as the fund's capitalization or its investment mandate. This article examines every provision of the board's design in detail.

## Part 2: The Composition of the Board

Section 10 establishes the Board of Directors (Sanchalak Samiti) — the supreme governing body of the fund. The board has the following composition:

**The Secretary of the Finance Ministry — Chair**

The chair of the board is not elected; it is automatically the Secretary of the Finance Ministry — the most senior civil servant in Nepal's most powerful ministry. This person is appointed by the government and serves at the government's pleasure.

Placing the Finance Ministry Secretary at the head of the board has both logic and risks. The logic: this person has the deepest knowledge of government financial policy, the most direct channel to the Finance Minister, and the institutional authority to represent the government's interests in the fund's governance. Having this person chair the board ensures the fund remains aligned with national financial policy.

The risks: the Finance Ministry Secretary is not an independent actor. They are a government appointee who will follow the government's direction. If the government of the day wants to steer the fund's investments for political reasons, the Secretary-Chair is not structurally positioned to resist. Moreover, the Secretary is busy with many other duties — they run one of the largest ministries in Nepal. The chair of a major development finance institution probably requires full-time engagement; a Finance Ministry Secretary cannot provide that.

**Joint Secretary of the Finance Ministry — Director**

One joint secretary (one level below secretary in the civil service hierarchy) from the Finance Ministry serves on the board as a director. This provides continuity and technical depth from the ministry.

**Two Joint Secretaries from Infrastructure Ministries — Directors**

Two joint secretaries from ministries involved in infrastructure development (and specifically with engineering service backgrounds) are designated as directors by the Finance Ministry. This provision ensures that people with technical knowledge of infrastructure — not just financial knowledge — sit on the board and can evaluate infrastructure projects on their technical merits.

**Representative from the Retirement Fund / Pension Fund Shareholder Group — Director**

One representative of the Category B shareholders (the pension and retirement funds) sits on the board. How the rotation among these shareholders works is specified in Section 10, Sub-section 2: the representation rotates among the group members every two years, with the order determined by agreement among the group. If they cannot agree, rotation follows alphabetical order.

**Representative from the Insurance Company Shareholder Group — Director**

One representative of the Category C shareholders (insurance companies) sits on the board on the same rotational basis.

**One Independent Expert Director — Director**

One independent expert director is appointed by the board itself, based on qualifications specified in Section 11. The term of this director is two years, renewable once. The independent director brings outside expertise and is intended to be free from both government and shareholder interests.

**The CEO — Permanent Invitee**

The Chief Executive Officer (CEO) attends board meetings as a permanent invitee — someone always present and participating, but without voting rights. The CEO is responsible for bringing information to the board, implementing board decisions, and managing the day-to-day operations. Making the CEO a non-voting participant in board meetings is a standard corporate governance structure that separates management (execution) from governance (oversight and strategy).

### What the Board Composition Tells Us

This board is predominantly government-and-shareholder composed, with a single independent director. Seven of the eight board participants have direct connections to either the government (the three ministry officials) or the institutional shareholders (two shareholder representatives), or are staff (the CEO). Only the independent expert director is structurally independent.

For comparison, international best practices for development finance institution governance typically recommend that independent directors represent a substantial minority or even a majority of the board. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision and the OECD Principles of Corporate Governance both emphasize the importance of board independence. A board that is predominantly government and stakeholder-composed is susceptible to representing those interests to the exclusion of good commercial and fiduciary judgment.

This board composition is not necessarily fatal to good governance, but it does place significant weight on:
1. The integrity and judgment of the government-appointed members
2. The quality and independence of the single independent director
3. The quality and authority of the CEO who, though non-voting, will be the primary driver of operational decisions
4. The quality of the sub-committees (more on these below)

## Part 3: Qualifications for the Independent Expert Director

Section 11 specifies the qualifications required for the independent expert director. This person must:

- Be a Nepali citizen
- Have at least a postgraduate degree in economics, management, commerce, development studies, law, engineering, financial analysis, or financial science
- AND either:
  - Have at least 20 years of management experience in a bank, financial institution, international financial institution, or infrastructure project; OR
  - Have at least 15 years of experience in alternative finance operation or project analysis; OR
  - Have at least 10 years of service in a gazette first class or higher government position
- Not currently hold any position in a bank, financial institution, insurance company, fund management institution, or securities-related company

These are demanding qualifications. They ensure that the independent director has genuine expertise — not a political appointment of a retired politician or a bureaucrat who merely spent years in a ministry. The prohibition on current employment in relevant financial institutions prevents the independent director from representing those institutions' interests rather than the fund's.

The 20-year experience requirement for certain paths is quite high — it would exclude many capable mid-career professionals who might bring fresh perspectives. The requirement for a government career path (10 years at gazette first class) creates an alternative entry route that could be used to place relatively senior former bureaucrats in the position, which may or may not bring genuine independence.

## Part 4: Disqualifications — Who Cannot Be an Independent Director

Section 12 specifies the disqualifications. A person cannot be appointed — or remain — as independent director if they:

**Do not meet the Section 11 qualifications.** Obviously.

**Are insolvent.** A person in personal bankruptcy or insolvency cannot serve on a financial institution's board — they lack the financial standing and judgment one would expect.

**Have been convicted of morally reprehensible offenses.** The list in the Act is extensive: corruption, forgery, fraud, theft, money laundering, human trafficking, abduction, rape, crimes against ancient monuments or wildlife, organized crime, and "any other organized crime or morally reprehensible felony." This is an appropriately broad prohibition. A person convicted of financial crimes should not sit on the board of a major financial institution.

**Currently hold a directorship, shareholding, or membership in an organization that has received loans from the fund and is implementing a project.** This prevents a board member from simultaneously overseeing the fund and holding an interest in a borrower — a profound conflict of interest.

**Have any involvement or interest in any contract or procurement with the fund.** This prevents contractors and service providers to the fund from sitting on its board.

**Are blacklisted under applicable law.** Nepal's financial regulatory system maintains a blacklist of persons and institutions that have defaulted on financial obligations or violated financial regulations. Blacklisted persons cannot serve as independent directors.

### Vacancy Conditions

Section 12, Sub-section 2 specifies when an independent director's seat becomes vacant:

- If they become disqualified under the above conditions
- If they are absent from three or more consecutive board meetings without prior notice or reasonable excuse
- If the board recommends, and Nepal's Cabinet confirms, removal on grounds of physical or mental incapacity to perform duties
- If they submit a written resignation that is accepted
- If they die

The three-meeting absence rule is important — it ensures directors do not hold positions passively without actively engaging. The mental or physical incapacity provision provides a mechanism for removing a director who is no longer capable of performing their duties without requiring a finding of misconduct.

## Part 5: Board Meeting Procedures

Section 13 specifies how the board conducts its meetings.

**Frequency:** Meetings are held as often as required, called by the chair at a time, date, and place the chair determines.

**Notice:** The Secretary must give all directors written notice with the agenda at least 24 hours before a meeting, under the chair's direction. Notice can be given electronically.

**Chairpersonship:** The chair presides. In the chair's absence, the Finance Ministry Joint Secretary (the next-ranking member) presides.

**Quorum:** More than 50% of directors must be present for the meeting to be quorate (able to take valid decisions). This means at least four out of seven voting members must be present.

**Remote attendance:** A director outside the Kathmandu Valley, or unable to attend physically due to unavoidable circumstances, can attend and be counted for quorum purposes via audio-visual means (video conference). This is a modern provision that acknowledges the geographic reality of Nepal — not all board members will always be in Kathmandu — and the practical reality that a major development finance institution will need to hold meetings efficiently.

**Decision-making:** Decisions are by majority vote. In case of a tie, the presiding chair has a casting vote.

**Meeting allowances:** Directors receive meeting allowances determined by the board with the Finance Ministry's approval. This ensures directors are compensated for their time but that compensation is transparent and subject to government oversight.

**External invitations:** The board can invite the Finance Ministry secretary, government officials, or experts to attend meetings in an advisory capacity. This provision allows the board to bring in specialized knowledge for specific agenda items without making those people permanent members.

**Conflict of interest in meetings:** Section 13, Sub-section 9 contains an important provision: a director appointed by insurance companies (Category C directors) cannot participate in discussions or decisions about projects that their own company has insured. This prevents a direct conflict of interest where a director's insurance company is affected by the fund's decision.

**Board self-governance:** The board determines its own additional procedures beyond what the Act specifies.

### What the Meeting Rules Say About Governance Quality

The meeting procedures are broadly sensible. The 24-hour notice requirement is a minimum — good governance would typically require longer advance notice to allow proper preparation. The 50% quorum requirement is reasonable. The tie-breaking mechanism is standard.

The video conference provision is practically important — it makes remote participation possible and reduces the cost of geographic barriers. But it also creates a risk: video-conference participation may be less engaged, less scrutinizing, and more susceptible to passive rubber-stamping than in-person participation.

The conflict-of-interest provision for insurance company directors is good but narrow — it covers only the director's own company's insurance interests. Broader conflicts should also be covered, and Section 15 (discussed below) provides a general framework for this.

## Part 6: Board Duties, Responsibilities, and Liabilities

Section 14 specifies the board's powers, functions, and duties. This is an extensive list that covers the board's full governance responsibilities:

- Approving policies, programmes, and plans for the fund and overseeing their implementation
- Providing policy guidance to the fund
- Approving investments and financial instruments as required
- Determining the operating procedures for the various funds established under the Act
- Studying and evaluating infrastructure and productive sector projects that create employment opportunities, economic growth, and export promotion
- Approving investments in specific projects under the Section 4 criteria
- Monitoring and evaluating the financial and investment instruments operated by the fund
- Ensuring the transparency of resource mobilization and deployment
- Maintaining institutional governance within the fund's operations
- Approving environmental protection standards, sustainable development considerations, and social impact criteria for investments
- Taking risk mitigation measures including insurance when making project investments
- Coordinating with domestic and international financial institutions as required
- Establishing subsidiary companies for specific project operations
- Converting loan investments to equity investments (debt-equity swaps)
- Performing any other functions related to alternative development finance

This is a governance mandate, not an operational mandate. The board sets policy, approves investments, monitors performance, and ensures accountability. It does not manage day-to-day operations — that is the CEO's job.

### Director Responsibilities and Personal Liability

Section 16 creates individual and collective accountability for board members. Each director has a personal responsibility to:
- Fulfill their obligations under the Act
- Perform their functions on behalf of the fund
- Act in the fund's interests

Board members are individually and collectively accountable for these responsibilities. If a director acts in bad faith or negligently, causing harm or loss to the fund, they are personally liable for that harm or loss. They can also face prosecution under applicable law.

This personal liability provision is important and should not be dismissed as nominal. Personal liability for board decisions creates real incentives for directors to exercise genuine judgment, to push back on bad proposals, to ask for independent analysis, and to recuse themselves from decisions where they have conflicts. Without personal liability, board service can become a rubber-stamping exercise where members feel no personal stake in the quality of decisions.

Whether this liability is enforced in practice in Nepal is a different question. Nepal's track record of pursuing corporate liability against politically connected individuals is limited. But the legal provision exists, and it creates at least some deterrent effect and a legal basis for future enforcement if political conditions change.

## Part 7: Disclosure and Conflict-of-Interest Rules

Section 15 creates an important transparency and conflict-of-interest framework. Within 30 days of appointment, each director must provide the board with written disclosure on:

- Details of their immediate family members
- Whether they or any immediate family member holds shares in the fund or in any company the fund has invested in, or is a promoter shareholder, institutional shareholder, or director of such a company
- The number and type of shares held (if any)
- Whether they or family members held management director, CEO, auditor, manager, executive director, or decision-influencing positions in any infrastructure company in which the fund has invested, in the three years before their appointment
- Whether any disqualifications apply to them
- Whether they have any interest in any matter that may come before the board

This disclosure must be recorded and maintained by the fund.

When the board is making a decision where a specific director has a conflicting interest, that director must not participate in the discussion or vote. Sub-section 4 adds that if a director fails to disclose a conflict they have, their participation in discussions on that matter does not automatically mean they had a conflict — meaning the burden of proof of conflict remains even if disclosure was not made.

### How Conflict-of-Interest Rules Work in Practice

Conflict-of-interest rules are only as effective as their enforcement. In the best-case scenario, directors proactively disclose all relevant interests, recuse themselves from relevant decisions, and the board's processes are documented well enough that any violation is detectable.

In the worst-case scenario, conflicts are systematically undisclosed, recusal is ignored or gamed (directors have informal influence on decisions even when formally recused), and the documentation is insufficient to prove violations.

Nepal's institutional environment has historically tended toward the latter scenario more than the former. High-profile cases of conflict of interest in public institutions — where officials award contracts to companies in which they or their family members have interests — are not unusual in Nepal's public sector. The legal framework for preventing conflicts has historically been weak; the Alternative Development Finance Act's provisions represent an improvement, but they are not self-enforcing.

## Part 8: Sub-Committees and Working Groups

Section 17 authorizes the board to establish sub-committees to assist with its work. The Act specifies three mandatory sub-committees:

**Audit Sub-Committee (Lekhapariksana Upasamiti):** Responsible for overseeing the fund's financial reporting, internal controls, and external audit processes. The audit committee is a standard element of good corporate governance globally — it provides more detailed, specialized oversight of financial matters than the full board can provide.

**Investment Sub-Committee (Lagani Upasamiti):** Responsible for reviewing and recommending individual investment decisions before they go to the full board. The investment committee provides technical scrutiny of specific project proposals — reviewing financial models, risk assessments, due diligence reports — and makes recommendations (approve, reject, modify) to the full board.

**Risk Reduction and Management Sub-Committee (Jokhim Nyunikaran tatha Byabasthapan Upasamiti):** Responsible for overseeing the fund's risk management framework, identifying emerging risks in the portfolio, and recommending risk mitigation measures.

The board can also establish ad-hoc working groups (karyadal) for specific studies, investigations, or purposes.

These sub-committees are important for several reasons. First, they allow board members with relevant expertise to focus their time on the areas where they add most value. Second, they create more detailed documentation of the deliberative process — sub-committee minutes, reports, and recommendations become part of the governance record. Third, they allow external experts to participate in the sub-committee process even when not board members.

The Act leaves the detailed structure, terms of reference, and membership of the sub-committees to the board's determination at the time of their formation. This flexibility is appropriate — the optimal structure may not be fully knowable at the time the law is written.
