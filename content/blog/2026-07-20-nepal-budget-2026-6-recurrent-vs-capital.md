In the last post we took the budget's headline total apart and learned to scale it against inflation, the previous year, the population, and the size of the economy. Now we make the first and most important cut *inside* that total. Of all the ways a budget can be divided, one division matters more than any other for understanding whether a government is feeding the present or building the future. It is the division between **recurrent** spending and **capital** spending — between the money that runs the state and the money that builds it.

This sounds technical, and the words are dull, but the distinction is the closest thing public finance has to a moral and strategic X-ray. It reveals, more honestly than any speech, whether a country is mostly paying its bills or mostly investing in its tomorrow. And the picture it reveals for Nepal's 2083/84 budget is one of the most important facts in this entire series: a country that spends roughly three rupees running itself for every one rupee it spends building itself. By the end of this post you will understand exactly what those two kinds of spending are, why the balance between them matters so much, why Nepal's balance worries economists, how the new government's large pay rise tilts it further, and how to catch the most common accounting trick in all of public spending — consumption dressed up as investment.

## The three streams, restated

Recall from Part 1 that the total budget of NPR 2,124.34 billion (about USD 14.16 billion) divides into three broad streams. **Recurrent** spending is NPR 1,270.58 billion (about USD 8.47 billion), or 59.8 percent of the whole. **Capital** spending is NPR 431.10 billion (about USD 2.87 billion), just 20.3 percent. And **financing**, or financial management, is NPR 422.64 billion (about USD 2.82 billion), 19.9 percent. We are going to spend this post understanding what each of these actually is, because the names are opaque and the differences are everything.

## Recurrent spending: the cost of running the state

Begin with the largest stream, the one that consumes nearly sixty percent of the entire budget. **Recurrent spending** — sometimes called current spending or, in older usage, regular spending — is the money the government spends simply to *operate*, year in and year out. It is the cost of keeping the machinery of the state running, as distinct from the cost of building anything new. The defining feature of recurrent spending is that it is *consumed within the year and leaves no durable asset behind*. You spend it, it does its job for that year, and then next year you have to spend it again to get the same thing. It recurs — hence the name.

What does it consist of? The single largest component, in Nepal as in most governments, is the **salaries and benefits of public employees** — the wages of the civil servants, teachers, police, soldiers, health workers, and all the other people the state employs to deliver its functions. Paying these people is not optional and is not waste; without them there is no administration, no schooling, no policing, no public health. But it is recurrent: you pay this month's salaries, they buy this month's work, and next month you must pay again. A second large component is the **operating cost of running offices and services** — the rent, the utilities, the fuel, the supplies, the maintenance, the day-to-day expenses of keeping government functioning. A third, and a crucial one we will return to in the posts on debt, is the **interest on past borrowing** — the cost of servicing the loans the country has already taken, which must be paid every year before anything else and which grows as the debt grows. And a fourth large category in Nepal's case is **transfers and grants** — money the federal government hands onward, including the large sums sent down to provincial and local governments (which we will examine in the post on federalism) and the social-security allowances paid to the elderly, to people with disabilities, and to other groups (which we will examine in the post on social protection).

The honest way to think about recurrent spending is this: it is the government's *cost of existing and functioning*. It is not, by and large, optional, and it is not, by and large, waste — a country needs teachers and soldiers and functioning offices and must honour the interest on its debts. But it builds nothing that outlasts the year. Spend it, and the year ends with the state having run — but with no new road, no new school building, no new power line to show for it. That is the key limitation, and it is why the *size* of the recurrent share, relative to the capital share, is watched so closely. A government can spend its entire budget on recurrent costs and produce a perfectly functioning administration that has added nothing to the country's stock of productive assets. Running is necessary. But a country that only ever runs, and never builds, does not develop.

## Capital spending: the cost of building the future

Now the second stream, the small one that gets only about a fifth of the budget but that development, in the physical sense, almost entirely depends on. **Capital spending** — also called capital expenditure or development spending — is money used to create *durable assets*: things that are built once and then deliver value for many years afterward. The defining feature of capital spending is the mirror image of recurrent's: it *leaves a lasting asset behind*. You spend it once to build the road, and the road then serves traffic for decades; you spend it once to build the school building, and it houses students for a generation.

What does it consist of? The classic examples are the physical infrastructure that a developing country needs and largely lacks: roads, bridges, and highways; power generation and transmission; irrigation systems; water supply; school and hospital buildings; airports. In economic terms, capital spending is what builds up the country's *stock of productive capital* — the physical foundation on which future economic activity rests. Economists have a phrase for the broad category — *gross fixed capital formation* — but the intuition needs no jargon: capital spending is the part of the budget that builds the things the country will still be using long after the fiscal year is over.

This is why the capital share matters so enormously for a poor country trying to develop. A nation like Nepal is held back, in significant part, by what it does not have built: too few good roads, leaving regions isolated and goods expensive to move; too little reliable power and irrigation, holding back farms and factories; too little of the physical scaffolding on which a modern economy stands. Capital spending is the budget's tool for closing those gaps. Every rupee of well-spent capital expenditure is, in principle, an investment that raises the country's future capacity to produce and to prosper. It is the opposite of consumption: instead of being eaten by the present, it is planted for the future. And that is precisely why a capital share of only about 20 percent — one rupee built for every three rupees run — is, on its face, a worrying number for a country whose central economic problem is how much it still needs to build.

## The third stream: financing, the part that is neither

The third stream, financing or financial management, at nearly 20 percent of the budget, is the one most people never think about and most easily misread, so we must be clear about what it is — and what it is not.

**Financing** spending is, broadly, money that goes to *managing the government's debts and financial positions* rather than to providing services or building assets. Its largest component is the **repayment of the principal on past loans** — not the interest (which, recall, sits in recurrent), but the repayment of the borrowed sums themselves as they come due. When the government borrowed money in past years, it promised to pay it back; the portions of that repayment falling due this year are financed here. A second component is **lending and investment by the government** — money the government lends onward to public enterprises or lower levels of government, or invests in the shares of public companies. The defining feature of this stream is that, unlike recurrent spending, it is not consumed in running the state, and unlike capital spending, it does not build a road or a school. It is, in a sense, *internal financial plumbing* — money moving to settle past obligations and to lend or invest, rather than money delivering a public service or a public asset.

Why does this distinction matter to a citizen? Because it is easy, and sometimes politically convenient, to blur the three streams together into one big "spending" number and lose sight of how little of it is actually building anything. When nearly 20 percent of the budget is financial plumbing — repaying old loans, on-lending, investing in shares — and nearly 60 percent is the recurrent cost of running the state, then the share genuinely left for building the country's future is the 20 percent capital stream and nothing more. The budget may total NPR 2.12 trillion, but the part of it pointed at new roads, power, and buildings is the NPR 431 billion capital slice. Keeping the three streams distinct is what stops you from mistaking the total budget for a development budget. Most of the budget is not development. It is running costs and debt management. Only the smallest of the three streams is building anything new.

## Why the balance worries economists

We have now seen what the three streams are. Let us be explicit about why the *balance* among them — heavy on recurrent, light on capital — is a source of persistent concern, while being fair about the other side of the argument, because this series flatters no view, including the critical one.

The core worry is straightforward. A developing country's central economic task is to build up the productive assets it lacks, and capital spending is the budget's instrument for doing that. A budget that allocates only about a fifth to capital, and three-fifths to recurrent running costs, is a budget that is mostly feeding the present rather than building the future. If the recurrent share keeps rising and the capital share keeps being squeezed, the country risks a slow trap: an ever-larger share of its resources consumed simply by the cost of running an ever-larger state, with ever less left over to build the roads, power, and infrastructure that would let the economy grow enough to afford that state comfortably. The worry deepens when you remember the execution gap from the last post: not only is the capital share small, but a large portion of even that small share has historically gone *unspent*, so the amount of capital that actually gets built is smaller still than the 20 percent allocation suggests. A government can thus end a year having paid all its salaries and interest in full — because those are easy to spend — while having built only a fraction of the roads and schools it budgeted, because those are hard. Over time, that asymmetry starves the country of exactly the investment it most needs.

There is a fairer, more complicated side, and an honest analysis must give it weight. First, not all recurrent spending is mere consumption with no future value. The salaries of teachers and health workers, for instance, deliver education and health, which build the country's *human* capital — its educated, healthy workforce — even though they are booked as recurrent rather than capital. A narrow focus on the physical capital share can undervalue spending on people, which also shapes the future. Second, a poor country with weak institutions may genuinely struggle to spend large capital sums *well*; pouring more money into capital projects than the system can plan, procure, and supervise can produce waste, white elephants, and corruption rather than useful assets. There is an argument that the binding constraint on Nepal's development is not the *amount* of capital budgeted but the *capacity* to spend it effectively, and that simply raising the capital allocation without fixing the execution machinery would change little. Third, some recurrent obligations — above all the interest on existing debt — are not discretionary at all; they must be paid, and they will only grow if the country borrows more, which is part of why the debt posts later in this series matter so much. So the picture is not simply "recurrent bad, capital good." It is that a heavy and rising recurrent share, combined with a small and under-executed capital share, is a genuine structural concern for a country that needs to build — while remembering that spending on people has value, that capacity to spend well is itself a constraint, and that some recurrent costs are unavoidable. The worry is real. It is also more nuanced than a slogan.

## The pay rise and the recurrent ratchet

This budget contains a specific decision that bears directly on the balance we have just discussed, and it is a vivid illustration of a phenomenon economists call the *recurrent ratchet* — the tendency for recurrent spending to climb and to resist ever coming back down. The decision is a significant increase in the pay of public employees.

The budget raises the basic salary scale of civil servants by 10 percent, and adds on top of that a monthly incentive or performance allowance worth a further 10 percent of the new scale, which together amount to a net increase on the order of 21 percent, with a minimum monthly remuneration set around NPR 40,000 (about USD 267), effective from the start of the fiscal year on Shrawan 1, 2083 — that is, around 16 July 2026. Let us weigh this with both hands, as always.

On one hand, there are real and legitimate reasons for it. Public employees, like everyone else, face rising prices; if their pay does not keep pace with inflation, their real incomes fall and their living standards erode. A pay rise can be a matter of basic fairness and of keeping the public service able to attract and retain capable people rather than losing them to the private sector or to emigration. The incentive-allowance component, in particular, is framed as an attempt to reward performance, which, if it genuinely worked that way, could improve the quality of public administration. For a new government promising better governance, investing in the people who deliver it is not obviously foolish.

On the other hand — and this is the recurrent ratchet — a pay rise is one of the *stickiest* possible additions to the recurrent budget. Once salaries are raised, they are almost never lowered; the higher level becomes the new permanent floor, and future years inherit it and build further on top. So a pay rise is not a one-year cost; it is a permanent upward shift in the largest component of recurrent spending, locked in for decades and compounding. This is precisely the mechanism by which the recurrent share creeps ever upward and the capital share gets squeezed: each year's pay rise ratchets the running costs higher, permanently, while the building budget competes for whatever is left. And there is a sharp irony to be noted, with appropriate caution about the exact figures: the same budget that raises recurrent costs through this pay rise also claims to *save* money by trimming the structure of government — the ministry mergers we turn to next — and it is entirely possible that the permanent cost added by the pay rise dwarfs the savings claimed from the trimming. Whether the net effect on the recurrent burden is up or down is a question the headline figures do not settle and that should be worked out from the detailed numbers. What is not in doubt is the direction of the pay rise itself: it pushes the recurrent share up, permanently, in a budget where that share is already the central worry.

## The savings claim, examined

The budget pairs its pay rise with a structural-reform story: a promise to make the state leaner and cheaper to run. Specifically, it proposes to reduce the number of federal ministries from 22 to 18, and to dissolve, merge, transfer, or restructure dozens of government agencies — the reported figures include 31 agencies dissolved, 6 merged, 6 transferred, and 18 restructured — with an estimated saving on the order of NPR 20 billion (about USD 133 million). This is exactly the kind of claim that deserves the skeptical treatment this series specialises in, so let us apply it.

Begin with scale. NPR 20 billion sounds large in isolation, but set against the budget's total of NPR 2,124.34 billion, it is less than one percent of the whole. As a share of the recurrent stream alone (NPR 1,270.58 billion), it is still only on the order of one and a half percent. So even if the saving is fully realised, it is a modest trimming, not a transformation of the cost of government. A citizen told that the state has been made dramatically leaner should note that the claimed saving amounts to under a penny in every rupee.

Next, the word "estimated." A projected saving from a reorganisation is a forecast, and forecasts of administrative savings are notoriously optimistic, because reorganisations have a way of costing money in the short run (severance, transition, duplication during the changeover) and because the staff and functions of dissolved agencies often do not vanish so much as migrate elsewhere, carrying much of their cost with them. Merging two ministries does not automatically halve their combined cost; the people, the offices, and the obligations largely remain. So the NPR 20 billion should be treated as a hopeful projection to be tested against what actually happens, not a banked result — and we are by now well trained to keep "announced" and "achieved" apart.

Finally, the juxtaposition we have already flagged. The budget simultaneously raises recurrent costs through a roughly 21 percent pay package and claims to cut them through reorganisation. The honest question is the *net* effect, and the honest answer is that it depends on figures the headlines do not provide — the size of the public wage bill, the true cost of the pay rise, and the genuine (as opposed to estimated) savings from the mergers. It is entirely plausible that the permanent cost added by the pay rise exceeds the modest, uncertain saving from the trimming, in which case the budget's structural-reform story would be, in net terms, a story of recurrent costs rising rather than falling, even as it is presented as making government leaner. We are not asserting that this is so; we are insisting that the question be asked and answered from the numbers rather than the rhetoric. A government can announce both a generous pay rise and a leaner state in the same speech, and a careful citizen notices that those two announcements pull in opposite directions and asks which one wins.

## Why the building money so often goes unspent

We have noted several times that even the modest capital share tends to be under-spent, and since this is so central we will preview the reasons here, before the dedicated infrastructure post examines them in full. The pattern is not random; it has consistent, structural causes, and understanding them is part of understanding why the recurrent-capital imbalance is even worse in practice than on paper.

Recurrent spending is *easy* to execute. Paying salaries, settling office bills, and transferring interest payments are routine, predictable transactions that the system performs almost automatically; the money goes out reliably because the actions required are simple and repetitive. Capital spending is *hard* to execute. Building something requires a long chain of difficult steps, each of which can stall: detailed design, competitive tendering, contract award, land acquisition, and construction itself — all of it, in Nepal, contending with slow procedures, with disputes over land, with limited contractor and supervisory capacity, and with a fiscal year whose first months coincide with the monsoon, when building is hardest. The predictable result, year after year, is that the easy recurrent spending happens in full while the hard capital spending lags, with a large share of the building budget consumed in a frantic end-of-year rush or simply left unspent as the authority lapses. So the real, executed balance of the budget is even more tilted toward consumption than the allocation suggests: not only is capital allocated only a fifth of the budget, but it actually delivers less than that fifth, because so much of it never gets spent. This is the deep, structural reason the recurrent-capital imbalance is so hard to fix — it is not merely a matter of choosing to allocate more to capital, but of building the capacity to actually spend capital well, which is a far harder thing than writing a larger number in a budget line.

## How budgets deceive: consumption dressed as investment

The technique of deception for this post follows directly from the recurrent-capital distinction, and it cuts in both directions: **consumption dressed up as investment**, and its mirror, *running costs misfiled as development*.

Here is the move. Because "capital" or "development" spending sounds productive and forward-looking, while "recurrent" spending sounds like mere overhead, there is a constant temptation — political and sometimes accounting — to make the development share look larger than it really is by classifying recurrent-type spending as capital. A government that wants to boast of a high "development budget" can do so by booking things that are really running costs — vehicles, furniture, the expenses of workshops and seminars, certain salaries and allowances attached to "projects" — under the capital heading, inflating the apparent investment while the genuine asset-building is smaller than the number implies. The reverse trick exists too: a government can describe ordinary recurrent spending — a pay rise, an expansion of allowances, the routine cost of a programme — using the vocabulary of *investment* ("investing in our people," "investing in the future"), borrowing the moral glow of capital spending for what is really consumption. In both cases the deception works by exploiting the fact that most listeners do not know the difference between the two kinds of spending — which is exactly why we have spent a whole post making sure you do.

The defence has two parts. First, distrust the labels and look at the substance: ask of any "investment" or "development" or "capital" claim whether it will actually leave a durable, productive asset behind, because that — not the vocabulary used to describe it — is what makes spending genuinely capital. A "development programme" that consists largely of salaries, allowances, vehicles, and workshops is recurrent spending wearing a capital costume, however it is booked or branded. Second, watch the *executed* capital figure, not the *allocated* one, and not the rhetoric. The true measure of how much a government is building is the amount of capital expenditure that is actually spent and that actually results in assets, as recorded after the fact and examined by the auditors — not the size of the "development budget" announced in the speech, which may be inflated by misclassification and which, in any case, history suggests will be substantially under-spent. When you hear a government celebrate its commitment to development and investment, translate it into the only question that tests it: how much will actually be spent building durable, productive things, and how do we know? Apply that translation and you will see, again and again, that the gap between the proud language of investment and the modest reality of building is one of the most reliable features of the budgets you will read.

## A worked example: the development line that wasn't

Make the misclassification concrete with a composite example. Suppose a ministry's budget contains a prominent line item under the capital, or development, heading — a "programme" with an impressive name and a substantial allocation, presented as part of the government's investment in the country's future. A citizen reading the budget, or hearing it described, naturally pictures something being built: an asset, an improvement, a durable thing.

Now open the line item up. Suppose that, on inspection, most of its money is not destined to build any asset at all. A large share is salaries and allowances for the staff attached to the programme. Another share is the cost of vehicles, office equipment, and travel. Another is the cost of a series of workshops, trainings, and consultations — meetings, in other words. Only a small remainder is actually spent on constructing or creating anything durable. In substance, this "development" line is overwhelmingly recurrent spending — the cost of running an organisation and holding meetings — wearing the capital label. It inflates the headline "development budget," it sounds productive, and it builds almost nothing. And if, on top of that, even the small genuinely-constructive remainder goes unspent because of the usual execution problems, then the line item delivers essentially no new asset at all, despite having been counted as investment in the future.

Notice, once again, that nothing here need involve an outright lie. The money is genuinely spent; the staff are genuinely employed; the workshops genuinely happen. The deception, such as it is, lives in the *classification* — in counting the cost of running and meeting as though it were the cost of building — and it works only because most people cannot tell the difference. You now can. When you encounter a proud "development" or "capital" figure, you know to ask what is actually inside it, how much of it will leave a durable asset behind, and how much will actually be spent. That single habit will protect you from one of the most common and most flattering illusions in all of public finance: the illusion of a country building its future, when much of what is labelled "building" is really just the cost of running the present under a more inspiring name.

## For the technically minded: the three streams as data, and a classifier

For readers who work with numbers, here is the three-stream structure as a compact object, followed by a sketch of how one would actually classify spending into recurrent versus capital — the very judgement the deception above tries to corrupt.

```json
{
  "fiscal_year": "2083/84 (2026/27)",
  "fx_rate_npr_per_usd": 150,
  "total_outlay": { "npr_billion": 2124.34, "usd_billion_approx": 14.16 },
  "streams": {
    "recurrent": { "npr_billion": 1270.58, "usd_billion_approx": 8.47, "share_pct": 59.8,
                   "contains": ["salaries_and_benefits", "operating_costs", "interest_on_debt", "transfers_and_allowances"],
                   "leaves_durable_asset": false },
    "capital":   { "npr_billion": 431.10,  "usd_billion_approx": 2.87, "share_pct": 20.3,
                   "contains": ["roads_bridges", "power_and_irrigation", "school_and_hospital_buildings", "other_construction"],
                   "leaves_durable_asset": true },
    "financing": { "npr_billion": 422.64,  "usd_billion_approx": 2.82, "share_pct": 19.9,
                   "contains": ["principal_repayment_of_loans", "on_lending", "share_investment"],
                   "leaves_durable_asset": false }
  },
  "run_to_build_ratio_approx": 2.95
}
```

The `run_to_build_ratio` of about 2.95 — recurrent divided by capital — is the number to remember: roughly three rupees run for every one rupee built. Now the classifier, which captures the substance-over-label discipline of the deception defence:

```text
classify(line_item):
    # The label in the budget is NOT decisive. Ask what the money actually does.
    if line_item.produces_durable_productive_asset():      # a road, a building, a power line
        return "CAPITAL (genuine investment)"
    if line_item.is_principal_repayment or line_item.is_on_lending_or_share_purchase:
        return "FINANCING (financial plumbing, not service or asset)"
    if line_item.is_salary or line_item.is_operating_cost \
       or line_item.is_interest or line_item.is_transfer_or_allowance \
       or line_item.is_workshop_or_travel_or_vehicle:
        return "RECURRENT (consumption, even if labelled 'development')"
    return "INSPECT FURTHER — do not trust the heading"

# Warning: a 'development' or 'capital' line item that is mostly salaries, allowances,
# vehicles, and workshops is RECURRENT wearing a capital costume. Open it up before you believe it.
```

The lesson of the classifier is the lesson of the whole post: the *heading* a budget gives a line — recurrent, capital, development, investment — is a claim, not a fact, and the fact is determined by what the money actually does. A disciplined reader, like a good auditor, classifies by substance, not by label, and reserves the proud word "investment" for spending that genuinely leaves the country something durable and productive to show for it. Do that consistently across a budget, and you will often find that the true investment share is smaller, and the true running-cost share larger, than the government's own presentation suggests.

## Where we go from here

We have made the most important cut inside the budget. You now know the difference between recurrent spending — the roughly 60 percent that runs the state and leaves no durable asset — and capital spending — the roughly 20 percent that builds the future — with the financing stream's roughly 20 percent of financial plumbing in between. You understand why a heavy recurrent share and a light, under-executed capital share is a genuine structural worry for a country that needs to build, and you have heard the fairer counter-arguments too. You have seen how the new government's roughly 21 percent pay rise ratchets recurrent costs permanently upward, how its claimed NPR 20 billion of merger savings is modest and uncertain and may be outweighed by that very pay rise, and why the building budget so reliably goes under-spent. And you have met the deception of consumption dressed as investment, with a classifier to defeat it.

In Part 7 we turn from how the money is spent to where it comes from, beginning with the largest source: revenue. We will learn what government revenue actually is, the difference between tax and non-tax revenue, and the striking degree to which Nepal's treasury depends, indirectly, on the wages its migrant workers send home — the remittance economy that quietly funds so much of the state through the taxes on everything those wages buy. And we will examine the central tension of this budget's revenue plan: a government that is cutting tax rates while simultaneously promising to collect more. The spending side is mapped. Now we follow the money to its source.

## A Note on Numbers and Assumptions

All US dollar figures use our single assumed rate of **1 US dollar = 150 Nepalese rupees**, an approximation close to the Nepal Rastra Bank reference rate in mid-2026; the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee at 1.60 and drifts daily, so treat dollar figures as intuition rather than accounting. All rupee figures are nominal unless stated otherwise.

The civil-service pay change is described here as a 10 percent rise in the basic scale plus a 10 percent incentive allowance, a net increase on the order of 21 percent, with a minimum remuneration around NPR 40,000 — figures reported from the budget that should be confirmed against the official documents and the implementing rules. The structural-reform figures (ministries reduced from 22 to 18; 31 agencies dissolved, 6 merged, 6 transferred, 18 restructured; estimated savings of about NPR 20 billion) are likewise as reported, and the savings figure in particular is an estimate that should be treated skeptically and tested against actual outcomes. The net effect of the pay rise and the merger savings on the recurrent burden cannot be settled from the headline figures and should be worked out from the detailed wage-bill and expenditure data.

And the assumption beneath the whole series: a budget allocation is an announced intention, not an enacted certainty, still less an implemented result — and this is especially true of the capital budget, whose real, executed size, after the year's under-spending, is reliably smaller than its allocation. The most important thing about the building budget is not what it is set at, but how much of it is actually spent building.
