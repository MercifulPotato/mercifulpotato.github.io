# The Long Seduction: Númenor, the Rings of Power, and the Downfall of the Second Age

*This is Part Five of the comprehensive in-universe chronological history of Middle-earth. Part Four ended with the War of Wrath, the sinking of Beleriand, and Morgoth's expulsion into the Void. The First Age was over. The world was remade. Now begins the longest single age of the Sun — three thousand four hundred and forty-one years from the rising of Gil-galad's kingdom to the moment the One Ring slid from Sauron's finger on the slopes of Orodruin. This is the Second Age: an age of gifts and pride, of beautiful craft and terrible corruption, of a civilisation raised to the height of human achievement and then drowned beneath the sea for reaching beyond it.*

---

## Part 1: The World After the First Age — A Changed Geography

The world that emerged from the War of Wrath was physically different from the world that had gone before it. Most obviously, Beleriand was gone — the land west of the Blue Mountains was beneath the sea, and the new coastline of northwestern Middle-earth was defined by the Ered Luin running north to south with the Gulf of Lune cutting through their southern end. This gulf, where the river Lhûn flowed to the sea, became the site of Círdan's new harbours: the Grey Havens, Mithlond, which would serve as the great port of Middle-earth's northwestern shore for the entire Second and Third Ages.

East of the Ered Luin lay Eriador — the region between the Blue Mountains and the Misty Mountains — which was largely uninhabited at the start of the Second Age. Further east, beyond the Misty Mountains, lay Rhovanion, the great wilderland, and beyond that the vast eastern reaches of Middle-earth that Tolkien rarely mapped in detail but which were inhabited by many peoples, most of them still under Morgoth's shadow or in various states of corruption from his long influence.

The south of Middle-earth was shaped by the great mountain ranges: the White Mountains in the northwest (later the domain of Gondor), the ranges of Harad, the coastlands of the Sundering Seas. The continent of Middle-earth as a whole was enormous — far larger than the portion of it that Tolkien's maps show. The known world of Eriador and Rhovanion and Gondor and Rohan is the northwestern corner of a landmass that extended far south and east, into regions that were merely names even in the most detailed Elvish records.

And there was a new island: Númenor. Raised from the sea by the Valar as a gift to the Edain, located between Middle-earth and Valinor but not in sight of either. Its star-shape — five great promontories extending from a central highland — was the most deliberate landscape in Middle-earth's history, shaped by Elvish and Valian art rather than geological chance. Its central mountain was Meneltarma, the Pillar of Heaven, which was sacred from the beginning: Ilúvatar's holy place, where no temple was ever built because none was needed, where the Númenóreans came three times a year in silence to worship, and where no weapon was ever borne.

Númenor was both paradise and prison. It was paradise because it was more fertile and more beautiful than any other land in the mortal world, blessed by the Valar with crops and weather and long life for its inhabitants. It was prison because of the one law that governed it: the Ban of the Valar. The Númenóreans could sail anywhere in the world, could go as far east and south as they wished. They could not sail west toward Valinor. The Ban was the condition of the gift, and it was always present, and the Númenóreans always chafed against it.

This is the central tension of the Second Age: the gift and the ban together, beauty and prohibition, extraordinary privilege and one forbidden door. It is the same structure as the Garden of Eden — and Tolkien, who was not writing allegory but who could not help his own deep structures — was aware of this. The Downfall of Númenor is his mythology's version of the Fall: the moment when the most privileged of Men reaches for the one thing withheld and destroys everything.

## Part 2: Elros Tar-Minyatur — The First King and the Gift of Long Life

Elros son of Eärendil had chosen to be of mortal kind. This distinguished him from his brother Elrond, who had chosen the Elvish fate and would live until the end of the Third Age. But Elros's mortality was not quite ordinary Mannish mortality — the Valar had given the Númenóreans a gift: a lifespan three times the ordinary span of Men. Where ordinary Men of the time lived perhaps sixty or seventy years, the Númenóreans in their earliest and purest generations lived three or even four hundred years.

Elros himself lived for five hundred years, dying at five hundred and ten in the year 442 of the Second Age. He was not immortal — he aged, though slowly; he felt the passage of time; he eventually grew old and tired and chose, as Tolkien says, to relinquish life rather than being taken by it. This voluntary death — the Númenórean king choosing the moment of his passing — was the expression of a gift that was always understood as a gift, not a curse, in those earliest days.

He founded the kingdom of Númenor and built its capital, Armenelos the Golden, in the eastern half of the island near the great harbour of Rómenna. He established the traditions that would define Númenórean culture: the reverence for Ilúvatar, expressed in the three annual festivals on Meneltarma; the friendship with the Elves of Middle-earth, particularly Gil-galad's kingdom of Lindon on the northwestern coast; the maritime culture that would eventually make the Númenóreans the greatest sailors in the world's history.

Elros took a sceptre name — Tar-Minyatur, meaning "First Lord" — establishing the tradition by which all Númenórean kings were known by their Quenya titles. Quenya was the language of high ceremony in Númenor, learned from the Elves and maintained as a mark of their heritage and alliance. The everyday language of Númenor was Adûnaic, a language entirely unrelated to the Elvish tongues, the native tongue of the Edain that had developed during the First Age.

The first few centuries of Númenor's history are largely a record of growth and achievement. The Númenóreans developed their maritime skills to extraordinary degrees, building ships that could cross the vast oceans and maintaining regular contact with Middle-earth's western coast. They were generous in those early centuries — they brought gifts to the Men of Middle-earth, taught them arts and crafts, helped them resist the shadow that Morgoth's ancient corruption had left. The Men of Middle-earth called them the Lords of the West, and their coming was greeted with joy.

The Elves of Lindon — Gil-galad's kingdom — were their closest allies. There was genuine friendship between the two peoples, built on the long alliance of the First Age and on the shared heritage of Eärendil, who was the father of both Elros and Elrond. This friendship expressed itself most importantly in the Elvish assistance with Númenórean craftsmanship and knowledge, and in the long diplomatic contact that kept the two realms in communication throughout the Second Age's early centuries.

## Part 3: Sauron in the East — The Long Patience

While Númenor flourished in the west and Lindon maintained its beauty in the northwest, Sauron was in the east of Middle-earth, doing what he had always done: rebuilding, corrupting, extending his influence.

His nature was fundamentally unchanged from what it had been in the First Age. He was a Maia of Aulë — a craftsman, an orderer — whose fundamental desire was to impose his pattern on all things, to bring everything under the governance of a single controlling will. Under Morgoth he had served that will; now that Morgoth was gone, the will was his own. He did not cease being a craftsman; he remained the most skilled craftsman in Middle-earth. But his craft was always in service of control.

In the early Second Age, Sauron wore fair forms and could still appear beautiful. He had not yet been forced into permanent ugliness — that came later, with the Downfall of Númenor. He took a fair name: Annatar, the "Lord of Gifts." This was his persona when he approached the Elves. Before he became Annatar, however, he spent several centuries simply rebuilding his power in the east, establishing a base of operations, gathering servants — the Men who had been corrupted by Morgoth and who transferred their service easily enough to the most powerful remaining spirit of that era.

He chose his stronghold carefully: Mordor, the black land southeast of the White Mountains, enclosed by mountain ranges on three sides and bordered by the great river Anduin on the west. Mordor had natural fortifications that made it essentially defensible if properly occupied — the Ash Mountains in the north (Ered Lithui), the Ephel Dúath in the west, the Morgai ridge running parallel to the Ephel Dúath, and the Orodruin, the mountain of fire, in the interior. He built Barad-dûr, the Dark Tower, in the angle between the Ered Lithui and the Ephel Dúath. He built it using the power of the One Ring — though the Ring had not yet been forged when construction began, he poured into its foundations a portion of his own power, so that the Tower could not be destroyed while the Ring existed.

Mordor was chosen for its volcanic activity as much as its defensibility. The Orodruin — which Men called Mount Doom, and the Elves Amon Amarth — was a perpetually active volcano whose fires could be used in the forging of objects of great power. The fire of Orodruin was not the same as ordinary volcanic fire; it was connected to the deep fires of the world, the fires that had been part of Arda's substance since its making. Sauron could use those fires for his greatest works in a way that ordinary forges could not serve.

Around the year 1000 of the Second Age, Sauron felt secure enough in his eastern base to begin his longer game.

## Part 4: Celebrimbor and Eregion — The Smiths of the Gwaith-i-Mírdain

In the western reaches of Middle-earth, between the Misty Mountains and the Ered Luin, lay the region of Eregion — Hollin in the Common Speech, so called for the holly trees that grew there abundantly and that the Elves loved. Eregion was founded early in the Second Age by Noldorin Elves who had not gone to Valinor after the War of Wrath — Celebrimbor among them.

Celebrimbor son of Curufin son of Fëanor was the last of the House of Fëanor. His father Curufin had been one of the worst of Fëanor's sons in the Beren-and-Lúthien episode, and Celebrimbor had repudiated his father's actions — he had stayed in Nargothrond when Curufin was driven out, and had served the Noldorin people there with genuine loyalty. He was the bearer of his grandfather's craft-gift without his grandfather's pride or his father's cruelty. He was, in terms of raw skill, the greatest smith among the Elves who remained in Middle-earth after the First Age.

Celebrimbor settled in Eregion and founded the Gwaith-i-Mírdain, the People of the Jewel-smiths. They were the finest craftspeople in Middle-earth during the Second Age, dedicated to the exploration of craft for its own sake, to understanding how beautiful and powerful things could be made. They were located near the West Gate of Moria — Khazad-dûm, the great Dwarf realm under the Misty Mountains — and the relationship between the Noldorin smiths of Eregion and the Dwarves of Khazad-dûm was one of the most productive creative partnerships in the history of Middle-earth. From this partnership came the invention of mithril-working at its highest level, and the creation of the Doors of Durin — the great gates of Moria that Gandalf and the Fellowship would encounter nearly three thousand years later, inscribed with the ithildin letters that only show in starlight: *Speak, friend, and enter.*

The doors were made by Celebrimbor for Durin III, king of Khazad-dûm, as a symbol of the friendship between the two peoples. The word "friend" inscribed in them — the riddle that Gandalf solves by understanding it as a riddle rather than a command — is in Sindarin: Mellon. The doors open to the password because they were made to be opened by friends, and the friends they were made for spoke Elvish. The password is the simplest possible word: the point of the riddle is that it is not a riddle at all, just an instruction, and the Fellowship overthinks it because they expect a secret when the answer is plain trust.

Galadriel was also in Eregion for a time, or at least in contact with it — the exact narrative is complex and changed across Tolkien's different versions. In some accounts, she was the founding figure of Eregion; in others she was there only briefly; in still others Celeborn is associated with the founding. The published Silmarillion and the Unfinished Tales give somewhat different accounts. What matters for the main story is that Galadriel and Celebrimbor were the two most important figures in the Elvish culture of Eregion, and that Galadriel's departure — which some accounts place before Annatar's arrival and some after — left Celebrimbor without the wisest counsel available to him at the moment when he most needed it.

## Part 5: Annatar — The Lord of Gifts Arrives

Around the year 1200 of the Second Age, a visitor came to Eregion. He was fair to look upon, spoke with great knowledge and persuasiveness, and called himself Annatar — the Lord of Gifts. He claimed to be an emissary of the Valar, sent to assist the Elvish peoples of Middle-earth in their works.

Gil-galad and Elrond refused to receive him. Their perception of his true nature was sufficiently acute that they rejected him immediately and warned the other Elvish peoples against him. Círdan also refused him. These three were the most perceptive Elvish lords in Middle-earth, and their rejection should have been conclusive.

It was not. Celebrimbor welcomed Annatar to Eregion.

This was not pure foolishness on Celebrimbor's part. Sauron came with genuine knowledge — he knew more of the craft of making powerful objects than any being in Middle-earth, because as a Maia of Aulë he had understood the deep principles of craft since before the world was made, and as Morgoth's lieutenant he had spent the First Age applying that knowledge in ways that produced real results. What he taught the smiths of Eregion was real. The techniques he shared were genuinely advanced, genuinely useful, genuinely extensions of what they already knew. The things they made together were extraordinary.

Tolkien is very careful about this. Sauron's deception was not crude. He did not simply lie and pretend to know things he didn't. He taught real knowledge to real craftspeople who were genuinely interested in craft. The corruption was in the intent — in the fact that the knowledge was being shared not out of love of craft but in order to make the craftspeople dependent on Sauron's framework, in order to create a situation in which Sauron's influence permeated their greatest work, in order to make them unknowing participants in his plan.

Together, Sauron and the Gwaith-i-Mírdain made the Rings of Power.

## Part 6: The Rings of Power — Their Nature and Their Purpose

The Rings of Power were not all made at once, and they were not all made the same way, and understanding the distinctions matters.

The lesser rings — small rings of various powers, used as tests of technique and as gifts — were made by the smiths of Eregion under Sauron's guidance in some cases and independently in others. They were not greatly powerful, and their significance in the later history is minimal.

The Seven and the Nine were made with Sauron's direct participation in the process. The Seven were made for Dwarf-lords: seven great rings intended to extend the natural Dwarvish qualities — their resistance to corruption, their love of gold, their skill in craft — while also binding them to Sauron's will. The Nine were made for mortal Men: nine rings intended to extend Men's lives and power indefinitely, while binding them completely to Sauron.

But before the Seven and Nine, there were three: the Three Rings of the Elves.

The Three Rings — Narya, the Ring of Fire; Nenya, the Ring of Water; Vilya, the Ring of Air — were made by Celebrimbor himself, without Sauron's direct participation in their making. This is crucial. They were made using the knowledge Sauron had shared, which was genuine knowledge, but they were not made by Sauron's hands. Their power was derived from Elvish craft informed by Sauron's teachings but not corrupted by Sauron's direct will.

Their purpose was preservation: to maintain beauty, to slow decay, to keep safe what would otherwise fade. They were not weapons. They were not instruments of domination. They were the expression of the deepest Elvish desire — to hold what was good against the passage of time, to prevent the diminishment that the world always brings. Rivendell under Vilya, Lothlórien under Nenya, the Havens under Narya — these are places where time moves differently, where beauty endures beyond what the world would otherwise allow, because the Three Rings are working constantly to preserve them.

Then Sauron returned to Mordor and made the One Ring.

## Part 7: The One Ring — Its Forging and Its Purpose

In the fires of Orodruin, in the year 1600 of the Second Age, Sauron forged the One Ring. The process required him to pour into it a great portion of his own power, his own will, his own capacity for domination — so that the Ring was not merely a magical object but an extension of Sauron himself, a piece of his very self in physical form. This is why destroying the Ring would destroy Sauron: not metaphorically, but literally. The Ring contained enough of what made Sauron Sauron that without it, his capacity to reconstitute himself was gone.

The Ring was made to control all the other Rings of Power — to be the master ring, the one ring that could govern all the others and through them govern all who wore the others. The inscription on the Ring, written in the Black Speech of Mordor, states this purpose directly:

*Ash nazg durbatulûk, ash nazg gimbatul,*
*ash nazg thrakatulûk agh burzum-ishi krimpatul.*

One Ring to rule them all, One Ring to find them,
One Ring to bring them all and in the darkness bind them.

The theory of the Ring's control is sophisticated and reflects Sauron's fundamental nature as a craftsman-turned-controller. When he wore the One Ring and the bearers of the other rings wore their rings, the One Ring would extend his will through the connections between rings, reaching into the minds and wills of the ring-bearers and bending them toward his purpose. The ring-bearers would not know what was happening — they would feel their own wills strengthened, their own power enhanced, while gradually the Ring's master influence seeped through.

The moment he put it on, the Elves knew. Every Elf who wore one of the great Rings felt his thought reaching for them, felt the attempt at domination, and they took the Rings off immediately. This was the failure of Sauron's plan: he had not anticipated that Celebrimbor would have made the Three in a way that was pure enough that their bearers could perceive the difference between Sauron's influence through the One and their own experience of the Three unconnected. They felt it as an intrusion, an alien will, and refused it.

Sauron was furious. He demanded the Rings. Celebrimbor refused. Sauron made war on Eregion.

## Part 8: The War of the Elves and Sauron — The Destruction of Eregion

The war that followed — from approximately 1693 to 1701 of the Second Age — was the most catastrophic conflict of the Second Age's first half. Sauron's armies came out of Mordor and struck west, sweeping through the lands between the Misty Mountains and the Ered Luin with devastating speed. Eregion was overwhelmed; Celebrimbor was captured.

Sauron tortured Celebrimbor for the location of the Rings. Celebrimbor told him where the Seven and the Nine were — they were in Eregion, and Sauron recovered them. But he would not tell Sauron where the Three had gone. He had sent the Three away before the war began — Nenya to Galadriel, Vilya and Narya to Gil-galad (who later gave Vilya to Elrond and Narya to Círdan, and Círdan gave Narya in turn to Gandalf when Gandalf arrived in the Third Age). Sauron did not get the Three.

Celebrimbor was killed — shot with arrows, and his body was used as a battle-standard, carried before Sauron's army as a banner of terror. He was the last of the House of Fëanor, and he died as his grandfather had died: in the service of something he loved, killed by the enemy, leaving his greatest works behind him to be used by others in ways he could not have predicted.

The forces of Gil-galad, aided by a Númenórean fleet that arrived just in time, stopped Sauron from overrunning the entire northwest of Middle-earth. The Númenórean intervention was decisive — their soldiers were among the greatest fighters in the world, and their ships gave Gil-galad's forces a strategic mobility that Sauron's armies could not match. Sauron was driven back, but he had the Seven and the Nine, and Eregion was destroyed, and Moria closed its doors against all comers — the Dwarves of Khazad-dûm sealed themselves in for safety, and the great partnership between Elves and Dwarves in the west was over.

Gil-galad established Rivendell — Imladris — as a fortress refuge in a hidden valley of the Misty Mountains during this war. Elrond commanded it. It began as a military camp and became, over centuries, the great house of learning and counsel that it is in The Lord of the Rings. The fact that Rivendell was founded during a desperate war, as a military necessity, is not obvious from its Third Age appearance of serene beauty and scholarship — but it is always there in the background.

## Part 9: The Nine — Mortal Men Doomed to Die

With the Nine Rings of Men in his possession, Sauron made his greatest and most terrible gift to mortal Men. He gave the Nine to nine great lords of Men — kings, sorcerers, warriors, the most powerful Men of the age — and they accepted.

At first, the Nine Rings seemed to fulfil their promise. The nine men who wore them became powerful — more powerful than they had been, possessed of vision and knowledge and long life. They felt the Ring's power as an extension of their own. They did not feel Sauron's influence at first; it came gradually, as the Rings extended their owners' lives beyond the natural Mannish span, year by year thinning the mortal substance and replacing it with something wraith-like, something that existed more in the spirit-world than in the physical.

The transition took different lengths of time for different bearers — the more powerful and pure the bearer, the longer the resistance. But the end was the same for all nine: they became the Nazgûl, the Ringwraiths, the Úlairi. They did not die; they could not die as long as their rings existed; but they ceased to exist as men and became instead shadows, beings of will and malice that had once been men, hollow, entirely subject to Sauron's command. They were utterly his, more completely than any other servant — even the Balrogs had retained something of their original Maiar nature. The Nazgûl had nothing left that was originally theirs except perhaps the faintest echo of what they had been.

The chief of the nine is the Witch-king of Angmar, who in the Third Age would destroy the kingdom of Arnor, break the line of Arvedui, and challenge the gates of Minas Tirith on the night of the Battle of the Pelennor Fields. His original identity is not known for certain in any published Tolkien text — there are hints and suggestions in various essays and notes, but no definitive answer. One theory, not confirmed by Tolkien, suggests he may have been a Númenórean king; Tolkien's note in Unfinished Tales mentions that three of the nine were great Númenórean lords. His power was such that no living man could kill him — a prophecy that would prove to matter enormously.

The Seven Rings of the Dwarves had different effects. Dwarves were made by Aulë and given genuine life by Ilúvatar, and their nature was not susceptible to the same corruption as Men's. The Seven could not subjugate Dwarf-lords as the Nine subjugated Men — the Dwarves were too stubborn, too insular, too focused on their own purposes to be bent to another's will in that way. What the Seven did instead was to amplify the Dwarves' existing nature: their desire for gold, their tendency to hoard. Each Dwarf-lord who wore a ring became fabulously wealthy — but also increasingly obsessed, increasingly unable to think of anything but treasure. Three of the Seven were eventually consumed by dragon-fire. The remaining four were reclaimed by Sauron over time through various means.

## Part 10: Númenor in the Second Age — The Long Decline

Let us return to Númenor, because while all of this was happening in Middle-earth, the island kingdom was undergoing its own long transformation.

The earliest Númenórean kings maintained the traditions established by Elros: reverence for Ilúvatar, friendship with the Elves, the three sacred festivals on Meneltarma, the prohibition on sailing west toward Valinor accepted as a condition of the gift. These were the years of the "Kings' Men" versus the "Faithful" — a distinction that took many generations to become a fracture but was always latent in Númenórean culture.

The issue was always mortality. The Númenóreans lived long lives — three hundred, four hundred years — but they lived them watching the Elves of Lindon seemingly unchanged, seemingly permanent, seemingly immune to the diminishment that eventually came to even the longest-lived Númenórean. The Elves could visit Númenor — they brought gifts, they shared knowledge, they maintained the friendship — but the friendship always had this ache at its center: the Elves would go on, and the Númenóreans would die. Even with five times the ordinary human lifespan, they would die.

Morgoth had whispered to the earliest Men that death was a curse, not a gift. That lie was slow-acting but effective. The Númenóreans, with their long lives and their Elvish friendships, had more time than ordinary men to feel the bitterness of mortality, more occasions to compare their own finite existence with the apparent permanence of their Elvish allies. And as the generations passed, the whisper became a preoccupation.

Several of the mid-period kings pushed the boundaries of the Ban without formally breaking it. They began sailing further west, looking toward Valinor from a greater distance, testing whether the Valar would react. The Valar did not react with force — they sent a message, but they did not send armies. This was interpreted as weakness by some Númenóreans. It was actually patience, and it was also a deliberate policy: the Valar respected the free will of the Children even when they were using it badly, and they would not coerce except at the very last extremity.

The great political division in Númenor crystallised around the attitude toward the Ban. The Faithful — concentrated in the region of Andúnië in the west of the island, under the lords of the House of Andúnië, from whom Elendil would eventually come — maintained the old ways: reverence for Ilúvatar, friendship with the Elves, acceptance of the Ban as the condition of the gift. The King's Men — the majority, who grew increasingly dominant as the centuries passed — believed that Númenor had outgrown the need to accept conditions, that their own power and achievement entitled them to more than they had been given.

The King's Men began to resent Elvish influence rather than welcoming it. They began to neglect the Elvish languages — Quenya fell from favour, becoming associated with the Faithful's loyalism — and to develop Adûnaic as the language of Númenórean identity and pride. They began to treat the Men of Middle-earth as subjects rather than as people to be helped — the early generosity of the Númenórean mariners curdled into something more like colonial exploitation. They extracted tribute and resources and left little in return.

The Elvish mariners brought reports of this change to Gil-galad and Elrond, who watched the decline with grief.

## Part 11: Ar-Pharazôn — The Last King

The final catastrophe of Númenor came with its last king: Ar-Pharazôn the Golden, whose original name was Pharazôn. He was a great warrior and a charismatic leader, and he came to the throne by what amounted to a coup: he married his cousin Míriel, who was the legitimate heir, against her will (and against Númenórean law, which did not allow forced marriages of this kind), and took the sceptre.

Míriel's Quenya name was Tar-Míriel — she had received the sceptre-name because the law of Númenor permitted women to rule. Her father Tar-Palantir had been a King of the Faithful, who had tried to reverse the long decline, who had reinstated the Elvish names and the reverence for Ilúvatar, who had spent his reign attempting to undo three centuries of corruption. His daughter was his heir and shared his values. Ar-Pharazôn's seizure of the throne was the end of that attempt at reversal.

Ar-Pharazôn wanted to be the greatest king who had ever lived. He wanted Númenor to be the dominant power in the world, acknowledged by everyone including the Elves and whatever powers remained from the Elder Days. He heard that Sauron — who was growing in strength in Middle-earth and who had taken the title Lord of the Earth — was challenging Númenórean interests in Middle-earth, attacking their settlements and trading posts and in some cases enslaving their sailors.

He assembled the greatest fleet and army that Númenor had ever mustered and sailed to Middle-earth to challenge Sauron directly.

Sauron saw the fleet coming. He did not fight. He came down to the shore of Middle-earth — alone, unarmed, in fair form — and surrendered. It was the cleverest thing he ever did.

He submitted completely. He allowed himself to be brought to Númenor as a captive — or so the Númenóreans believed. He allowed himself to be displayed as a trophy of Ar-Pharazôn's greatest victory. He answered all questions with apparent candour. He was charming, learned, brilliant — all of which was entirely genuine; Sauron was all of those things — and he had the specific advantage of understanding exactly what the Númenóreans wanted to hear, because he had spent millennia studying the weaknesses of Men.

Within a few years, he was Ar-Pharazôn's chief counsellor.

Within a few more years, he had persuaded Ar-Pharazôn to do things that no Númenórean had done before.

## Part 12: Sauron's Seduction of Númenor — The Temple of Morgoth

Sauron's approach to corrupting Númenor was methodical and patient. He began with things that the more extreme of the King's Men already believed — that the Valar were not the true powers, that Ilúvatar was not necessarily the ultimate deity, that there might be other forces in the universe worth cultivating. He presented himself as someone who had knowledge of these other forces, ancient secrets that the Valar had concealed from Men.

Then he introduced Morgoth.

He told the Númenóreans that Morgoth — whom he called by the old name, Melkor the Giver of Freedom — was the true power in the universe, the force that would ultimately prevail, whose dominion was permanent and the Valar's only temporary. He told them that worshipping Morgoth was the rational choice of people who saw clearly. He told them that death, which they feared, was the weapon of the Valar used to keep Men subjugated, and that Morgoth offered an escape from it.

He persuaded Ar-Pharazôn to build a temple in Armenelos — the first temple ever built in Númenor, because the Númenóreans had always worshipped on the open summit of Meneltarma, without walls, without structures, because Ilúvatar's sacred space needed no man-made form. This temple was enormous: five hundred feet high, with a dome of silver sheathed in gold. It was built to Morgoth.

On the altar of that temple, the first human sacrifices in Númenórean history were offered. The victims were initially criminals and prisoners, then the Faithful — those Númenóreans who maintained the old worship, who still used the Elvish names, who still regarded Ilúvatar as the proper object of reverence. The Faithful were designated enemies of the state. Some were killed in the temple. Others fled to Middle-earth, establishing refugee communities on the shores of the Bay of Belfalas, settlements that would eventually become the kernel of Gondor and Arnor.

Meneltarma itself, the sacred mountain, was not defiled — the Faithful who survived said that Morgoth's servants were afraid to go there, that some power still protected the summit. But the three sacred festivals were abandoned. The White Tree — Nimloth the Fair, a cutting of Celeborn's White Tree of Eregion, which was itself descended from Galathilion, given to Númenor by the Elves in the early Second Age — was felled and burned on Sauron's altar, because it was a symbol of the old friendship with the Elves and their Valar-given ways.

A young man of the Faithful, Isildur son of Elendil, crept into the courts of Armenelos by night — while the cutting was still young and had not yet been felled — and stole a fruit from it. He was wounded by guards in his escape. The fruit he had stolen, carried to safety in Middle-earth, would eventually grow into the White Tree of Gondor. From that tree, through generations of cuttings and descendant saplings, the White Tree would continue into the Fourth Age — the same genetic line as the Two Trees of Valinor, preserved through the entire history of the world. The moment Isildur bled to recover that fruit is one of the most significant acts in the history of Númenor, however unnoticed it seemed at the time.

---

## Part 13: The Great Armament — Ar-Pharazôn Sails West

Sauron had been working toward one specific goal throughout his time in Númenor: to get the Númenóreans to sail against Valinor. His reasoning was not quite what the Númenóreans believed it was. He told them that Valinor held the secret of immortality — that by conquering the Blessed Realm, they would attain the eternal life that the Valar had withheld from them. He may have believed this himself, in some perverted way; or he may have been calculating purely in terms of what would cause the maximum destruction.

The maximum destruction, if the Númenóreans attacked Valinor, would be the destruction of Númenor by Ilúvatar's direct action. And with Númenor destroyed, the threat that Númenórean power posed to Sauron's own ambitions would be permanently eliminated. He was gambling — betting his own physical body and his own immediate comfort against the permanent removal of the one mortal power in the world capable of opposing him. It was a reasonable gamble from his perspective, if a desperate one.

Ar-Pharazôn spent years preparing the fleet that the Númenóreans called the Great Armament. It was the largest fleet in the world's history — not hundreds of ships but thousands, filled with soldiers and weapons, prepared for war against Valinor itself. The description in the Akallabêth is one of Tolkien's most powerful pieces of writing: the fleet so enormous that the ports of Númenor could barely contain it, the armour and banners making the sea bright for miles, the trumpets sounding as the fleet left harbour in a display of pride so absolute it had moved beyond ordinary hubris into something genuinely blasphemous.

Tar-Míriel — Ar-Pharazôn's wife, the legitimate queen, the one whose throne had been stolen — tried to climb Meneltarma as the fleet departed. She was going to pray. The wave caught her before she reached the summit. This small, human detail in the Akallabêth's account of the cataclysm is one of Tolkien's finest: the righteous queen, trying to do the right thing at the last moment, not quite reaching it.

Manwë saw the fleet coming. He laid down his governance of Arda — in some sense, he stepped back from his own authority, because what was about to happen exceeded anything that could be governed by the rules of the world as they currently stood. He called upon Ilúvatar. And Ilúvatar answered directly — one of the very few moments in the history of Arda where Ilúvatar acts within the world rather than through intermediaries.

The sea opened. The great cleft that swallowed Númenor was not a tsunami or a volcanic event; it was the sea making a hole and the island sinking into it, the physical world obeying a direct divine instruction. Ar-Pharazôn's fleet was overwhelmed — buried under falling mountains at the shores of Aman, crushed by the waves. The king and his warriors were entombed in the Caves of the Forgotten, which are described in the Akallabêth as a kind of living death — they did not die immediately but were imprisoned in the foundations of the world, held there until the Dagor Dagorath, the Last Battle.

Númenor sank in a matter of hours. The island that had been raised from the sea by the Valar's art was taken back beneath the sea by Ilúvatar's will. The populations who had not fled — the great majority — drowned with it. The Temple of Morgoth. The gardens of Armenelos. The White Tree's stump. All of it gone.

Meneltarma alone, some traditions say, remained above the waves as a small island in the western ocean. If any mariner ever found it, no record survives.

---

## Part 14: The Change of the World — Aman Removed

The Downfall of Númenor did not only destroy an island. It reshaped the world.

The Valar, recognising that the world as it had been constructed was no longer appropriate for the age that was coming — that if Men could build a fleet large enough to reach Valinor, the protection of the Blessed Realm needed to be something other than geographical — made a change to the very structure of Arda. They bent the world.

Before the Downfall, the world was flat — or rather, it was a disc, and a ship sailing far enough west would eventually reach the shores of Aman. This is the cosmology implicit in the First Age's geography, and it explains why the Númenóreans could sail to Middle-earth from an island considerably west of the mainland without crossing any impenetrable barrier. After the Downfall, the Valar removed Aman from the circles of the world. Valinor was taken off the flat disc and placed somewhere that ordinary sailing could not reach. The world was bent into a sphere, and the sea that the Númenóreans had sailed was now a curved ocean that led only to more ocean and eventually back to where you started.

This has enormous cosmological implications for everything afterward. The world of the Second and Third Ages is spherical — the world as we experience it. But within that spherical world, there is a straight road — visible only to the Elves, navigable only by those the Valar permit. The straight road leads to Valinor even though the bent world does not. It runs above the curvature of the earth, following the original path. When Elves sail west from the Grey Havens in their swan-ships, they are taking the straight road, departing from the curved world into the uncurved realm beyond it.

Men cannot take the straight road. They sail into the west and the road curves beneath them and they come back east, or they sail too far and fall off the edge of the world — which is not a flat-edge but something more complex, the boundary of Eä itself. This is why the passage of the Ring-bearers at the end of The Return of the King is extraordinary: Frodo and Bilbo, mortal Hobbits, are permitted to take the straight road because of what they have carried and endured. It is a grace extended beyond their nature.

---

## Part 15: The Faithful — Elendil and the Survivors

Not everyone in Númenor was lost. The Faithful had been watching the preparations for the Great Armament with increasing dread, and the wisest of them — Elendil son of Amandil, lord of Andúnië — had made preparations of his own.

Amandil, his father, was the last lord of Andúnië and a man of the old Faithful tradition. He had attempted to sail to Valinor to beg the Valar to intervene before the catastrophe, knowing that this was against the Ban but hoping that the urgency of the situation might justify it. He was never heard from again — whether he reached Valinor or was lost at sea is not stated. His disappearance into the west before the Downfall has a certain quiet heroism: he tried to do the right thing, knowing he might fail, and apparently did fail, and is remembered for the attempt.

Elendil had nine ships prepared in secret, loaded with goods and people and the most important heirlooms of the Faithful: the palantíri — the seeing stones, gifts from the Elves — and the fruit of Nimloth the Fair that Isildur had rescued, and other things of value. When the Downfall came, his ships were pushed by an enormous wind — which some accounts attribute to Manwë's mercy — and they were carried east, away from the destruction, and came to the shores of Middle-earth.

There were two groups of survivors. Elendil himself with his sons Anárion and Isildur landed in different places. Elendil came to the northern shores of Middle-earth and founded Arnor — the North Kingdom — in the lands of Eriador, which had been largely unsettled except for scattered communities of Men. Anárion and Isildur together founded Gondor — the South Kingdom — in the lands between the White Mountains and the sea, which had already had Númenórean settlements since the earlier centuries of the Second Age when the Faithful had been fleeing Númenórean persecution.

The kingdoms they founded were Númenórean in culture, language, and spirit — the continuation of the Faithful's tradition, transplanted to Middle-earth. Their capital of Gondor was Osgiliath, the Citadel of the Stars, built where the river Anduin divided into two channels. They built Minas Anor — the Tower of the Sun, later renamed Minas Tirith — to the west of Osgiliath, and Minas Ithil — the Tower of the Moon, later renamed Minas Morgul — to the east, near the entrance to Mordor. They built Orthanc, the tower of Isengard. They recovered the palantíri and set them in the high towers of their kingdoms. They planted the seedling of Nimloth in Minas Anor, and it grew into the White Tree of Gondor.

And they found Sauron on their doorstep.

---

## Part 16: Sauron After the Downfall — A Changed Enemy

Sauron had not gone to Valinor with Ar-Pharazôn's fleet. He had done something that showed his essentially physical nature at this stage of his existence: he left his body behind. He used the full power of his Maia nature to depart his physical form before the Downfall, to escape as a pure spirit — a bat-shadow, the texts suggest, something horrible and formless — and to fly back to Middle-earth while his body was destroyed.

The loss of his body was not temporary. This is the key to understanding Sauron's situation in the Second Age after the Downfall and in the Third Age: he could not take a fair form again. He could reconstitute a physical form — he did, after a time — but it was no longer beautiful. It was dark and terrible: the Dark Lord as he appears in the Ring's vision in the Mirror of Galadriel, as he appears to the Elves and Men who encounter him. The fair form of Annatar was gone permanently. He could never again deceive by appearing as a beautiful being.

This changes his strategy completely. He could no longer seduce. He could only dominate. The subtlety of Annatar — the persuasive, gentle, apparently helpful figure who shares genuine knowledge in service of corruption — was gone. What remained was naked power and the threat of it. He became, in a literal sense as well as a moral one, monstrous.

He still had the One Ring. The nine Nazgûl were still his. He rebuilt Barad-dûr. He moved again toward domination. And he found the new kingdoms of Gondor and Arnor in his territory.

He attacked Minas Ithil — the Tower of the Moon — early in the history of Gondor, seizing it and turning it into Minas Morgul, the Tower of Sorcery, lair of the Witch-king. This was a direct provocation of the new kingdoms, and it required a direct response.

---

## Part 17: The Last Alliance of Elves and Men

In the year 3434 of the Second Age, Gil-galad King of the Noldor and Elendil King of Arnor and Gondor formed the Last Alliance of Elves and Men. It was the last time the two kindreds would formally combine their military power, and it was also the largest army assembled in Middle-earth since the War of Wrath.

The army marched east from the Ered Luin. Elrond was there — he was Gil-galad's herald, as he had always been, and the memory of this army and this march would stay with him for the rest of the Third Age. He saw Númenórean pride at its best in those years — the discipline of Elendil's soldiers, the courage of Isildur and Anárion, the grandeur of what Gondor had built in so short a time. He also saw what war cost, even victorious war.

The army crossed the Misty Mountains. It came down into Rhovanion — the broad wilderland east of the mountains — and there it rested for a year, at Dagorlad, gathering strength and allies. The great plain of Dagorlad was itself a haunted place — later ages would add to its burden of grief, but even then it had seen death.

Then the army crossed the Dead Marshes and came to the gates of Mordor.

The siege of Barad-dûr lasted seven years. This is an astonishing length of time for a military siege, and it tells us something about both sides. Sauron's fortress, built with the One Ring's power, could not be taken quickly even by the combined might of the Last Alliance. The Ring had bound too much of Sauron's will into the Tower's foundations. The Alliance had to wait him out, or find a way to draw him forth.

During those seven years, Anárion was killed by a stone thrown from the walls of Barad-dûr. He was struck on the head, and died. This loss was profound for Gondor — Anárion had been the more present of the two royal brothers, the one who remained in the south while Isildur went north to support the siege. His death was a foreshadowing of the long diminishment that would follow.

Then Sauron came out. He came himself, in his terrible physical form, and fought on the slopes of Orodruin. The battle on the slopes of Mount Doom is one of the most consequential moments in the entire history of Middle-earth, because everything that happens in The Lord of the Rings flows from what happened there.

Gil-galad was killed. Elendil was killed. They fought Sauron together — the Elven-king and the Númenórean king against the Dark Lord — and both were killed. But Elendil's sword Narsil was not destroyed. It broke under him as he fell, but its pieces remained.

Isildur took the hilt-shard of Narsil and used it to cut the One Ring from Sauron's hand.

The Ring left Sauron's finger. His physical form collapsed — he could not maintain it without the Ring's sustaining power. His spirit fled, reduced to a shadow, barely capable of coherent existence. For nearly three thousand years, he could not take form again.

---

## Part 18: Isildur's Choice — The Seed of Everything

Isildur held the Ring. He had cut it from the hand of the Dark Lord. He had the power to destroy it — he stood on the slopes of Orodruin, the only place in the world where it could be unmade.

He did not destroy it.

Elrond and Círdan urged him to cast it into the fire. They told him clearly that it must be destroyed. Isildur refused. He claimed the Ring as his own — as weregild for his father and his brother, as a prize of victory, as his by right of conquest. He said he would keep it in memory of Elendil his father.

Tolkien does not condemn Isildur simplistically for this. The Ring was already working on him — its influence was immediate, its persuasiveness real. No ordinary Man could hold the Ring moments after it left Sauron's hand and remain entirely clear-sighted about what to do with it. The Ring whispered to him that it was a prize, that he deserved it, that destruction would be waste. He heard what he wanted to hear and went.

But the choice was still his, on whatever level the choice could be said to be anyone's under the Ring's influence. And it was the choice that made everything else necessary — the long watch of the North Kingdom, the guardianship of the Rangers of the North, the loss of Gondor's strength, the eventual rise of the Necromancer in Mirkwood, the need for the Fellowship, the journey to the Crack of Doom. Every event in The Lord of the Rings begins here, on the slopes of Orodruin, with Isildur closing his hand around the Ring and saying: I will keep it.

Two years after the Downfall of Sauron, Isildur was killed in the Gladden Fields. He was ambushed by Orcs while crossing the marshes of the Anduin — Orcs who were still numerous in the Misty Mountains, leaderless now but still dangerous. He had the Ring on his finger, trying to use its invisibility to escape through the river, and the Ring slipped off. The Ring betrayed him. It chose — in whatever way a Ring can choose — to abandon him, to seek Sauron rather than serve Isildur. It slipped from his finger into the cold water of the Anduin, and Isildur was shot by Orc arrows in the shallows. He died. The Ring was lost.

The Second Age ended in that same year, 3441, with Sauron's defeat and Isildur's assumption of authority. The Third Age began with Isildur's death two years later, in what is conventionally reckoned as Year 2 of the Third Age.

---

## Part 19: What the Second Age Left Behind — An Accounting

The Second Age lasted 3,441 years. At its end, the accounting of what had been made and what had been lost was:

Lost: Númenor, the greatest human civilisation in the world's history, drowned. The direct Númenórean bloodline was broken — there were survivors, but no line of pure descent from Elros existed anymore; the Faithful were of mixed heritage. The Two Kingdoms of Arnor and Gondor were the inheritors, but they were already weakened by the Siege of Barad-dûr's cost.

Lost: Gil-galad, the last High King of the Noldor in Middle-earth. With his death, there would never again be a High King of the Noldor in Middle-earth. The Noldorin line was essentially extinguished in the male line — Elrond was Half-elven, not purely Noldorin, and he was a counsellor not a king. The Noldor continued as a people through the Third Age but without kingship.

Lost: Sauron's physical form, though not his existence.

Lost: The One Ring, in the Gladden Fields.

Remaining: The Three Rings of the Elves — Narya, Nenya, Vilya — which had been uncorrupted by the One Ring's making and which would maintain the beauty of Rivendell and Lothlórien and the Havens through the Third Age.

Remaining: The Nine Nazgûl, still existing, still the servants of Sauron though without his direct presence to guide them.

Remaining: Elrond, Galadriel, Círdan — the great remaining Elvish lords who had lived through the entire Second Age and who would guide and counsel the peoples of Middle-earth through the Third.

Remaining: The White Tree, planted by Isildur's courage in Minas Anor.

Remaining: The shards of Narsil, Elendil's broken sword, kept in Rivendell where they would wait three thousand years for the heir who could reforge them.

And remaining, though everyone thought it lost: the One Ring, on the bed of the Anduin, waiting.

## Part 20: A Note on Sauron's Nature — Why He Could Not Simply Win

The Second Age raises a persistent question: Sauron was clearly the most powerful single entity in Middle-earth during most of its duration. Why didn't he simply conquer everything?

The answer is in the nature of his power and his fundamental mistake. When Sauron forged the One Ring, he poured into it a great portion of his own power — his capacity for domination, his will, his creative force. This was not a temporary loan; it was a permanent transfer. The Ring held that power, and without it, Sauron was significantly diminished. With it, he was enormously amplified. But this meant that whenever the Ring was not on his finger — whenever it was lost, or held by an enemy, or simply not near him — he was less than he would have been if he had never made it.

He had bet his power on a single gambit: that the Ring, binding all other Rings to it, would give him such total control that the risk was worth it. He almost won that gamble — in the Second Age's middle centuries, when the Nazgûl were fully in his service and the free peoples were fragmented and Númenor had not yet moved against him, he was very close to dominion over all of Middle-earth. The Númenórean intervention stopped him, and then Númenor's own destruction and the Last Alliance finished the job.

But the Ring survived. And as long as the Ring survived, Sauron could return. This is the fundamental structure of Tolkien's Third Age: the incomplete victory of the Last Alliance, the lost Ring, and the slow return of the shadow.

Everything in The Lord of the Rings is the consequence of Sauron having made the Ring in the year 1600 of the Second Age and having poured too much of himself into it to ever fully win without it — and too much of himself into it to ever be truly gone while it existed.

---

## Conclusion: The Age That Made Everything

The Second Age is the age that made the Third Age. The Rings of Power, Sauron's permanent hideous form, the founding of Gondor and Arnor, the creation of the Nazgûl, the death of Gil-galad, the breaking of Narsil, the loss of the Ring in the Anduin — all of these are Second Age events whose consequences dominate every page of The Lord of the Rings.

The Downfall of Númenor is also the moment that most directly parallels the Fall in Tolkien's mythology — the moment when the greatest human civilisation reached for what was withheld and was destroyed by the reaching. And like the Fall in many traditions, it is not simply a punishment: it is a consequence, the natural and logical result of what was done. The Númenóreans who sailed against Valinor were destroyed not because Ilúvatar is cruel but because the act was its own destruction — you cannot make war on goodness and survive the attempt.

The Second Age also gives us the deepest portrait of Sauron. In the First Age he was Morgoth's lieutenant, skilled and terrible but not the primary figure. In the Second Age he is the centre of the story: the patient rebuilder, the subtle seducer of Annatar, the maker of the Ring, the destroyer of Númenor's moral fabric, the near-victor of the Last Alliance. He is at his most intelligent and most dangerous in the Second Age — and at his most instructive, because we can see exactly how his mind works, what his vulnerabilities are, and what it would take to defeat him.

The Third Age is both simpler and more complex: simpler because Sauron's power is lesser and his methods cruder (he can no longer appear beautiful, can no longer seduce with knowledge); more complex because the Ring has been loose in the world for three thousand years and has accumulated its own story.
