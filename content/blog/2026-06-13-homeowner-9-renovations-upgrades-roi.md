## Part 1: The Renovation Temptation

Within months of moving into a new home, most owners begin making a list. The kitchen is dated. The master bathroom has the same tiles it had in 1994. The basement is unfinished. The garage needs epoxy flooring. The backyard needs a deck. The list grows.

Renovation is one of the great joys of home ownership — the freedom to make a space exactly the way you want it. It is also one of the great financial traps, particularly for first-time owners who are learning the economics of their new situation as they go.

This post is about making renovation decisions intelligently. It covers which projects genuinely add value, which ones are financially questionable, how to manage the renovation process without being taken advantage of, how permits work and why they are not optional, and what to expect when you take on a major project for the first time.

## Part 2: The Return on Investment Reality

Every spring, Remodeling Magazine publishes its Cost vs. Value Report, which surveys real estate professionals across the country about how much specific renovation projects return at resale. The results are consistently instructive and consistently surprising to homeowners who expect their renovations to pay for themselves.

**The short version:** No renovation project consistently returns 100 cents on the dollar. Most return 50 to 80 cents on the dollar at resale. A few specific projects — primarily exterior improvements and certain energy-efficient upgrades — return more than 100 cents in recent years. The more personalized and luxury-level a renovation, the lower its resale return tends to be.

This does not mean you should not renovate. It means you should renovate with clear eyes about why you are doing it:
- **If you are renovating for your own enjoyment and quality of life:** The ROI calculation is less relevant. You derive value from the improvement for years before you sell. This is a legitimate reason to renovate.
- **If you are renovating specifically to add value before a sale:** Focus rigorously on the projects that real estate professionals in your specific market confirm buyers value. Consult a real estate agent before spending money.
- **If you are renovating to bring the home to market standard:** Updating a kitchen or bathroom that is dramatically below the standard for your neighborhood and price range is different from a luxury upgrade. Baseline improvements often do pay off.

### Projects That Tend to Return the Most

The following categories have historically shown strong returns in Remodeling Magazine's Cost vs. Value surveys and in the experience of real estate professionals. Note that returns vary significantly by region, price range, and current market conditions.

**Garage door replacement:** Consistently one of the highest-ROI projects — often returning 90% to 100%+ of cost. A new garage door dramatically improves curb appeal, is immediately visible from the street, and represents a functional safety upgrade. Cost: $1,200 to $4,000 installed for a quality steel or composite door.

**Entry door replacement (steel):** A new steel entry door with a modern lockset and perhaps a sidelight improves both curb appeal and security. Returns roughly 70% to 100%+ in most markets. Cost: $1,000 to $3,000 installed.

**Minor kitchen remodel:** A "minor" remodel — replacing cabinet doors and hardware, installing new countertops, updating the sink and faucet, replacing the appliances, and painting — returns roughly 70% to 85% of cost in most markets. This is the sweet spot: meaningful visual and functional improvement without the cost of a full gut renovation. Cost: $15,000 to $30,000 depending on materials and market.

**Deck addition (wood):** Adding a wood deck where none exists returns roughly 65% to 75% in most markets. In regions with strong outdoor living culture (Pacific Northwest, Mountain West, suburban South), returns can be higher. Cost: $15,000 to $35,000 for a well-built wood deck.

**Manufactured stone veneer accent:** Adding stone veneer to a portion of the facade (typically the front foundation or an entry accent) dramatically improves curb appeal at relatively modest cost and returns 90%+ in many markets. Cost: $8,000 to $15,000 for a partial facade treatment.

**Fiber cement siding replacement:** Replacing deteriorated siding with fiber cement (Hardie plank) returns roughly 70% to 85% and dramatically improves the home's first impression. Cost: $15,000 to $30,000 for a full exterior.

**Roof replacement:** A new roof is expected, not celebrated. Buyers do not pay a premium for a new roof — they expect the existing roof to be functional. But a failing roof is a significant negative. Replacing a visibly failing roof before sale returns close to 60% to 70% of cost because it removes a major buyer objection and enables the home to be sold at full market value. The lack of a major capital expense looming is priced in. Cost: $10,000 to $25,000.

**HVAC replacement:** Similar to the roof — buyers expect the HVAC to work. A new system removes a negotiation point and may justify a slight price premium. Returns roughly 50% to 65% of direct cost but enables full-price sale. Cost: $8,000 to $18,000.

**Bathroom addition (half bath):** Adding a half bath (toilet and sink, no shower or tub) where the home has fewer bathrooms than comparable properties in the neighborhood returns 50% to 70% of cost. A home with one full bath that competing homes have two is at a pricing disadvantage that this project helps correct. Cost: $5,000 to $15,000 depending on complexity.

### Projects That Tend to Return Less

**Major kitchen remodel:** The full gut renovation — moving appliances, new custom cabinetry, high-end countertops, new flooring throughout, new lighting, perhaps moving a wall — typically returns 50% to 65% of cost. The more expensive the kitchen relative to the home's price range, the lower the return. A $90,000 kitchen in a $350,000 house returns very little of that investment.

**Master bathroom remodel:** Full bathroom renovations — heated floors, steam showers, freestanding soaking tubs, double vanities — typically return 55% to 65% of cost. The luxury items that homeowners love cost the most and return the least.

**Basement finishing:** Converting unfinished basement space to livable square footage returns roughly 55% to 75% depending on quality and whether the market recognizes basement square footage at full value (in many markets, basement square footage is valued at 50% to 75% of above-grade square footage). Cost: $20,000 to $75,000+ depending on scope.

**Home office addition/conversion:** The pandemic-era surge in home office demand has faded somewhat. A dedicated home office is now an expected feature in many markets but not typically a significant premium item. Converting an existing bedroom to a home office reduces bedroom count, which typically reduces value.

**Sunroom addition:** A sunroom — a glass-enclosed space attached to the house — returns roughly 50% to 60% of cost in most markets. The space is expensive to build and adds conditioned square footage that is often less usable than main living space.

**Swimming pool:** A pool is the canonical example of a renovation that costs money and may reduce value. In hot climates (south Florida, Phoenix, Las Vegas) a pool is essentially expected on higher-end properties and its absence is a negative. In northern climates, a pool is a liability — buyers see it as a maintenance cost, a safety concern (particularly families with young children), and a liability risk. Returns vary from -10% to +30% depending entirely on climate and neighborhood norms. Never install a pool to add value; install one because you want it.

**Whole-house generator:** A standby generator is genuinely valued by buyers in areas with frequent power outages, but the return is typically 50% to 70% of cost. It is a feature that differentiates your home rather than driving a premium.

## Part 3: Before You Renovate — The Pre-Project Checklist

Before you spend a dollar on a renovation, answer these questions:

**Does your home have any deferred maintenance?** Renovating a kitchen in a house with a failing roof, a 20-year-old HVAC system, or plumbing that needs attention is putting lipstick on a pig. Address the fundamentals before the aesthetics. A buyer who walks through a beautiful kitchen and then discovers in the inspection that the HVAC is ancient and the roof needs replacement will renegotiate — and they should.

**Is your emergency fund fully funded?** A renovation that depletes your emergency fund leaves you financially exposed if something goes wrong — which it often does. Always keep the emergency fund intact.

**How long are you staying?** Renovations that make life better for you are worth doing if you have years to enjoy them. Renovating six months before a planned sale is almost always financially suboptimal — the ROI is worse (no enjoyment years to justify the full cost) and the timing is stressful.

**What does your neighborhood support?** The economic principle of "conformity" means that your home's value is pulled toward the median of the neighborhood. A $100,000 kitchen in a neighborhood of $250,000 homes will not produce a $350,000 home. Know the ceiling for your area before you invest significantly in luxury improvements.

**Have you gotten your permits?** Any work that requires a permit must have a permit. We will address this in detail in Part 5.

## Part 4: Finding, Vetting, and Managing Contractors

The contractor relationship is one of the most consequential and most stressful parts of the renovation process. A great contractor makes a difficult project manageable. A bad contractor turns a simple project into a nightmare. And the home renovation industry — particularly at the residential level — has more than its share of bad actors.

### The Hierarchy of How to Find a Contractor

**First choice — personal referral from a trusted source:** Someone you know, who has recently had similar work done, and who is happy with the result. Not a referral from someone who had work done three years ago and has not checked in since. A fresh referral from a trusted person is worth ten reviews on Angi.

**Second choice — local referral from an established professional:** Your real estate agent, your architect, your plumber's recommendation of an electrician, your HVAC company's recommendation of a roofer. These professionals have seen many contractors' work in real life and have reputational skin in the game when they refer someone.

**Third choice — verified online reviews across multiple platforms:** Google, Yelp, Houzz, Angi, HomeAdvisor, Nextdoor, the local Facebook neighborhood group. Look for:
- Volume of reviews (a contractor with 200 reviews is more meaningful than one with 5)
- Consistency across platforms (a contractor with 5 stars everywhere is more credible than 5 stars on one platform and 2 stars on another)
- Response to negative reviews (do they respond professionally, or defensively?)
- Recency (reviews from 5 years ago are less relevant than recent ones)
- Specificity (reviews that describe specific projects and outcomes are more useful than generic praise)

**Last choice — cold solicitation:** A contractor who contacts you (door knock, flyer, cold call) after a storm, a tree falls, or any other crisis is in the highest-risk category. Storm chasers and disaster-opportunist contractors are a well-documented problem. Be extremely cautious.

### The Verification Process

Before engaging any contractor for significant work:

**Verify their license.** Most states require licenses for general contractors, electricians, plumbers, HVAC technicians, and roofers. Search your state's contractor licensing board database for the contractor's name and license number. Verify the license is current and in good standing and that there are no disciplinary actions on record.

**Verify insurance.** Ask the contractor for a certificate of insurance showing:
- General liability insurance (protects your property if they damage something)
- Workers' compensation insurance (protects you if a worker is injured on your property)

Do not accept their word for it — request the actual certificate from their insurance broker, with your name and address listed as the certificate holder. Call the insurance company to verify the policy is in effect. An unlicensed or uninsured contractor creates significant legal and financial exposure for you.

**Check the Better Business Bureau.** Note the rating and any complaints. Unresolved complaints are a red flag.

**Ask for references from similar recent projects.** And then actually call them. Ask: Was the project completed on time? Was the final cost close to the estimate? Were there any problems and how were they handled? Would you hire this contractor again?

### Getting and Comparing Quotes

For any project over $2,000 to $3,000, get at least three quotes. For major projects ($15,000+), three to five quotes.

What a proper quote should include:
- A detailed scope of work — specific materials (brand, model, grade), specific tasks, specific quantities
- A timeline with start date and estimated completion date
- Payment schedule
- What is specifically excluded
- Warranty terms (both for labor and materials)
- A line-item breakdown, not just a total

**Comparing quotes:** The lowest quote is not always the best, and the highest quote is not always the best. A significantly lower quote often means:
- Lower-quality materials (verify what is actually being proposed)
- Cutting corners on labor
- A contractor who wins work by underbidding and then makes it up with change orders
- An unlicensed or uninsured contractor

A significantly higher quote may reflect:
- Higher-quality materials or labor
- More comprehensive scope of work
- Higher overhead (established company with insurance, workers' comp, trucks, and a physical office)
- Simply a higher margin

Compare quotes on the basis of equivalent scope and equivalent materials. Ask any contractor whose quote seems unusually low or high to explain the difference.

### The Contract

Never begin work without a written contract signed by both parties. At minimum, the contract should specify:
- Detailed scope of work (reference the quote document)
- Start date and estimated completion date
- Total contract price
- Payment schedule (see below)
- Change order process — any changes to scope must be agreed in writing before the work is done
- Warranty — how long the contractor warrants their labor, and what the process is for addressing defects
- Dispute resolution
- Insurance and license numbers
- Right to cancel (most consumer contracts in most states provide a 3-day right of rescission)

**Do not sign a contract that says "terms and conditions on the back" without reading the back.** Many contractor contracts contain one-sided arbitration clauses, limitation of liability clauses, and other terms that significantly affect your rights.

### The Payment Schedule — Protecting Yourself

The payment schedule is the primary financial protection mechanism in a renovation contract. The general principle: tie payments to completed, verified milestones, not to the calendar.

A typical payment structure for a major renovation:
- **10% to 20% deposit** at contract signing (to cover materials ordering and initial overhead)
- **30% to 40% at rough-in completion** (framing, plumbing rough, electrical rough, HVAC rough — verified by inspection if applicable)
- **30% to 40% at substantial completion** (everything done except minor punch list items)
- **10% at final completion** (punch list addressed, final inspection passed, final walkthrough completed)

**Never pay more than 20% to 30% upfront for a large project.** A contractor who demands 50% or more upfront is either financially unstable (they need your money to buy materials for other jobs) or not trustworthy. Either situation is a red flag.

**Never pay the final payment until the punch list is complete.** Once the contractor has all their money, their motivation to address small outstanding items evaporates. Retain the final payment until everything is done.

**Do not pay cash.** Pay by check or credit card. Cash payments cannot be documented, create tax issues, and give you no recourse if there is a dispute.

### Managing the Relationship During the Project

Check in regularly but do not micromanage. Walk through the project at major milestones. Document what you see with photos and dated notes. Communicate in writing — email or text — so there is a record. If something is wrong, address it immediately and in writing.

**Change orders:** Any deviation from the original scope of work — whether you requested it or the contractor discovered a surprise condition — should result in a written change order that specifies the additional work, the additional cost, and your signature approving both before the work proceeds. A contractor who does additional work without a signed change order and then presents you with an invoice for it at the end is engaging in a problematic billing practice. Insist on change orders for everything.

**Schedule delays:** Construction projects almost always take longer than estimated. This is normal to a point, but persistent delays with no explanation, the contractor disappearing for days at a time, or sub-contractors who show up for a day and then vanish for a week are all warning signs that the project is not being managed properly.

### When a Contractor Relationship Goes Wrong

If a contractor does poor work, abandons the project, or refuses to correct defects:

1. Document everything — photos, written communications, the contract, all payments made
2. Send a formal written demand by certified mail specifying the defects, the required remediation, and a deadline
3. If the contractor is unresponsive, file a complaint with:
   - Your state's contractor licensing board (they can investigate and potentially revoke the license)
   - The Better Business Bureau
   - Your state's Attorney General consumer protection office
4. If you have a substantial financial loss, consult an attorney — many states have consumer protection laws (little FTC acts) that provide for attorney's fees in contractor fraud cases
5. If the contractor is licensed, your state may have a contractor recovery fund that compensates consumers for losses caused by licensed contractors

**Mechanic's liens:** Be aware that subcontractors and material suppliers who are not paid by the general contractor can file a mechanic's lien on your property, even if you have paid the general contractor in full. In many states, you can protect yourself by requesting lien waivers from the contractor and major subcontractors as you make each payment. A "conditional lien waiver" releases the lien upon receipt of payment; an "unconditional lien waiver" releases it regardless of whether the check clears. Understand the lien law in your state for major projects.

## Part 5: Permits — Why They Are Not Optional

Permits are the least glamorous part of any renovation, and the most skipped. Homeowners skip them to save the permit fee, to avoid an inspection that might reveal other issues, or simply because the contractor suggested it to save time. All of these are short-sighted.

### What Requires a Permit

The specific list varies by jurisdiction, but in most places, the following require a permit:

- Any structural work (adding or removing load-bearing walls, opening up ceilings to expose roof structure, adding a room)
- Any new electrical circuits or panel work
- Any new plumbing — adding fixtures, moving existing fixtures, replacing a water heater (in most jurisdictions)
- HVAC installation or replacement of major equipment
- Roofing (in some jurisdictions)
- Additions of any kind
- Decks above a certain height (typically 30 inches off the ground)
- Fences above a certain height (typically 6 feet)
- Pools and hot tubs
- Finished basements that add habitable square footage
- Garage conversions to living space

Work that typically does not require a permit: painting, flooring, cabinet replacement (without moving plumbing or electrical), fixture replacement (replacing a toilet, sink, or faucet with an identical or similar fixture on an existing connection), landscaping.

### Why Permits Matter

**Safety.** Permits exist because permitted work is inspected by a code official. The inspector catches work that does not comply with the building code — improper structural connections, undersized wire, inadequately vented plumbing, missing fire-blocking. Code is not arbitrary bureaucracy; it is distilled knowledge about how buildings fail and people get hurt. An inspector is your ally.

**Insurance.** Unpermitted work may not be covered by your homeowner's insurance. If an unpermitted addition burns down, your insurer may deny the claim or reduce the payout, arguing that the work was not done to code and the non-compliance contributed to the loss.

**Mortgage and sale.** When you sell your home, the buyer's lender will order an appraisal. Appraisers note unpermitted additions and improvements. Lenders may refuse to lend on a home with significant unpermitted work. Buyers may demand that unpermitted work be permitted retroactively (which can require opening walls for inspection) or may simply walk away. An unpermitted addition can be a very serious obstacle to selling a home.

**Retroactive permitting.** If you discover (or a buyer discovers) unpermitted work, it can often be retroactively permitted — called "permit to legalize" or "as-built permit." But this typically requires opening walls to expose the work for inspection, which is disruptive and expensive. Far easier to permit correctly the first time.

### The Permit Process

The process varies by jurisdiction, but typically:

1. **Application:** You (or your contractor) submit an application to the local building department, including a description of the work, drawings or plans (required for structural work and some other categories), and pay the permit fee.
2. **Plan review:** For projects requiring drawings, the building department reviews the plans for code compliance. This can take days to weeks depending on the jurisdiction's workload.
3. **Permit issuance:** The permit is issued. Work can begin.
4. **Inspections:** At specified milestones during the work, an inspector visits to verify code compliance. Typical inspection points: footing inspection (before concrete is poured), framing inspection (before walls are closed), rough electrical/plumbing/HVAC (before walls are closed), insulation, and final inspection.
5. **Certificate of occupancy (or final approval):** After passing the final inspection, the project is officially approved.

**Who pulls the permit — homeowner or contractor?** Either can pull the permit in most jurisdictions. When the contractor pulls the permit, they are responsible for code compliance and inspections. When you pull it yourself, you take on that responsibility. For DIY work, pulling your own permit is straightforward. For contracted work, the contractor should pull their own permit — a contractor who asks you to pull the permit for their work is offloading risk and responsibility.

## Part 6: Planning a Major Renovation

A major renovation — kitchen, bathroom, addition, basement finish — is a project that takes weeks to months, costs tens of thousands of dollars, and significantly disrupts your daily life. Planning well makes the difference between an experience you will look back on fondly and one that strains your budget, your relationships, and your sanity.

### Design First

Before you talk to contractors, know what you want. A vague vision ("I want to update the kitchen") produces vague quotes that cannot be compared. A specific design ("I want to remove the peninsula, extend the counter along the north wall, replace the cabinets with semi-custom white shaker style in this configuration, install quartz countertops, and keep the existing sink location") produces specific quotes that can be compared accurately.

Work with a designer — a kitchen designer, a bathroom designer, or a full interior designer — before going to contractors. Many kitchen and bath showrooms offer free design services as part of the process. The cost of design is almost always recovered in better decisions and more accurate quotes.

For structural work (adding a room, removing a wall, building a deck), an architect or structural engineer produces construction drawings that are required for the permit and give contractors a precise scope to bid against.

### The Budget — Build in a Contingency

Construction projects encounter unexpected conditions. Walls opened up reveal rot, inadequate framing, outdated wiring, or plumbing that was not where it was supposed to be. Surprises cost money.

Build a contingency into every renovation budget:
- For projects in older homes (pre-1980): 20% to 25% contingency
- For projects in homes with unknown history: 20% contingency
- For projects in newer, well-maintained homes: 10% to 15% contingency

If the contingency is not used, it goes back to your savings. If it is used, you have the funds to handle surprises without derailing the project.

### Living Through a Renovation

Major kitchen renovations typically make the kitchen unusable for 4 to 12 weeks. Have a plan for cooking during this period — a camping stove, a toaster oven, a countertop air fryer, and a refrigerator in the garage or living room can get you through. Eating out every night adds up to thousands of dollars over a long renovation.

Bathroom renovations are similarly disruptive. If you have only one bathroom, ensure the timeline allows for some functional bathroom access during the work, or plan alternative arrangements.

Communicate clearly with your household about the timeline, the disruption, and the plan. Renovation-related stress is a common source of household conflict. Regular conversations about how the project is going — not just with the contractor but with the people who live in the house — reduce tension.

## Part 7: The DIY Decision — Honest Assessment

Many renovation tasks are within reach of a motivated homeowner. Many are not. Knowing the difference saves money when you do things yourself and saves money (and injury, and time) when you hire them out.

### Good DIY Candidates (for capable, patient homeowners)

- Painting walls, ceilings, trim, and exterior surfaces
- Tiling floors and walls (with proper preparation — substrate, layout, grouting)
- Installing laminate or engineered wood floating floors
- Replacing cabinet hardware
- Installing simple light fixtures (with circuit off and verified)
- Replacing a toilet (shut off the water, remove old toilet, set new toilet with new wax ring)
- Installing shelving and storage systems
- Building simple raised garden beds or small wood structures
- Installing a tile backsplash
- Refinishing or painting kitchen cabinets
- Replacing a faucet or sink
- Installing a ceiling fan (with proper box)
- Caulking and weather-stripping
- Landscaping — planting, mulching, building simple garden walls with block or stone

### Not Good DIY Candidates (unless you have specific training or experience)

- Any structural work — removing walls, adding beams, altering load paths
- Electrical panel work
- Gas line work of any kind
- Plumbing that requires opening walls
- Roofing (working on a sloped roof safely requires training and equipment)
- HVAC installation or major service
- Work requiring specific permits that mandate licensed contractor
- Any project where a mistake could cause structural failure, fire, flood, or injury

The YouTube effect — watching a 15-minute video and concluding that any task is straightforward — has led many homeowners into projects they were not equipped for. The video shows the highlight reel. It does not show the mistakes made and corrected, the specialized tools required, or the things that can go wrong when the conditions are not exactly as shown.

A useful test: before deciding to DIY something, ask yourself what happens if you do it wrong. If the answer is "I repaint it" or "I reinstall it," the risk is low. If the answer is "the ceiling collapses," "there is a fire," "the basement floods," or "someone gets hurt," the risk is high. Calibrate accordingly.

## Part 8: Specific Project Guidance

### Kitchen Remodel

The kitchen is the most complex renovation in a typical home. It involves all the trades — carpentry, plumbing, electrical, gas (sometimes), tile work, and finish work — in a relatively small space, often with existing conditions that do not cooperate with the plan.

**The major decisions:**
- **Layout:** Moving the sink requires moving plumbing (expensive). Moving the range requires moving gas or electrical (expensive and usually permitted). If you can achieve your design goals without moving the sink or range, the project costs significantly less.
- **Cabinets:** Stock (available off-the-shelf at home centers, lowest cost), semi-custom (ordered from a catalog of standard sizes, mid-range cost), or custom (built to your exact dimensions, highest cost). For most homeowners, semi-custom cabinets offer the best balance of quality, flexibility, and cost.
- **Countertops:** Laminate (most affordable, durable, no longer as stigmatized as it once was), butcher block (warm, beautiful, requires oiling maintenance), quartz composite (engineered stone, very durable, non-porous, excellent for kitchens), granite (natural stone, requires sealing annually), quartzite (harder than granite, not to be confused with quartz composite).
- **Appliances:** Decide before finalizing the cabinet design — appliance dimensions drive cabinet configuration. Energy-efficient appliances can qualify for utility rebates and the federal Energy Star credit.

**Timeline:** A modest kitchen remodel — replacing cabinets, countertops, and appliances without moving plumbing or gas — takes 3 to 6 weeks. A full gut renovation with layout changes takes 8 to 16 weeks.

### Bathroom Remodel

**The major decisions:**
- **Shower/tub:** A custom tile shower is the most expensive option and the most visually impressive. Prefabricated acrylic or fiberglass units are far less expensive and surprisingly durable. A walk-in shower is currently preferred by most buyers over a combination tub/shower in the primary bathroom. A soaking tub in a secondary bathroom is often not valued by buyers — consider whether you will actually use it.
- **Tile:** The largest visual impact in a bathroom. Porcelain tile is the most practical — durable, water-resistant, available in an enormous range of styles including realistic wood and stone looks. Natural stone (marble, travertine) is beautiful but requires sealing and is more porous and vulnerable to staining and etching.
- **Ventilation:** Every bathroom must have mechanical ventilation that exhausts to the exterior. If you are renovating a bathroom, upgrade to a quiet, efficient exhaust fan (Panasonic and Broan make excellent products). An improperly ventilated bathroom produces chronic moisture problems and mold.

**Timeline:** A bathroom renovation typically takes 2 to 4 weeks for a full gut renovation of a single bathroom.

### Basement Finishing

Converting an unfinished basement to livable space requires careful attention to moisture management before any framing or finishing begins. A basement that has any history of water intrusion must have that problem permanently solved before it is finished — finished walls conceal moisture problems and turn a manageable maintenance issue into a catastrophic mold problem.

**Before framing:**
- Address any water intrusion at the foundation wall (see Post 7)
- Verify the sump pump is functional and adequate
- Install a vapor barrier on the concrete floor before the subfloor
- Consider a dimple mat (drainage mat) against the foundation walls before framing to allow any wall moisture to drain to the floor drain

**Egress windows:** For a basement bedroom to be legal, it must have an egress window — an opening large enough for a person to escape in a fire. Standard egress window requirements: minimum 5.7 square feet of opening, minimum 24 inches in height, minimum 20 inches in width, maximum 44 inches from the floor to the bottom of the opening. This typically requires cutting through the foundation wall — a specialized job that requires a concrete saw and proper shoring.
