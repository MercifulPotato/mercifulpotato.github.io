## Part 1: The Two Systems That Scare People Most

Ask a group of first-time homeowners which systems in their home they are least comfortable with and the answer is almost always the same: plumbing and electrical. Both feel technical, both feel dangerous, and both have failed in spectacular and expensive ways for enough homeowners that the fear is not irrational.

But fear built on ignorance is different from informed caution. Understanding how these systems work — not at the engineering level, but at the homeowner level — fundamentally changes your ability to maintain them, respond to emergencies, and make intelligent decisions about when to call a professional.

This post covers both systems comprehensively: how they work, what breaks, what you can safely manage yourself, what requires a licensed professional, and what safety situations require immediate action.

## Part 2: The Plumbing System — How It Works

Your home's plumbing consists of two independent subsystems that never touch:

**The supply system** brings fresh water in under pressure. Water enters the house from the main supply (municipal water or a private well) at the main shutoff valve, passes through the water meter (if on city water), through the main shutoff, and then distributes through a network of pipes to every fixture and appliance in the house. Hot water is supplied by a parallel network of pipes connected to the water heater. The supply system operates at pressure — typically 45 to 80 PSI.

**The drain-waste-vent (DWV) system** removes used water and waste from the house by gravity (no pressure). Drain pipes slope continuously downward toward the main sewer line (or septic tank). Every drain in the house — every sink, toilet, shower, tub, dishwasher, washing machine, and floor drain — connects to the drain system. Every drain fixture has a P-trap (the curved pipe visible under sinks) that holds a small amount of water to seal off sewer gas. The vent system — vertical pipes that extend through the roof — allows air into the drain pipes so water can flow freely (without a vent, drains would gurgle and drain slowly) and allows sewer gases to escape the building safely.

### Water Pressure

Residential water pressure should be between 40 and 80 PSI. Pressure above 80 PSI damages appliances, increases the likelihood of pipe failures, and causes "water hammer" — the banging noise when a fast-closing valve (like a dishwasher solenoid) suddenly stops water flow.

You can check your water pressure with an inexpensive pressure gauge ($10 to $15 at hardware stores) that threads onto a hose bib.

If pressure is above 80 PSI, a **pressure reducing valve (PRV)** installed on the main supply line reduces it to a safe level. PRVs are typically installed near the main shutoff and can be adjusted. If your PRV is failing (pressure fluctuates widely, or is higher or lower than the set point), have a plumber replace it — it is a straightforward job.

### The Shutoffs — Know All of Them

When something goes wrong, being able to quickly shut off the water is the difference between a minor incident and a disaster. Know these shutoffs:

**Main shutoff:** Typically a gate valve or ball valve on the main supply pipe, usually in the utility room, basement, mechanical closet, or crawl space. In some homes, it is outside near the meter. A ball valve (lever handle) is preferred — one quarter-turn shuts it off. A gate valve (round wheel handle) requires multiple turns and can fail in the partially-open position over time.

**Individual fixture shutoffs:** Under every sink (two supply lines — hot and cold), behind every toilet (one cold water supply), behind the washing machine (two), behind the refrigerator (one, if ice maker or dispenser), behind the dishwasher (one), and near the water heater (one cold inlet, sometimes a hot outlet valve as well).

Exercise these valves annually. Gate valves and stop valves that have not been moved in years can seize or fail to close completely when operated. Operate each one gently — open fully, then turn back 1/4 turn from fully open so it does not seize in position.

### Pipes — What You Have and What It Means

**Copper:** The standard residential supply pipe material from the 1950s through the 2000s. Very reliable, long lifespan (50+ years). Joins by soldering (sweat fitting) — a skill that requires practice and a propane torch, but is learnable. Repair fittings using push-to-connect connectors (SharkBite-type) allow repairs without soldering and are reliable for permanent repairs.

**CPVC (cream-colored plastic):** Common in residential supply systems from the 1970s through the 2000s. Solvent-welded joints. Reliable in general, but can become brittle over decades and is somewhat susceptible to failure at joints if the pipe was not properly cleaned before cementing. Incompatible with some petroleum-based products in contact.

**PEX (cross-linked polyethylene):** The current standard for residential supply plumbing. Flexible, freeze-resistant (it can flex rather than burst in a freeze), fast to install, very reliable. Connected with crimp rings (requiring a special tool) or with push-to-connect fittings. Color-coded: red for hot, blue for cold. PEX cannot be directly connected to water heaters (there must be a short section of copper or CPVC between the water heater and PEX due to heat).

**Galvanized steel:** Found in pre-1960s homes. Zinc-coated steel pipe that corrodes from the inside out over time. The rust and scale that accumulates inside galvanized pipe eventually clogs the pipe to a fraction of its original diameter. Homes with galvanized steel supply pipes often have poor water pressure, rusty water at first use, and frequent failures at the corroded sections. Galvanized steel supply piping is overdue for replacement in any home that still has it — budget for a whole-house repipe ($8,000 to $20,000+).

**Cast iron (drain pipes):** The standard drain pipe material in older homes. Very durable, long-lasting, but eventually corrodes and requires replacement. Inspection with a sewer camera can reveal the condition of underground cast iron drains.

**ABS and PVC (plastic drain pipes):** The modern standard for drain-waste-vent systems. ABS (black) was widely used from the 1960s through the 1980s. PVC (white) is the current standard. Both are reliable and long-lasting. Joined with solvent cement.

## Part 3: Plumbing Problems — What You Can Fix and What You Cannot

### DIY-Appropriate Plumbing Repairs

**Fixing a running toilet:** A toilet that runs continuously is wasting hundreds of gallons of water per day. The most common causes:
1. **Flapper failure:** The rubber flapper at the bottom of the tank is the seal that prevents water from the tank from continuously draining into the bowl. When it ages, it deforms, warps, or develops mineral buildup and no longer seals. Replacement flappers cost $5 to $10 and install in minutes — shut off the toilet's supply valve, flush to empty the tank, unhook the old flapper, hook in the new one. When buying a replacement, bring the old one to the hardware store to match the size and style.
2. **Float issues:** The float (the ball or cup that rides the water level) controls when the fill valve shuts off. If the float is set too high, water continuously runs into the overflow tube and down the drain. Adjust the float arm or float clip to lower the target water level to about 1 inch below the top of the overflow tube.
3. **Fill valve failure:** The fill valve (the mechanism that fills the tank after a flush) can fail and allow water to continuously run in. Replacement fill valves (Fluidmaster 400A is the standard) cost $10 to $15 and installing one is a straightforward task with the water shut off.

**Fixing a leaky faucet:** A dripping faucet is typically a worn seat washer (compression faucets), a worn ceramic disc, or a worn O-ring or cartridge. Most faucet repairs involve shutting off the supply valves, disassembling the faucet handle and cartridge, and replacing the worn component. Cartridge faucets (the most common modern type — ball faucets and single-handle faucets) usually require replacement of a $15 to $30 cartridge specific to the faucet brand.

**Clearing simple drain clogs:** Most bathroom sink clogs are hair accumulation at the pop-up drain stopper. Remove the stopper (usually by unscrewing the pivot rod at the back of the drain tailpiece, or by simply pulling or twisting it out), and clean out the accumulated hair. A $5 drain hair removal tool (a barbed plastic stick) is remarkably effective.

For kitchen sink clogs, the P-trap under the sink is usually the location. Place a bucket under the trap, unscrew the slip nuts on both ends of the trap, remove it, clean it out, and reassemble. Many kitchen clogs are in the trap or just past it.

**Do not use liquid drain cleaners** as a primary solution. Chemical drain cleaners (Drano, liquid Plumr) are harsh chemicals that damage pipes over time (particularly old pipes, PVC P-traps, and rubber gaskets). They also create a hazardous situation for plumbers who may need to work on the drain afterward. A drain snake (hand snake or electric) is more effective and does no damage.

**Replacing a faucet:** Replacing a kitchen or bathroom faucet is a DIY-appropriate job with the water shut off and basic hand tools. New faucets include instructions. The most challenging part is usually working in the cramped space under the sink. A basin wrench — a specialty tool with a long handle and pivoting jaw — makes removing and tightening the faucet mounting nuts much easier.

**Replacing toilet seats, toilet flappers, supply lines, and shutoff valves:** All DIY-appropriate with the water shut off.

### When to Call a Plumber

- Any work involving the main supply line
- Any work involving the sewer line (main drain or lateral to the street)
- Any work requiring opening walls to access pipes
- Water heater installation (requires a permit in most jurisdictions; the permit requires a licensed plumber or requires the homeowner to pull the permit themselves and arrange for inspection)
- Adding new supply or drain lines (permit required)
- Any gas line work (separate from plumbing but licensed plumbers often do this work — never DIY gas lines)
- A clog that does not clear with a hand snake (the clog is deeper in the system)
- Any suspected sewer line backup (sewage coming up through floor drains or multiple fixtures backing up simultaneously — this is the main drain and requires a professional)

### Sewer Line Health

The sewer line — the underground pipe that connects your home's drain system to the municipal sewer or your septic tank — is one of the most expensive systems to repair when it fails, and one of the most invisible.

Signs of a sewer line problem:
- Multiple drains backing up simultaneously
- Sewage odor in the house or yard
- Wet spots or unusually lush green areas in the yard along the sewer line path (effluent from a leak fertilizes the grass)
- Tree roots in the drain (a sewer camera will reveal this)

For older homes (pre-1980s), have the sewer line inspected with a camera before or shortly after purchase. Clay tile and cast iron sewer lines in older homes can be infiltrated by tree roots, offset at joints from soil settlement, or simply broken. A sewer camera inspection costs $150 to $350 and reveals what you have.

## Part 4: The Electrical System — How It Works

Your home's electrical system begins at the utility service entrance — either overhead (service drop from a utility pole) or underground — where the utility's wires connect to your home. The service passes through the electric meter (which the utility reads to bill you) and into the main electrical panel (also called the breaker box, load center, or service panel).

### The Main Panel

The main panel contains:
- **The main breaker:** A large double-pole breaker that controls power to the entire panel. Turning it off kills power to all circuits in the house. (It does not shut off power to the meter or the service entrance — the utility's lines to the meter are always live.)
- **Branch circuit breakers:** Individual breakers for each circuit in the house. A single-pole breaker handles 120V circuits (lights, standard outlets, small appliances). A double-pole breaker handles 240V circuits (dryers, electric ranges, HVAC systems, EV chargers, well pumps, electric water heaters).
- **Bus bars:** Metal rails to which the breakers connect. The hot bus bars are energized by the main breaker. The neutral bar connects neutral wires to ground.

### Circuit Breakers — How They Work

A circuit breaker protects a wire from overheating by interrupting the circuit when the current exceeds the breaker's rated capacity. A 15-amp breaker will trip when the current on that circuit exceeds 15 amps (sustained overload) or more immediately at very high current (a short circuit or ground fault).

A tripped breaker moves to the middle position (or all the way to off, depending on the breaker type). To reset:
1. Turn the breaker fully to the OFF position first
2. Then turn it to ON
3. If it immediately trips again, there is an overload or fault on that circuit — do not repeatedly reset it

**A breaker that frequently trips** is telling you something: either the circuit is overloaded (too many devices pulling too much current) or there is a fault in the wiring or a connected device. Find and address the cause.

### Understanding Your Circuits

Every outlet and switch in your house is on a circuit connected to a specific breaker. Most homes have 15-amp and 20-amp circuits:
- 15-amp circuits use 14-gauge wire and feed most general lighting and outlet circuits
- 20-amp circuits use 12-gauge wire and are required for kitchen counter outlets (GFCI-protected), bathroom outlets (GFCI-protected), garage outlets, and the laundry area

**Labeling your panel** — knowing which breaker controls which outlets and lights — is one of the most useful things you can do in your home. If the panel is not labeled, spend an hour systematically identifying each circuit: have a helper call out when outlets and lights lose power as you flip breakers.

### GFCI and AFCI Protection

**GFCI (Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter):** Required by code in all wet locations — bathrooms, kitchens (within 6 feet of a sink), garages, outdoors, and crawl spaces. A GFCI monitors the balance of current between the hot and neutral conductors; if it detects even 5 milliamps of imbalance (indicating current flowing to ground through a person's body), it trips in 1/40 of a second — fast enough to prevent electrocution. Test monthly.

A GFCI outlet protects itself and any outlets downstream of it on the same circuit. A GFCI breaker protects the entire circuit. Determine which type you have and verify that all required locations are protected.

**AFCI (Arc Fault Circuit Interrupter):** Detects the electrical signature of arcing faults — the kind that occur in damaged wiring inside walls and that can start fires. Required by modern codes in most living spaces. An AFCI breaker looks like a standard breaker but has a "Test" button. Homes with older panels may not have AFCI protection — discuss adding it with a licensed electrician.

## Part 5: Electrical — What You Can Safely Do and What You Cannot

### What Homeowners Can Safely Do

**Replace outlets and switches:** With the circuit breaker OFF and verified with a non-contact voltage tester, replacing a standard outlet or switch is safe for a careful homeowner. Take a photo of the existing wiring before removing the old device. Connect wires to the same terminals on the new device: black (hot) to the brass-colored screw, white (neutral) to the silver screw, bare or green wire (ground) to the green screw. Never work on wiring without verifying with a voltage tester that the circuit is de-energized — a breaker labeled incorrectly is more common than you would like.

**Replace light fixtures and ceiling fans:** With the circuit de-energized and verified. Ceiling fans require a ceiling box rated for fan support — a standard light fixture box is not adequate for the weight and movement of a fan. If you are replacing a light with a ceiling fan, verify the box is fan-rated (it will say so on the box) or replace it with one that is.

**Replace GFCI outlets:** With the circuit off. Follow the LOAD/LINE terminal directions — LINE connects to the incoming power; LOAD connects to downstream outlets that should be protected by this GFCI.

**Install dimmer switches:** Most modern dimmers are designed for DIY installation. Verify that the bulbs on the circuit are compatible (not all LED bulbs are dimmer-compatible — check the bulb packaging for "dimmable").

**Replace a thermostat:** Low-voltage thermostat wiring is safe to work on with the furnace/AC power off. Take a photo of the existing wiring before removing the old thermostat. Modern smart thermostats include clear installation guides.

### What Requires a Licensed Electrician

**Panel work of any kind:** Adding or replacing breakers, upgrading panel capacity, installing a subpanel, adding a main disconnect. The main panel bus bars are energized even with the main breaker off — only the utility can de-energize the service entrance. Work inside the panel on the bus bars or main lugs requires a licensed electrician and in most jurisdictions requires a permit.

**Adding new circuits:** Running new wiring from the panel to new outlets, light fixtures, or appliances requires a permit and must be done by a licensed electrician in most jurisdictions.

**240V circuits:** Installing or modifying circuits for electric dryers, ranges, EV chargers, hot tubs, or HVAC equipment.

**Wiring inside walls:** If access requires opening walls.

**Any work where you are not certain the wiring is de-energized:** When in doubt, call a professional. The cost of an electrician's service call is infinitely less than the cost of electrocution.

## Part 6: Whole-House Electrical Safety Assessment

### Know Your Panel's Brand and Age

As discussed in Post 1, certain panel brands have documented safety issues:

**Federal Pacific Electric (FPE) Stab-Lok:** Distinguished by the red lettering on the breakers and the FPE name on the panel cover. These panels have been the subject of extensive study and legal action related to breakers that fail to trip during overload conditions. If you have one, consult a licensed electrician about replacement. Many insurance companies charge higher premiums or decline to insure homes with FPE panels.

**Zinsco and GTE-Sylvania:** Similar concerns — breakers that can fail to trip, overheat, and in some cases fuse to the bus bar. Another panel to discuss replacement with an electrician.

**Pushmatic:** A mid-century panel design with a different breaker mechanism — the breakers push in to reset. Generally functional but very old and typically in need of replacement due to age alone. Parts are not readily available.

**General Electric, Siemens, Square D, Eaton/Cutler-Hammer, Leviton:** Reputable brands with generally reliable panels. GE and Square D (by Schneider Electric) are the most common in American homes.

### Aluminum Branch Wiring

As described in Post 1, some homes from roughly 1965 to 1973 used aluminum for branch circuit wiring. Aluminum wiring is not inherently dangerous, but it requires specific aluminum-rated devices (outlets, switches, and fixtures rated "CO/ALR" or "AL-CU") and anti-oxidant compound at connections. The standard solution for aluminum-wired homes is pigtailing — attaching a short piece of copper wire to the aluminum at every connection point using a COPALUM crimp connector or an AlumiConn connector. This work should be done by a licensed electrician.

### Ground Fault and Arc Fault Coverage

Walk your home and verify:
- Every bathroom outlet is GFCI-protected (either a GFCI outlet or a GFCI breaker)
- Every kitchen counter outlet within 6 feet of a sink is GFCI-protected
- The garage has GFCI-protected outlets
- All outdoor outlets are GFCI-protected
- The laundry area has GFCI protection (required in modern code)
- The basement has GFCI protection (required in modern code)
- All bedroom circuits have AFCI protection (test the breakers)

Any missing protection is a safety deficiency. Address it with a licensed electrician.

### Extension Cords and Surge Protectors

Extension cords are for temporary use. A permanently installed extension cord — running under a rug, through a doorway, or behind furniture — is a fire hazard. If you need a permanent outlet in a location, have a licensed electrician add one.

Surge protectors protect electronics from voltage spikes. Use them for computers, televisions, and home entertainment equipment. Not all power strips are surge protectors — check for a joules rating (higher is better; 1,000 joules minimum) and look for UL listing.

Whole-house surge protectors, installed at the main panel, protect all circuits in the home from external voltage surges (lightning, utility switching events). These are installed by electricians and cost $150 to $400 installed. They are an excellent investment for electronics-heavy homes in lightning-prone areas.

## Part 7: Fire Safety — Prevention, Detection, and Response

Home fires kill approximately 2,500 to 3,000 Americans per year and injure many more. Most home fires are preventable, and most fire deaths are preventable with working smoke detectors.

### Smoke Detector Placement and Maintenance

Requirements from NFPA 72 (the National Fire Alarm and Signaling Code):
- One smoke alarm on every level of the home
- One smoke alarm inside every sleeping room (bedroom)
- One smoke alarm outside every separate sleeping area

Most experts recommend combination units (ionization + photoelectric) or interconnected systems where all alarms sound when any one detects smoke.

**Interconnected alarms** — either hardwired or wireless interconnected — are strongly recommended. When the bedroom smoke alarm goes off at 3 AM, you want the hallway alarm and the living room alarm going off too, because the person sleeping in the back bedroom may not hear the alarm in the front of the house.

Hardwired interconnected alarms with battery backup are the best solution for new installations. Wireless interconnected alarms (like the Kidde or First Alert interconnected battery-operated systems) are the best retrofit solution for older homes without wiring.

**Replace smoke detectors at 10 years.** Every smoke alarm has a lifespan. Write the installation date on the back in permanent marker.

### Carbon Monoxide Detectors

CO detectors are required on every level and near every sleeping area in a home with gas appliances, attached garage, or any combustion appliance. The same alarm interconnection principles apply.

CO detectors have a 5- to 7-year lifespan for the sensing element. The device may still power on and beep after the sensor has expired — this does not mean it will detect CO. Write the installation date on the back and replace on schedule.

**If a CO detector alarms:** Leave the house immediately. Do not stop to investigate. Call 911 from outside. Do not re-enter until emergency responders have cleared the building.

### Fire Extinguishers

Every home should have at minimum one multi-purpose (ABC) dry chemical fire extinguisher in the kitchen, easily accessible. A 5-pound extinguisher is the minimum; a 10-pound extinguisher provides longer discharge time.

Using a fire extinguisher — the PASS method:
- **P**ull the safety pin
- **A**im the nozzle at the base of the fire (not the flames)
- **S**queeze the handle to discharge
- **S**weep the nozzle from side to side at the base of the fire

Only attempt to fight a fire with an extinguisher if:
- The fire is small and contained
- You have a clear escape path behind you
- You have already called 911

If the fire grows or does not quickly extinguish, evacuate immediately. Do not sacrifice your escape route attempting to fight a larger fire.

**Fire extinguisher service:** Have extinguishers professionally inspected every 6 years. Replace or hydrostatically test every 12 years per NFPA 10. Extinguishers that have been used, even partially, should be recharged immediately.

### Fire Escape Planning

Every household should have a fire escape plan:
- Identify two ways out of every room (door + window if necessary)
- Identify a meeting place outside the house
- Practice the plan — especially with children
- Sleep with bedroom doors closed (a closed door dramatically slows fire spread and keeps smoke and heat out of the room for significantly longer than an open door)

## Part 8: Water Safety — Lead, Contaminants, and Testing

### Lead in Water

Lead can enter drinking water from lead service lines (the pipes that connect the main to your house — these were standard in many cities until the 1980s), lead solder in older copper plumbing (pre-1986), and brass fixtures containing lead alloy.

There is no safe level of lead exposure, particularly for children. If your home was built before 1986, or if you are in a city with known lead service line issues (check your local utility's Consumer Confidence Report), test your water for lead. Testing kits are available from certified labs; many municipalities provide free testing.

If lead is detected:
- Use a certified lead-reducing water filter (NSF/ANSI 53 certified for lead reduction) on drinking and cooking water
- Flush the tap for 30 seconds before using water for drinking (this clears the standing water that has been in contact with lead-containing pipes)
- Replace lead-containing plumbing as budget allows; advocate to your municipality for lead service line replacement (many utilities are now required to replace these)

### Well Water Testing

If your home uses a private well (no city water connection), the water quality is your responsibility. The EPA and most states recommend testing well water annually for:
- Coliform bacteria (indicator of sewage contamination)
- Nitrates (particularly important if you have agricultural land nearby or an infant in the household)
- pH
- Total dissolved solids

Additionally test for local contaminants of concern based on your area — radon in water, arsenic, manganese, PFAS, or others. Your county health department can advise on local concerns and often provides free or low-cost testing.

## Part 9: Emergency Response — What to Do When Things Go Wrong

### Water Emergency (Burst Pipe or Major Leak)

1. **Shut off the water** at the nearest fixture shutoff, or at the main shutoff if the fixture shutoff does not stop the flow or cannot be located quickly
2. Call a plumber
3. If water has entered walls, floors, or ceilings, photograph the damage thoroughly for insurance documentation before beginning cleanup
4. Remove standing water as quickly as possible — water in contact with structural materials for more than 24 to 48 hours can cause mold
5. Run fans and a dehumidifier to dry out affected areas
6. Contact your homeowner's insurance company — sudden and accidental water damage is typically covered; gradual damage (a slow leak you knew about and did not address) is typically not covered

### Electrical Emergency (Burning Smell, Sparks, or Shock)

**If there is a burning smell from an outlet, switch, or appliance:**
- Turn off the circuit at the panel immediately
- Do not plug anything else into that outlet or use that circuit until it has been inspected by a licensed electrician
- If the smell is coming from inside a wall, evacuate and call 911 — wall fires can spread invisibly for a significant time before becoming visible

**If someone receives an electrical shock:**
- If the person is still in contact with the electrical source, do NOT touch them — you will complete the circuit
- Shut off the power at the main breaker before touching the person
- Call 911 immediately
- Even mild shocks can cause internal injuries that are not immediately apparent — medical evaluation is warranted after any electrical shock

**If a circuit breaker trips and repeatedly re-trips after reset:**
- Leave it in the OFF position
- Do not continue to reset it
- Have a licensed electrician investigate before using that circuit again

### Gas Emergency

**If you smell natural gas or propane inside the house:**
- Do not operate any electrical switches (no light switches, no fans, no appliances — do not even plug in or unplug anything)
- Do not use a phone inside the house
- Leave the house immediately, leaving the door open
- From a safe distance away from the house, call the gas company's emergency line (usually printed on your gas bill) and 911
- Do not re-enter until the gas company has inspected and cleared the house
