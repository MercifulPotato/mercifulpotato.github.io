## Part 1: Why Criminal Provisions Matter for a Financial Institution

Every financial institution faces the risk of fraud — people lying to obtain money they are not entitled to, people falsifying documents to secure loans or investments, people providing false collateral to back financial transactions. Without criminal sanctions, the financial incentive to commit fraud is pure: if you can successfully deceive the institution, you get money; if you get caught, the worst that happens is you have to return the money. The asymmetric payoff structure favors fraudsters.

Criminal sanctions change this calculus. The prospect of imprisonment — not just financial liability — creates a deterrent that civil liability alone cannot. And in the development finance context, where the fund is deploying public money and carrying government guarantees, fraud is not just a harm to the institution; it is a harm to the public.

Sections 43 through 45 of the Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 create the criminal law framework for offenses under the Act. These provisions are shorter than the governance and investment provisions, but they are among the most practically consequential.

## Part 2: What Constitutes an Offense

Section 43, Sub-section 1 defines what constitutes an offense under the Act. A person commits an offense under this Act if they:

- Submit false or incorrect information, evidence, reports, or documents to the fund
- Pledge false or incorrect collateral
- Engage in either of the above actions for the purpose of obtaining investment, loans, guarantees, or other benefits under the Act

The core offense is straightforward: lying to the fund to get money. This covers a range of actual behaviors:

- Falsifying financial statements to show a project's revenues as higher than they actually are or will be
- Fabricating or inflating estimates of project costs or benefits
- Providing forged contracts (fake power purchase agreements, fake land title documents, fake construction contracts) as supporting documentation
- Pledging collateral (land, equipment, financial assets) that does not exist, does not belong to the pledger, or has been overvalued
- Making false representations about the project's regulatory status, environmental clearances, or government approvals

These are all real-world fraud patterns that development finance institutions encounter. The explicit criminalization of these behaviors under the specific Act (in addition to their coverage under general criminal law) creates a direct legal hook that does not require prosecutors to navigate complex general fraud provisions.

### The Act Covers Both Perpetrators and Facilitators

Sub-section 2 specifies that the penalty applies both to those who commit the offense and to those who cause it to be committed ("garne ya garaune"). This "causing" language is important because fraud at the scale relevant to a development finance institution rarely involves a single actor. A fraudulent loan application might involve:

- The project developer who submits the false application
- A lawyer or financial advisor who prepared the false documents
- An appraiser who provided an inflated valuation of the collateral
- An accountant who certified fraudulent financial statements
- A government official who provided false regulatory certifications

All of these people, under the Act's language, can face criminal liability as co-perpetrators or facilitators.

## Part 3: The Penalties

Section 43, Sub-section 2 specifies the penalties for those convicted under this provision:

**Financial penalty:** Recovery of the full amount of the loss caused to the fund, PLUS a fine equal to double that amount.

So if a fraudster obtains Rs. 100 million through false documentation, they must repay the Rs. 100 million PLUS pay an additional Rs. 200 million fine — a total financial liability of Rs. 300 million.

**Imprisonment:** Up to two years.

Both penalties apply simultaneously — it is not a choice between fine and imprisonment. A convicted person faces both the financial penalty and the prison sentence.

### How the Penalty is Calculated

The financial penalty is anchored to "the amount of the harm caused to the fund" (koshma bhaeko khashani noksanee barabarko rakam) plus double that amount. The harm to the fund is the amount of money the fund actually lost — not necessarily the total amount fraudulently obtained, but the actual financial loss after any partial recovery.

This structure creates a significant multiplier: a fraud that causes Rs. 10 million in actual loss creates a potential criminal financial liability of Rs. 30 million (the loss plus double the loss). For smaller frauds, the prospect of triple liability may be a deterrent. For very large frauds — hundreds of millions or billions of rupees — financial liability alone may be insufficient deterrent for those who can reasonably hope to evade or hide assets, which is why the imprisonment provision matters.

### Is Two Years Enough?

The maximum imprisonment sentence of two years has attracted some commentary in discussions of Nepali financial law. For a major financial fraud against a public institution — potentially involving hundreds of millions of rupees of public money — two years might seem lenient. In many countries, major financial fraud involving public institutions or government-backed funds carries sentences of five, ten, or even more years.

The Act's designers may have considered two years appropriate given Nepal's current criminal justice context, or may have calibrated it to the general sentencing patterns for similar offenses in Nepal's penal law. They may also have intended this provision to work alongside general criminal law — Section 55 explicitly states that if an offense under this Act is also an offense under another law, prosecution under that other law is not barred. This means prosecutors could pursue additional charges under the Criminal Code (Muluki Aparadh Sanhita) or the Anti-Money Laundering Act, which might carry heavier sentences for the same conduct.

## Part 4: The Investigation and Prosecution Process

Section 44 specifies the process for investigating and prosecuting offenses under the Act.

**Investigation authority:** The government designates an investigating officer through a notice published in the Nepal Gazette. The investigating officer is responsible for conducting investigations into alleged offenses under the Act.

This provision has an important practical implication: investigations are conducted by government-appointed officers, not by the fund itself. This separation prevents the fund from being both the victim and the investigator — a conflict that could arise if fund management were responsible for investigating fraud against itself. An independent investigator, appointed by the government and publicly notified, provides more credible and impartial investigation.

**Expert assistance:** The investigating officer can obtain expert assistance for the investigation — bringing in financial experts, forensic accountants, valuation specialists, or other professionals needed to analyze complex financial fraud. The cost of this assistance is borne by the fund (as the injured party). This provision is realistic — complex financial fraud requires specialized expertise that a general-purpose investigating officer may not possess. Allowing the fund to pay for expert assistance increases the quality and credibility of investigations.

**60-day deadline:** The investigating officer must complete the investigation and submit their investigation report to the relevant Government Attorney's office within 60 days of beginning the investigation.

The 60-day deadline is ambitious. Financial fraud investigations involving complex documentation, multiple parties, and potentially large amounts of money often take months or years in more capable jurisdictions. In Nepal's current investigative capacity context, 60 days is very short.

The provision may be aspirational — setting a target without hard enforcement — or it may create perverse incentives if strictly enforced: investigations rushed to meet the deadline may be incomplete, missing crucial evidence. Incomplete investigations produce weak prosecution cases that are more likely to fail in court.

**Prosecution:** Based on the investigation report, the Government Attorney (Sarkari Wakil) prepares a charge sheet and files the case in the relevant District Court.

## Part 5: The Nepal Government as Plaintiff

Section 45 states simply: in all criminal cases under this Act, Nepal's government is the plaintiff (wadee).

This provision has several important implications:

**The fund is the victim, not the prosecutor.** The government, representing the public interest, brings the prosecution. The fund does not have to manage its own prosecutions — it reports to the investigating authority and cooperates with investigators, but the legal burden of prosecution rests with government lawyers.

**The case is treated as a public matter.** When the government is the plaintiff, the prosecution is not just about recovering the fund's money — it is about vindicating a public interest, deterring future fraud, and demonstrating that public financial institutions are protected by the full force of the state's criminal justice system.

**Political considerations can affect prosecution.** When the government is the plaintiff, political actors can influence whether prosecutions are pursued vigorously, settled quietly, or dropped for "insufficient evidence." This is one of the risks of government-as-plaintiff structures in countries where the rule of law and judicial independence are not fully established. A government that has political reasons to protect a particular defendant — perhaps a politically connected company that has committed fraud against the fund — may not pursue prosecution aggressively.

This is not a theoretical concern in the Nepali context. There have been cases where financial crimes against public institutions have not been prosecuted aggressively because the alleged perpetrators had political connections. The legal framework creates the right structure; whether it is used depends on political will and institutional independence.

## Part 6: The Interaction with Other Laws

Section 55 is a meta-provision that clarifies the Act's relationship with other criminal law: if conduct that is an offense under this Act is also an offense under any other law, prosecution under that other law is not prevented by this Act. In other words, this Act does not create a closed criminal law universe for the fund — it adds to the criminal law toolkit without replacing or excluding other applicable laws.

This provision matters because fraud against a public financial institution may simultaneously violate:
- This Act (false documentation to obtain investment)
- The Criminal Code (general fraud, forgery, misrepresentation)
- The Anti-Money Laundering Act (if fraudulent proceeds are laundered)
- The Corruption Prevention Act (if government officials are involved)
- The Asset Recovery Act
- Company law provisions (if directors committed fraud against their own company)

Prosecutors can and should use all applicable law to build the strongest possible case. The convergence of multiple applicable statutes can increase both the likelihood of conviction and the potential severity of punishment.

## Part 7: An Honest Assessment of the Enforcement Landscape

The criminal provisions in the Act are reasonably well-designed. The offense definition is clear, the penalty is meaningful (though perhaps not maximally deterrent), and the investigation process has sensible features like expert assistance and government-as-plaintiff.

But the effectiveness of criminal law depends not just on the law itself but on the entire criminal justice system: the police and investigators who build cases, the prosecutors who bring them, the courts that adjudicate them, and the prisons that enforce sentences.

Nepal's criminal justice system faces significant challenges:

**Investigation capacity:** Financial fraud at the scale relevant to a development finance institution requires specialized financial crime investigation units. Nepal's investigative capacity for complex financial crimes is limited compared to countries like Singapore, South Korea, or the United Kingdom that have dedicated financial crime squads.

**Prosecution capacity:** The Government Attorney's office handles enormous caseloads. Complex financial fraud cases require significant prosecutorial time, expertise, and resources. The 60-day investigation deadline may create pressure for shortcuts.

**Judicial capacity and independence:** Financial fraud cases in Nepal, as in many developing countries, can take years to resolve through the court system. Appeals can further extend timelines. Judicial capacity for complex financial cases — including the ability to understand and rule on sophisticated financial structures — is variable.

**Political influence:** As noted above, political considerations can affect whether prosecutions are pursued at all, how vigorously they are pursued, and how the justice system ultimately resolves them.

None of these challenges makes the criminal provisions useless. Even imperfect enforcement creates some deterrent. A legal framework that can support prosecution when circumstances align is better than no legal framework at all. And as Nepal's institutions develop — as financial crime investigation capacity grows, as the judiciary develops expertise in financial matters, as political independence of the prosecution function improves — the criminal provisions will become more consistently effective.

But anyone evaluating the Act's likely effectiveness against fraud should have realistic expectations: in Nepal's current institutional context, criminal enforcement of these provisions will be inconsistent, slower than the 60-day provision implies, and somewhat vulnerable to political influence. The non-criminal accountability mechanisms — governance, disclosure, audit, civil liability of directors — may in practice be as important or more important than the criminal provisions in deterring misconduct.
