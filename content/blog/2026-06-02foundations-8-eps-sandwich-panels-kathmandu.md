# Part 8 of 10 — Building on Shaking Ground: EPS/PUF Sandwich Panels and Foundations in Kathmandu, Nepal

On April 25, 2015, at 11:56 AM local time, a Mw 7.8 earthquake struck Nepal approximately 77 kilometers northwest of Kathmandu. The shaking lasted nearly two minutes. Nearly 9,000 people died. Almost 22,000 were injured. Nearly 500,000 homes were destroyed. Another 250,000 were damaged.

The most lethal structures were not the oldest or most primitive. They were unreinforced brick masonry buildings — the dominant construction type in Kathmandu Valley — that had been built without seismic consideration over decades of rapid urban growth. The buildings that performed best were those built with modern seismic detailing: reinforced concrete frames, confined masonry, and — in a small but notable group — lightweight prefabricated panel structures that in many cases survived with minimal damage.

This article addresses a specific question: if you are building a house in Kathmandu using EPS (Expanded Polystyrene) or PUF (Polyurethane Foam) sandwich panels, what foundation do you need? And to answer that properly, we need to understand what kind of ground Kathmandu sits on, what the seismic hazard is, and how lightweight panel construction interacts with foundation design in this extraordinarily demanding environment.

---

## Part 1: Kathmandu Valley — Geological Setting

### What Kathmandu Sits On

Kathmandu Valley is a bowl-shaped depression in the Himalayan foothills at approximately 1,400 meters (4,600 feet) elevation. The Bagmati River and its tributaries drain the valley. Geologically, the valley is an ancient lake basin — Lake Baidam — that filled with lake sediments over hundreds of thousands of years before gradually draining through the Bagmati gorge. The valley is now underlain by this thick sequence of lacustrine (lake) deposits, which are deeply problematic from a seismic engineering perspective.

**Stratigraphic sequence in Kathmandu Valley**:

**Upper Lacustrine Deposits (0 to 50 meters depth)**: Fine-grained sediments — silty clay, clayey silt, fine sand — deposited in the shallow margins and deeper center of the ancient lake. These materials are the primary concern for foundation engineering:
- High plasticity (PI typically 20 to 40)
- Moderate to high compressibility
- Very low shear wave velocity (Vs30 typically 150 to 250 m/s — site classification E in some areas, borderline D/E in others per ASCE 7)
- High liquefaction susceptibility in the sandy layers

**Kalimati Clay**: A specific thick unit of highly plastic, soft to medium-stiff lacustrine clay found across much of the central valley. This clay is so well-known in Kathmandu geotechnical engineering that it has its own name. Standard penetration test N values in Kalimati Clay are typically 2 to 8 — very soft by any measure. Undrained shear strength: 20 to 50 kPa (about 400 to 1,000 psf). This is challenging for shallow foundation bearing.

**Sandy Gravels and Boulders (deeper)**: Below the lacustrine sequence, the pre-lake alluvial gravels and glacifluvial deposits provide good bearing capacity. Depth to this competent material varies enormously across the valley: it may be at 10 meters in some areas, 80 to 100+ meters in others.

**Bedrock**: Phyllite, schist, quartzite, and granite of the Lesser Himalayan Crystalline Complex and Mahabharat Range. Bedrock is only accessible by deep borehole or at the valley margins where it outcrops.

### Shear Wave Velocity and Site Amplification

The shear wave velocity profile of Kathmandu Valley sediments is the critical parameter for understanding seismic site effects. Soft, young sediments amplify earthquake ground motion — they take the bedrock motion and make it larger and longer in duration at the surface. This amplification can increase peak ground acceleration by a factor of 3 to 5 and shift the dominant period of shaking to the natural period of the soil column.

For the 2015 Gorkha earthquake:
- Bedrock peak ground acceleration (PGA): approximately 0.15 to 0.25 g
- Ground surface PGA in Kathmandu Valley: 0.16 to 0.30 g (somewhat attenuated by soil, which is characteristic of soft soil response at high-frequency ground motion but amplified at lower frequencies)
- The dominant period of shaking at many Kathmandu locations was 0.5 to 5 seconds — exactly the range that affects taller, flexible structures more than short, stiff ones

Research after the 2015 earthquake (Bhatt et al., 2017; Hashash et al., 2015; Rajaure et al., 2017) confirmed that buildings on soft Kalimati Clay in the central valley experienced greater damage than buildings at the valley margins on stiffer alluvial deposits. This is consistent with theoretical site amplification predictions.

### Liquefaction Hazard

The sandy layers within the upper lacustrine deposits — particularly uniform fine sand layers found at depth in some parts of the valley — are potentially liquefiable under seismic loading. Liquefaction requires:
1. Saturated, loose to medium-dense sand (fine or silty sand is most susceptible)
2. Seismic shaking sufficient to generate large pore pressure

A liquefaction susceptibility assessment of Kathmandu Valley (Subedi et al., 2013; Sharma and Deng, 2019) identified significant portions of the valley as moderately to highly susceptible to liquefaction for a Mw 7.5 to 8.0 earthquake. The 2015 earthquake did not produce dramatic surface liquefaction in Kathmandu (the city is not on obviously sandy deposits), but lateral spreading and foundation distress attributable to liquefaction were documented in some riverside areas.

## Part 2: Nepal's Seismic Hazard

### The Tectonic Setting

Nepal sits on the convergent boundary between the Indian Plate and the Eurasian Plate. India is moving northward at approximately 40 to 50 mm per year, constantly compressing the Himalayas. The interface between the two plates — the Main Himalayan Thrust — is a low-angle fault that extends beneath the entire Himalayan range. Great earthquakes occur on this fault when accumulated stress is released catastrophically.

The historical seismic record of Nepal includes:
- **1255 CE**: Large earthquake, reportedly killed half the population of Kathmandu Valley
- **1934 CE (Bihar-Nepal earthquake)**: Mw 8.0 to 8.4; approximately 8,500 killed in Nepal alone; widespread collapse in Kathmandu
- **1988 CE (Udaypur earthquake)**: Mw 6.9; 721 killed
- **2015 CE (Gorkha earthquake)**: Mw 7.8; approximately 8,900 killed; massive damage

Paleoseismic studies indicate that major sections of the Main Himalayan Thrust have not ruptured since at least 1255 CE. There is significant accumulated stress that has not been released. Nepal lies in a zone of very high seismic hazard — the highest in South Asia.

### Nepal National Building Code (NBC) — Seismic Design Requirements

Nepal has its own national building code, the **Nepal National Building Code (NBC)**, developed with international assistance over multiple revision cycles. The most recent comprehensive revision followed the 2015 earthquake.

Relevant codes for housing construction:
- **NBC 105:2020**: Seismic Design of Buildings in Nepal. This is the primary seismic design standard, now aligned with modern performance-based seismic engineering principles.
- **NBC 201:1994 (revised)**: Guidelines for Construction of Buildings with Load Bearing Brick Masonry Walls (traditional construction — now being phased out for most applications in seismically active areas)
- **NBC 109:2020**: Materials and Dimensions for Construction of Small Buildings

**NBC 105:2020 Seismic Zonation**:

Nepal is divided into seismic zones based on horizontal spectral acceleration:
- Zone I: Low to moderate hazard (remote mountain areas)
- Zone II: Moderate hazard (mid-hills)
- Zone III: High hazard — includes Kathmandu Valley
- Zone IV: Very high hazard — includes parts of the Terai and some hill areas

For Kathmandu (Zone III), NBC 105 specifies:
- Peak ground acceleration at rock (Z): 0.35g to 0.40g for a 475-year return period earthquake (10% probability of exceedance in 50 years)
- Site amplification factors: Applied to the rock PGA based on site class, using a site coefficient table similar to ASCE 7. For soft soil (Site Class E, Vs30 < 180 m/s): amplification factor Fa = 2.5 (short period) and Fv = 3.5 (1-second period). This yields a design spectral acceleration at short periods (SMS) of 0.35 × 2.5 = 0.875g for soft soil sites.
- Design base shear: Depends on occupancy category, structural system, height, and site class; for a typical single-family house on soft soil, design base shear (horizontal force as percentage of building weight) can range from 10% to 20%+ of total building weight.

For comparison: the IBC seismic design provisions in California's most hazardous zones require similar base shear percentages. Kathmandu is in the same league as the most seismically active parts of California.

## Part 3: EPS and PUF Sandwich Panel Construction

### What Sandwich Panels Are

EPS (Expanded Polystyrene) and PUF (Polyurethane Foam) sandwich panels are composite structural elements consisting of a rigid foam core bonded to two structural face sheets. They combine insulation and structural function in a single prefabricated element.

**EPS sandwich panels** use expanded polystyrene foam cores. EPS is a closed-cell, rigid foam with excellent compressive strength (10 to 35 psi depending on density), very low thermal conductivity (R-3.6 to R-4.2 per inch), and low cost. Face sheets can be:
- Steel (for structural panels in commercial and industrial construction)
- Fiber cement
- Oriented strand board (OSB) (for SIPs — Structural Insulated Panels)
- Concrete (shotcrete applied over a wire mesh-reinforced EPS core — the ICF-adjacent "3D panel" or "PolySteel" type)

**PUF (Polyurethane Foam) sandwich panels** use rigid polyurethane or polyisocyanurate foam cores. PUF has higher insulation value (R-6 to R-7 per inch) than EPS and is stronger per unit weight. Face sheets are typically steel for commercial applications. More expensive than EPS panels.

In Nepal and South Asia, the term "EPS panel" or "foam concrete panel" often specifically refers to a system where:
- An EPS foam core (50 to 200 mm thick) is sandwiched between two layers of wire mesh
- The panel is installed in the field and both faces are shot with concrete (shotcrete) to create a composite reinforced concrete-over-foam sandwich
- The result is a monolithic concrete wall with a foam core, providing both structure and insulation

This "3D panel" or "ferro-cement sandwich" system is distinct from pure prefabricated EPS structural panels and from SIPs.

### Advantages of Sandwich Panel Construction in Seismic Contexts

**Light weight**: This is the most critical advantage in seismic design. Seismic force = Mass × Acceleration. Reduce mass, reduce seismic force. A sandwich panel wall weighs 15 to 40 kg/m² (3 to 8 psf). A comparable masonry wall weighs 200 to 400 kg/m² (40 to 80 psf). The sandwich panel wall imposes 5 to 20× less seismic force on the foundation than masonry.

For a 100 m² (1,000 sq ft) single-story house:
- Masonry construction: Wall mass approximately 15,000 to 25,000 kg; seismic base shear at 0.2g design acceleration: 30,000 to 50,000 N (3 to 5 tons)
- Sandwich panel construction: Wall mass approximately 2,000 to 5,000 kg; seismic base shear at same design acceleration: 4,000 to 10,000 N (0.4 to 1 ton)

The foundation and connections must resist the seismic force — lighter structure means smaller foundation demand.

**Ductility (for properly designed systems)**: Masonry is brittle. When overloaded, it collapses suddenly. Reinforced concrete-faced sandwich panels, properly connected, can develop ductile behavior — yielding before failure, warning occupants, and absorbing energy through inelastic deformation.

**Construction speed**: Sandwich panel systems can be erected much faster than masonry, reducing construction time and exposure to weather during construction.

**Good insulation**: At Kathmandu's elevation (1,400 m), winters are cold. EPS and PUF panels provide substantially better thermal performance than brick masonry (which has very low insulation value).

**Potential disadvantages**:
- Requires skilled installation and shotcrete application (for 3D panel systems)
- Connections between panels and between panels and foundations are critical and must be designed and constructed carefully
- Fire resistance requires careful specification of face materials and coatings
- Seismic performance depends heavily on the specific system and connection details — not all sandwich panel systems are equal

## Part 4: Foundation Design for EPS/PUF Sandwich Panel Construction in Kathmandu

### Step 1: Site Investigation

This cannot be skipped in Kathmandu. The variability of subsurface conditions across the valley is enormous — the difference between a site with stiff gravels at 3 meters depth and a site with Kalimati Clay to 30 meters depth is completely invisible from the surface but has massive implications for foundation design.

**Minimum site investigation for a residential building in Kathmandu**:

1. **Review existing data**: 
   - Consult the Geological Survey of Nepal's published geological maps of Kathmandu Valley
   - Review the Department of Urban Development and Building Construction (DUDBC) geological hazard zonation maps
   - USAID, JICA, and World Bank post-2015 earthquake assessments include detailed maps of site class and liquefaction susceptibility across the valley

2. **Minimum field investigation**:
   - Two or three soil borings to 10 to 15 meters depth (or until refusal on dense material)
   - SPT testing at 1-meter intervals in the upper 5 meters, 1.5-meter intervals below
   - Note groundwater depth
   - Collect disturbed samples at 1.5-meter intervals for classification testing

3. **Laboratory tests on samples**:
   - Grain size analysis and Atterberg limits for all major soil layers
   - Moisture content
   - Unconfined compression test on any cohesive samples to measure undrained shear strength

4. **Shear wave velocity**: For accurate site classification and seismic site factor determination, a shear wave velocity profile is extremely valuable. MASW (Multichannel Analysis of Surface Waves) or downhole velocity measurement in the boreholes. This may not be feasible for very small residential projects, but for any project in an uncertain geological location it is worthwhile.

5. **Geotechnical report**: A licensed geotechnical engineer should prepare a report interpreting the investigation data and making foundation recommendations specific to the site conditions.

Cost of geotechnical investigation in Kathmandu: NPR 50,000 to 150,000 (approximately $400 to $1,200 USD) for a residential-scale investigation with 2 to 3 borings.

### Step 2: Soil Classification and Foundation Type Selection

Based on the investigation results, classify the site and select the appropriate foundation:

**Site Class A (Vs30 > 1,500 m/s)**: Rock — extremely rare at the surface in Kathmandu Valley
**Site Class B (Vs30 760 to 1,500 m/s)**: Rock — also rare
**Site Class C (Vs30 360 to 760 m/s)**: Stiff soil — found in some valley margin areas and where gravels are near surface
**Site Class D (Vs30 180 to 360 m/s)**: Stiff soil — most common classification for established neighborhoods in central Kathmandu with weathered alluvial deposits or compacted fills
**Site Class E (Vs30 < 180 m/s)**: Soft soil — found in Kalimati Clay areas and some filled tidal/wetland areas

**Foundation selection based on site class and soil conditions**:

**Competent bearing at < 2 meters depth (stiff alluvial gravel or weathered colluvium)**: Shallow spread footings are appropriate. Allowable bearing capacity typically 100 to 200 kPa (2,000 to 4,000 psf). Footing depth: minimum 0.75 to 1.5 meters.

**Soft clay or fill in upper 5 meters, gravel below**: Shallow footing bearing may be marginal. Options:
- Remove and replace soft material with compacted granular fill (if depth is less than 2 meters)
- Slightly deeper footing to reach the gravel layer
- Driven timber or concrete piles to the gravel

**Kalimati Clay throughout**: Deep foundation required. Driven piles or bored piles to the deeper alluvial gravels (which may be at 10 to 30+ meters depth). This significantly increases foundation cost.

**Mix of sand and clay (typical in much of the valley)**: Moderate-depth footings reaching stiff layers below the soft upper material. Allowable bearing on identified stiff layers (N > 10): approximately 75 to 120 kPa (1,500 to 2,500 psf).

### Step 3: Seismic Demand on the Foundation

For a sandwich panel house in Kathmandu, calculate the design seismic base shear using NBC 105:2020:

**Example calculation for a typical single-story, 100 m² EPS panel house**:

*Assume*:
- Kathmandu Valley, Zone III
- Site Class D (Vs30 ≈ 250 m/s, stiff alluvial deposit)
- Building height: one story = 3 meters; fundamental period T ≈ 0.1 second (very stiff and light)
- Importance factor I = 1.0 (standard residential)
- Response modification factor R = 4.0 (for reinforced concrete shear wall system — the shotcrete faces of the sandwich panel act as shear walls)

Design spectral acceleration (NBC 105):
- PGA at rock: Z = 0.35g (Zone III)
- Site amplification factor Fa (for Site Class D, short period): 1.3
- Design spectral acceleration Ss = Z × Fa = 0.35 × 1.3 = 0.455g

Design base shear coefficient:
- V/W = Ss × I / R = 0.455 × 1.0 / 4.0 = 0.114

*Total building weight* (EPS panel house):
- Walls: 100 m² footprint × 4 m perimeter wall height × 35 kg/m² panel weight = ~14,000 kg
- Roof: 100 m² × 60 kg/m² (lightweight metal roof) = 6,000 kg
- Floor/contents/live load: 100 m² × 100 kg/m² = 10,000 kg
- Total: ≈ 30,000 kg = 294 kN

*Design base shear*:
- V = 0.114 × 294 kN = 33.5 kN ≈ 3.4 metric tons

This 3.4-ton lateral force must be transferred from the sandwich panel walls to the foundation and from the foundation to the soil.

For comparison, a similar-footprint masonry house:
- Wall mass alone: ~100 m² footprint × 4 m height × 250 kg/m² = 100,000 kg
- Total building weight: ≈ 150,000 to 200,000 kg
- Design base shear: 0.114 × 1,700 kN = 194 kN ≈ 20 metric tons

The sandwich panel house imposes approximately **6× less seismic demand on the foundation** than masonry. This is the fundamental reason lightweight construction is advantageous in high seismic hazard areas.

### Step 4: Foundation Design for the EPS Panel House

#### Scenario A: Good Soil (Stiff Gravel at < 2 m Depth)

**Foundation type**: Reinforced concrete isolated footings under all wall intersections plus continuous strip footings under all bearing walls, with a reinforced concrete ring beam (grade beam) at the footing level connecting all footings.

**Footing design**:
- Allowable bearing pressure on stiff alluvial gravel: 150 kPa (3,000 psf)
- Footing area required = building weight / allowable bearing pressure = 300 kN / 150 kPa = 2.0 m²
- Perimeter of a 10×10 m house: 40 m of strip footing
- Strip footing width = 2.0 m² / 40 m = 0.05 m — only 5 cm, which is well below the minimum practical size
- Use minimum footing width: 300 mm wide × 300 mm deep (NBC minimum for residential), with rebar

Since the gravity load demands are so low (light building), the footing size is governed by minimum practical dimensions and construction tolerances, not by soil bearing capacity.

**Seismic demand on footing**:
- Horizontal force (base shear): 33.5 kN along the full foundation perimeter = 33.5 kN / 40 m = 0.84 kN/m along each footing
- This horizontal force is resisted by friction between the footing and the soil: friction coefficient ≈ 0.4 to 0.5 for concrete on gravel
- Friction resistance = 0.4 × 300 kN (vertical reaction) = 120 kN >> 33.5 kN ✓
- The footing has more than adequate friction to resist the base shear — no passive resistance or anchor system needed

**Grade beam**: A 200 mm wide × 300 mm deep reinforced concrete grade beam connects all footings at the footing elevation, tying the foundation together and creating a rigid base that distributes seismic forces uniformly.

**Reinforcement**:
- Grade beam: 4 × 12 mm bars longitudinal (2 top, 2 bottom), 8 mm ties at 200 mm spacing, 10 mm ties at 100 mm spacing at corners and junctions
- Footing pad: 12 mm bars at 200 mm each way, top and bottom
- Concrete: minimum 20 MPa (approximately 3,000 psi) — NBC minimum for structural concrete

**Panel-to-foundation connection**: This is the most critical detail. The sandwich panel walls must be rigidly connected to the foundation to transfer seismic shear. Standard details:

1. Vertical reinforcing bars (starter bars) project from the footing into the wall base: minimum 12 mm diameter at 300 mm on center, projecting 400 mm above the footing top surface (50× bar diameter development length for good bond)
2. The EPS panel is installed over the starter bars (the bars pass through the foam core between the wire mesh layers)
3. Shotcrete is applied to both faces, fully encasing the starter bars in the concrete face layers
4. The connection is now a rebar-to-concrete mechanical bond — the wall cannot slide or overturn at the footing junction without fracturing the concrete

This connection detail is essentially a doweled shear connection. The reinforced concrete faces of the panel become the shear wall elements, and the starter bars are the shear transfer mechanism.

#### Scenario B: Soft Upper Clay, Stiff Gravel at 5 to 10 m Depth

This is the more challenging and more common scenario in central Kathmandu.

**Option B1: Remove and Replace (for shallow soft layer < 2.5 m)**

If the soft Kalimati Clay layer is shallow (0 to 2 meters), excavate the full building footprint to the underlying stiff layer, replace with compacted granular fill (well-graded gravel compacted to 95% modified Proctor), and bear the footing on the engineered fill.

- Excavation depth: 2 to 2.5 meters
- Fill material: Crushed stone or well-graded natural gravel, NPR 1200 to 1800 per m³
- Cost: NPR 80,000 to 200,000 (approximately $650 to $1,600 USD) for fill material alone on a 100 m² footprint
- This approach requires adequate site access for excavation equipment (not always feasible in dense Kathmandu neighborhoods)

**Option B2: Driven Bamboo or Timber Piles (Traditional Low-Cost Option)**

In parts of Nepal and South Asia, driven bamboo or small-diameter timber piles have been used to densify loose soil and provide some load transfer. This is a traditional technique with limited modern research validation but has historical precedent.

For an EPS panel house (very light), bamboo piles at 600 mm on center driven 3 to 5 meters into soft clay can provide sufficient capacity. However, this system has limited seismic validation and should be used with caution — the piles provide little lateral resistance and the soft clay around them offers essentially no passive resistance.

**Option B3: Cast-In-Place Concrete Piles (Bored Piles)**

Small-diameter (200 to 300 mm) bored concrete piles drilled to the underlying stiff layer (5 to 10 meters). This is the most reliable solution but requires drilling equipment and skilled labor.

- Pile diameter: 250 mm
- Pile depth: 8 meters (to reach N > 30 alluvial gravel)
- Pile capacity: (skin friction in clay 20 kPa × π × 0.25 m × 8 m) + (end bearing 150 kPa × π/4 × 0.25²) = 125 kN + 7 kN = 132 kN per pile
- Design load per pile (FS = 2.5): 52.8 kN
- Number of piles for 300 kN building: 300 / 52.8 = 6 piles minimum (use 8 to 10 for symmetry and for lateral resistance)
- Pile cap and grade beam connecting piles

**Seismic behavior of piles**: Piles in soft clay during earthquakes experience "kinematic interaction" — the soil wants to move laterally with the earthquake ground motion, but the pile is stiffer than the soil and bends. This bending creates moments in the pile that must be resisted by the pile's flexural capacity. The critical locations are:
- The pile head (where the pile cap constrains lateral movement)
- The interface between soft clay and stiff layer (where relative lateral displacement is largest)

For an EPS panel house, the pile bending demand is modest (the superstructure lateral force is small). 8 to 12 mm longitudinal rebar in the pile provides adequate flexural capacity.

#### Scenario C: Kalimati Clay to Great Depth (> 15 m)

For sites on deep Kalimati Clay without accessible stiff layer at reasonable depth, a more radical approach is needed.

**Option: Raft Foundation with Soil Improvement**

A reinforced concrete raft (mat) foundation spanning the entire building footprint distributes the very light load of the EPS panel house over such a large area that the bearing pressure on even very soft clay is within acceptable limits.

- Raft area: 100 m² (full building footprint)
- Average bearing pressure: 300 kN / 100 m² = 3.0 kPa = 3,000 Pa

Even very soft Kalimati Clay with undrained shear strength of 20 kPa has a bearing capacity of approximately:
- q_ult = 6 × cu = 6 × 20 = 120 kPa

Allowable bearing (FS = 3): 40 kPa >> 3 kPa ✓

The raft easily satisfies bearing capacity for the very light EPS panel building. The concern is settlement:
- Kalimati Clay is highly compressible
- Even 3 kPa of average pressure will cause some consolidation settlement over time
- Differential settlement is the real risk — if load is uneven across the raft, one side settles more than another

Mitigation:
- Use a stiffened raft (thickened ribs in both directions, creating a waffle pattern) to increase rigidity and reduce differential deflection
- Maintain uniform load on the raft (symmetric building layout; no heavy point loads from chimneys or tanks)
- Consider soil improvement beneath the raft: lime columns (mixing quicklime into the clay to reduce plasticity and increase strength) or vertical drains with preloading to accelerate consolidation before the building is constructed

**Soil improvement options available in Nepal**:
- **Lime/cement stabilization**: Mixing hydraulic lime or Portland cement into the upper 1 to 2 meters of soil significantly increases bearing capacity. Used successfully in road construction in the Terai; applicable to foundation subgrade.
- **Stone columns**: Aggregate columns driven into soft clay by vibration, creating dense gravel inclusions that increase bearing capacity and reduce compressibility. Requires specialized equipment not always available in Nepal.
- **Sand/gravel compaction piles**: Similar to stone columns; more accessible with local equipment.

## Part 5: Earthquake-Resistant Detailing for EPS Panel Houses in Kathmandu

### The Critical Connections

In earthquake engineering, a chain is as strong as its weakest link. For a sandwich panel house, the potential weak links are:

**1. Foundation-to-Wall Connection (Critical)**

As described above: starter bars from the foundation, bent into the wall at 90° or straight, develop full bar capacity in the shotcrete face. This is the "held down" connection that prevents the wall from sliding or overturning at the footing.

**2. Wall-to-Wall Connections at Corners and Intersections**

At building corners and at the intersection of perpendicular walls, the panels must be connected to transfer shear forces in multiple directions. Detail:
- Vertical reinforcement bars at each intersection, extending the full height of the wall, lapped with horizontal bars from each wall panel
- Shotcrete fully encases all reinforcement at connections
- No gaps or unconnected joints at corners

**3. Wall-to-Roof Connection (Diaphragm Connection)**

The roof must be able to transfer lateral forces (wind and seismic acting on the roof level) to the walls below. For a lightweight metal roof:
- Install a continuous reinforced concrete ring beam at the top of the wall panels (bond beam)
- The ring beam encircles the entire building and provides a strong, stiff element at the top of the shear wall system
- Connect roof framing to the ring beam with anchor bolts or straps designed for the design lateral force

**4. Openings (Doors and Windows)**

Openings interrupt the shear wall continuity. Reinforced concrete lintels above openings and posts (jamb columns) beside openings maintain structural continuity through the opening zone. For EPS panels:
- Preformed concrete columns are cast in place at opening edges before panels are erected, or
- Additional shotcrete is applied in a thickened zone at opening edges

**5. Diaphragm — Roof and Floor**

The horizontal diaphragm (roof or floor structure) collects the distributed lateral loads from walls and delivers them to the vertical shear walls. For a one-story EPS panel house:
- A lightweight metal roof provides minimal diaphragm action
- A reinforced concrete ring beam at the top of the walls compensates — it is the diaphragm chord, collecting the lateral forces at the eaves level
- Alternatively, a lightweight concrete slab roof provides excellent diaphragm action

### Detailing for Soft Soil (Site Class E)

NBC 105:2020 and ASCE 7-22 both require additional detailing precautions for structures on Site Class E soil (soft clay) in high seismic hazard areas:

- **Foundation mat or grade beam**: Required to tie all foundation elements together
- **Increased rebar in piles**: For bending resistance during kinematic interaction
- **No unreinforced masonry**: Absolutely prohibited for structures on soft soil in high seismic zones
- **Avoid plan irregularities**: L-shaped or U-shaped buildings on soft soil concentrate seismic demands at reentrant corners; square or rectangular plans are strongly preferred
- **Keep building light**: Every kilogram of reduced building mass reduces seismic force

## Part 6: Post-2015 Building in Kathmandu — What Has Changed

### The Regulatory Response

After the 2015 earthquake, Nepal strengthened its building code enforcement significantly:

- **DUDBC's Technical Assistance Programme**: Trained local engineers and masons in seismic-resistant construction techniques
- **Nepal Seismic Risk Management Initiative**: Documented damage patterns and identified which construction systems performed best
- **Updated NBC 105:2020**: Comprehensive revision of the seismic design code, adopting performance-based principles
- **Owner-builder assistance**: The National Reconstruction Authority (NRA) provided technical assistance, model designs, and in some cases materials to households rebuilding after the earthquake

For sandwich panel construction specifically:
- Several NGOs (including UN-Habitat, USAID-funded organizations, and Build Change) promoted lightweight EPS and ferro-cement sandwich panel systems in the reconstruction, particularly in rural areas where traditional masonry had failed catastrophically
- Build Change published technical guidelines for sandwich panel construction in Nepal, specifically addressing connection details and seismic performance
- These systems performed well in aftershock sequences and in subsequent smaller earthquakes

### Case Example: Post-Earthquake EPS Panel House in Bhaktapur

Bhaktapur, a historic city east of Kathmandu, was severely damaged in 2015. One documented rebuilding project used a ferro-cement sandwich panel system for a 60 m² two-room house:

**Site**: Alluvial gravel at 1.5 m depth, N = 15 to 25
**Foundation**: Reinforced concrete strip footing, 400 mm wide × 350 mm deep, at 1.2 m depth; grade beam connecting all footings; starter bars at 300 mm on center
**Walls**: 100 mm EPS core with wire mesh both faces, shotcrete applied in two passes (15 mm each side), total wall thickness 140 mm
**Roof**: Lightweight galvanized steel truss with metal roofing; ring beam at wall top
**Cost**: Approximately NPR 750,000 (approximately $6,000 USD) for the complete structure including foundation and finish
**Performance**: Survived two magnitude 5.0 aftershocks and multiple smaller events with no structural damage

This example illustrates that a properly designed and built lightweight panel house on a properly engineered foundation in Kathmandu is not only feasible but cost-effective and highly earthquake-resistant.

## Part 7: Summary Recommendations for EPS/PUF Sandwich Panel Construction in Kathmandu

Based on the above analysis, here are practical recommendations for anyone building with EPS or PUF sandwich panels in Kathmandu:

### Foundation Selection Decision Tree

1. **Conduct a geotechnical investigation** (non-negotiable in Kathmandu Valley)
2. **If stiff alluvial gravel or colluvium is within 2 meters**: Reinforced concrete strip footings + grade beam, 1.0 to 1.5 m deep, 300 mm wide minimum, with seismic-designed starter bar connection
3. **If stiff material is at 2 to 5 meters**: Remove and replace soft material with compacted gravel fill; or construct slightly deeper footings to the stiff layer
4. **If stiff material is at 5 to 15 meters**: Bored concrete piles (200 to 300 mm diameter) to the stiff layer + reinforced concrete pile cap and grade beam
5. **If stiff material is at > 15 meters (deep Kalimati Clay)**: Raft foundation with soil improvement (lime stabilization of upper soil; stiffened raft); or deep piles if budget allows
6. **If site is in flood zone (low-lying near rivers)**: Elevate first floor above flood level; consider pile-supported foundation

### Critical Details for All Cases

- Use 20 MPa (3,000 psi) concrete minimum in all structural elements
- Grade beam or ring beam connecting all foundation elements is mandatory
- Starter bars from foundation into wall: 12 mm diameter at maximum 300 mm spacing, with 50-diameter development length into both foundation concrete and wall shotcrete
- Shotcrete on wall faces: minimum 35 MPa compressive strength; applied in at minimum two passes of 15 mm each; full compaction
- Ring beam at wall top: 200 mm wide × 300 mm deep reinforced concrete, 4 × 12 mm longitudinal bars, 8 mm ties at 200 mm
- Engage a licensed structural engineer (registered with Nepal Engineering Council) to review and stamp all structural drawings
- Obtain building permit from the relevant Municipality before starting construction

## Conclusion: Lightness Is a Structural Asset

The counterintuitive lesson of earthquake engineering is that lighter buildings are often safer buildings. Not because they are weaker — but because they are subjected to smaller forces. A building that weighs 50% less than its neighbor experiences 50% less seismic force. A foundation that needs to transfer 50% less force is smaller, cheaper, and easier to build correctly.

Expanded polystyrene and polyurethane foam sandwich panels, when properly designed and connected to a seismically appropriate foundation, represent a genuinely excellent solution for residential construction in earthquake-prone areas like Kathmandu Valley. The foundation must be designed for the actual loads — much lighter than masonry — and for the actual seismic demand, which in the Nepal context is among the highest in the world.

The engineering knowledge exists. The products exist. The construction techniques exist. What killed people in 2015 was not insufficient technology — it was the absence of that technology in the buildings that failed. Every new building in Kathmandu, built with proper seismic design and proper foundation engineering, is a life saved in the next earthquake.
