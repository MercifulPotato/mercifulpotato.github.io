So far in this series, when we have spoken of "the government," we have meant a single thing: the federal government in Kathmandu that writes the budget we are studying. But this is a simplification we promised, back in Part 3, to correct — and now we correct it, because it changes how you should read a large part of the budget. Nepal does not have one government. It has three layers of government, stacked on top of one another, each with its own elected officials, its own responsibilities, and its own claim on public money. And a very large share of the federal budget — more than NPR 600 billion, on the order of USD 4 billion — does not stay with the federal government at all. It flows *downward*, to the provinces and to hundreds of local governments below them.

This is the subject of the present post: the system of dividing power and money among multiple levels of government, which is called **federalism**, and specifically the financial side of it, which is called **fiscal federalism**. We will learn what federalism is and why a country would choose it; how Nepal's three tiers are arranged; how money flows down from the centre to the lower tiers, through several distinct kinds of grants; and why this whole arrangement is, simultaneously, a way of bringing government closer to the people and a source of great expense, duplication, and dispute. As always, we will weigh both sides honestly: federalism is neither the salvation its champions claim nor the waste its critics allege, but a genuine trade-off whose costs and benefits a citizen should be able to see clearly.

## What is federalism?

Begin with the basic choice every country faces about how to organise its government: should power be concentrated in one central authority, or divided among several levels?

A **unitary** state concentrates power at the centre. There is one government that holds ultimate authority over the whole country; any regional or local administrations exist only as creations of, and subordinates to, that central government, which can grant them powers and take those powers back. Most countries in the world are unitary, and Nepal itself was unitary for most of its history — governed from Kathmandu, with local administration as an extension of central authority rather than a power in its own right.

A **federal** state, by contrast, *divides* sovereign power among more than one level of government, each with its own constitutionally guaranteed sphere of authority that the others cannot simply abolish. In a federal system, there is a central (federal) government for the whole country, and there are regional governments (in Nepal's case, provinces) and often local governments, each with powers assigned to it by the constitution itself — powers it holds in its own right, not merely on loan from the centre. The defining feature of federalism is this constitutional division: lower tiers of government are not mere branch offices of the capital but governments in their own right, with their own elected leaders, their own responsibilities, and their own resources. Nepal became a federal state with its 2015 constitution, in one of the most profound transformations in its modern history — a deliberate dismantling of centralised rule in favour of dividing power across the country.

Why would a country make such a change? The arguments for federalism are real and worth understanding, because they explain what Nepal was trying to achieve. The first is *bringing government closer to the people*: a local government, elected by and embedded in a particular community, is in principle far better placed to understand and respond to that community's needs than a distant central ministry in the capital — it knows which local road needs repair, which local school needs a teacher, in a way Kathmandu cannot. The second is *accommodating diversity*: in a country as varied as Nepal — many ethnic groups, languages, and regions, layered across that vertical geography — federalism allows different regions some power to govern themselves according to their own circumstances, which can ease the tensions that a single, distant, one-size-fits-all central government might inflame. The third is *checking central power*: dividing authority among levels is itself a safeguard against the over-concentration of power in any single capital. These were, broadly, the hopes behind Nepal's embrace of federalism after decades of centralised rule that many regions had experienced as neglectful or domineering. Whether those hopes have been realised — and at what cost — is precisely the kind of question this post exists to weigh, without either the romanticism of federalism's champions or the dismissiveness of its detractors.

## Nepal's three tiers

Nepal's constitution establishes not two but *three* tiers of government, and you should hold all three clearly in mind, because money flows between all of them.

At the top is the **federal government** — the central government in Kathmandu, responsible for matters that affect the whole country: defence, foreign affairs, the national currency and central banking, major national infrastructure, and the overall framework of law and policy. This is the government whose budget we are studying. It is the largest and most powerful tier, and crucially, it controls the largest and most lucrative sources of revenue — which is why so much money must then flow downward from it to the tiers below.

In the middle are the **provinces** — seven of them, each with its own provincial government, its own elected provincial assembly, and its own chief minister and ministers, responsible for matters at the provincial scale. The provinces are the newest and, in some ways, the least settled tier; created from scratch in 2015, they have had to build their institutions and define their roles largely from nothing, and debate continues in Nepal about their effectiveness and even their necessity.

At the base are the **local governments** — and here is a number that surprises people: there are **seven hundred and fifty-three** of them. These are the municipalities and rural municipalities into which the entire country is divided, each with its own elected local council, mayor or chair, and local administration, responsible for the most immediate, ground-level functions: local roads and infrastructure, basic services, local schooling and health facilities, and the daily business of governing a particular place. The local tier is, in the federal vision, the one closest to the people — the level at which a citizen is meant to be able to walk into an office and have a say in how their own community is run. Seven hundred and fifty-three separate local governments, plus seven provincial governments, plus the federal government: that is the architecture, and every one of those units has elected officials to pay, offices to run, and functions to fund. The sheer number of governments is itself one of the central facts of Nepali fiscal federalism, and, as we will see, one of the central sources of its expense.

## The fiscal problem federalism creates

Here is the financial puzzle at the heart of every federal system, and understanding it makes the whole structure of transfers comprehensible. When you divide a country's government into multiple tiers, you create a mismatch between *who has the money* and *who has the responsibilities*.

The central government, almost everywhere, controls the largest and most efficient sources of revenue — the major taxes that are best collected at the national level, such as customs duties at the borders and the largest shares of value-added tax and income tax. It is simply more practical to collect these centrally than to have hundreds of local governments each running their own customs and income-tax systems. So the money is concentrated at the top. But the *responsibilities* — the actual delivery of many services, the building of local infrastructure, the running of local schools and clinics — are pushed *downward*, to the provinces and especially to the local governments, precisely because those lower tiers are meant to be closer to the people and better placed to deliver. The result is a structural imbalance: the upper tier has more money than its own direct responsibilities require, while the lower tiers have more responsibilities than their own revenue-raising powers can fund. Economists call this the problem of *fiscal imbalance* — a vertical mismatch between revenue and responsibility across the tiers.

The solution, in every federal system, is **transfers**: the central government collects the bulk of the revenue and then *transfers* large sums downward to the provincial and local governments to fund the responsibilities it has assigned them. This is why a huge share of Nepal's federal budget — over NPR 600 billion, on the order of USD 4 billion — does not stay at the centre but flows down to the lower tiers. It is not the federal government being generous; it is the federal government funding the responsibilities that the federal *system* has placed on the tiers below, using the revenue that the system has concentrated at the top. Understanding this resolves what would otherwise be a puzzle — why a "federal budget" spends so much of its money on governments other than the federal one. The answer is that in a federal system, moving money from where it is collected to where it is needed is one of the central functions of the national budget.

## How the money flows down: the kinds of grants

The transfers from the federal government to the provinces and local governments do not all work the same way. The constitution and the law establish several distinct *kinds* of grants, each with a different purpose and a different degree of control attached, and the distinction matters enormously — because the *kind* of grant determines how much real freedom the lower tier has to use the money according to its own judgement, and this is where a great deal of the politics of federalism lives. There are, broadly, four kinds, and the allocation of these grants is meant to be guided by the recommendations of an independent constitutional body established for the purpose — a commission charged with advising on the fair distribution of resources among the tiers, so that the division is based on objective criteria rather than political favouritism.

The first and most important kind is the **fiscal equalization grant**. This is *unconditional* money — funds transferred to a province or local government for it to spend according to its *own* priorities and judgement, with no strings attached as to exactly what it must be spent on. The purpose of an equalization grant is in its name: to *equalize*, to reduce the disparities between richer and poorer regions, by giving more to the tiers and places that have greater needs and weaker revenue-raising capacity of their own, so that a poor, remote local government can still fund basic services that its own meagre local revenue could never cover. The equalization grant is the truest expression of the federal idea, because it trusts the lower tier with the freedom to decide how to use the money for its own people. In this budget, the equalization grants amount to about NPR 61.50 billion (around USD 0.41 billion) to the provinces and about NPR 90.20 billion (around USD 0.60 billion) to the local governments — money handed down for those tiers to spend largely as they see fit.

The second kind is the **conditional grant**. This is *tied* money — funds transferred for a *specific* purpose, which the lower tier must spend on the thing the federal government has designated, according to the federal government's conditions and standards. A conditional grant might fund a particular national programme — a health initiative, an education scheme, a specific kind of infrastructure — that the central government wants delivered uniformly across the country, and the lower tier is, in effect, executing the centre's programme with the centre's money under the centre's rules. Conditional grants are how the federal government ensures that national priorities are pursued everywhere, but they are also, by their nature, a *constraint* on the autonomy of the lower tiers, because the money comes with instructions. In this budget the conditional grants are large — about NPR 39.72 billion (around USD 0.26 billion) to the provinces and a very substantial NPR 206.08 billion (around USD 1.37 billion) to the local governments. Notice that figure: the conditional grants to local governments dwarf the unconditional equalization grants to them, and this imbalance is significant, as we will see when we examine the deceptions of federalism — because a system in which most of the money flowing down comes with strings attached is a system in which real local autonomy is more limited than the rhetoric of decentralisation suggests.

The third and fourth kinds are smaller but worth knowing. There are **complementary** or **matching grants** — money the federal government provides to *co-fund* particular projects alongside the lower tier's own contribution, typically for infrastructure, so that a province or local government that puts up part of the cost of a project can have the federal government match it. And there are **special grants** — funds for particular purposes such as developing services in disadvantaged areas or supporting specific balanced-development goals. These two kinds give the federal government further tools to direct money toward particular projects and priorities, and they too, by their targeted nature, involve federal direction over how the money is used.

So the money flows down through these channels — unconditional equalization grants that trust the lower tiers, conditional grants that direct them, and complementary and special grants that co-fund and target particular ends. And alongside the grants there is one more important flow, which we should name.

## Revenue sharing

Beyond the grants, Nepal's fiscal federalism includes **revenue sharing** — an arrangement under which certain taxes collected are *divided* among the tiers according to set proportions, rather than kept entirely by whichever tier collected them. The most important shared revenues are major taxes such as value-added tax and excise duty, a portion of which is pooled and distributed among the federal, provincial, and local tiers, and the **royalties** from natural resources — the payments earned from the use of resources such as hydropower, forests, and minerals — which are similarly shared, so that the regions where resources are located, and the lower tiers generally, receive a portion of the wealth those resources generate. Revenue sharing differs from grants in that it is a *rule-based division of jointly-generated revenue* rather than a transfer of the centre's own money: the tiers share in a common pool by formula. It is another mechanism by which the resources concentrated at the top are spread across the system, and another expression of the principle that, in a federal country, the wealth of the nation is meant to be shared among its tiers rather than hoarded at the centre.

## Why federalism costs money — and whether it is worth it

We come now to the honest reckoning, which a citizen most needs and which partisans on both sides most often avoid: federalism is *expensive*, and we should be clear-eyed about why, while also being fair about what is bought with the expense.

The costs are real and largely unavoidable in a federal system. Most obviously, *three tiers of government cost more to run than one*. Seven provincial governments and seven hundred and fifty-three local governments each require their own elected officials, their own civil servants, their own offices, their own administrative machinery — all of which must be paid for, year after year, out of recurrent spending. A great deal of the money that flows down to the lower tiers, and a great deal of what they raise themselves, goes simply to the *running costs* of these many governments rather than to services or to building anything — a multiplication of the administrative overhead that a single unitary government would not incur. Beyond the sheer number of governments, federalism creates *duplication and coordination costs*: functions that overlap between tiers, requiring negotiation and sometimes producing confusion about who is responsible for what; the cost of coordinating policy and spending across three levels; and disputes between tiers over money and authority. And it creates *capacity problems*: many of the seven hundred and fifty-three local governments, especially the smaller and poorer ones, lack the trained staff and the administrative capacity to plan, spend, and account for money well, so that funds transferred down to them may be poorly used, unspent, or vulnerable to mismanagement — a local-level echo of the capital-execution problem we examined at the national level. These are not hypothetical costs; they are the everyday reality of running a country through three layers of government, and they consume real resources that might otherwise fund services or investment.

There is a specific recurring friction worth naming because citizens encounter it directly: *overlapping and disputed taxation*. Because all three tiers have some power to raise revenue, and because the boundaries of those powers are not always perfectly clear, businesses and individuals have at times found themselves taxed by more than one tier on the same activity, or caught in disputes between tiers over who has the right to levy a particular charge. This *double taxation* and the disputes around it are a real cost of a federal system whose revenue boundaries are still being worked out, and they fall on ordinary economic actors who must navigate the competing claims of federal, provincial, and local authorities.

And yet — the honest reckoning has two columns. Against these real costs stand the genuine benefits the federal system was meant to deliver, and a fair assessment must weigh them. To the extent that local governments, close to their communities, do deliver services and infrastructure better attuned to local needs than a distant capital could; to the extent that bringing government closer to the people deepens democratic participation and gives communities a real say in their own affairs; to the extent that dividing power eases the regional and ethnic tensions that decades of centralised rule had aggravated, and checks the over-concentration of power in Kathmandu — to that extent, the costs of federalism buy something valuable that a cheaper unitary system would not provide. The question is not whether federalism costs money — it plainly does — but whether what it buys is worth the cost, and that is a genuine and contested question on which reasonable Nepalis disagree. Some argue the benefits have been real and are worth the expense, or will be as the young system matures; others argue that the costs have proved excessive, that the provincial tier in particular has added expense without commensurate benefit, and that the system is more burdensome than the country can afford. This series does not pretend to settle that debate. It insists only that you see both columns — the real costs of running three governments and the real benefits of bringing power closer to the people — and judge the trade-off with open eyes, rather than accepting either the uncritical celebration of decentralisation or the reflexive condemnation of "wasteful" federalism. The truth is a trade-off, and a citizen's job is to weigh it, not to cheer one side.

## A worked example: the grant that couldn't be spent

Make the capacity problem and the conditional-grant constraint concrete with a composite example of a kind that recurs across Nepal's local governments.

Imagine a small, poor, remote rural municipality — one of the seven hundred and fifty-three local governments — with a modest staff, few of them with deep technical or administrative training. From the federal government it receives, among its transfers, a substantial *conditional* grant: money tied to a specific national programme, which it must spend on a particular designated purpose, according to detailed federal rules and standards, with specific procedures for procurement and reporting. The grant is real money, intended to help; on paper, the municipality has been funded to deliver an important service.

Now watch what can go wrong. The conditions attached to the grant may not fit the municipality's actual circumstances — the designated purpose may not match its most pressing local need, but the money cannot be redirected because it is tied. The detailed federal procedures for spending and reporting may exceed what the municipality's small, undertrained staff can manage — the paperwork, the procurement rules, the technical requirements may simply be beyond its capacity to execute correctly. As a result, the money may go *unspent* — the local-level version of the capital-execution problem — sitting unused because the municipality cannot navigate the conditions, or spent late and poorly in a year-end rush, or spent in ways that an auditor later flags as improper because the rules were not followed. Meanwhile, the municipality's most urgent actual need — perhaps a different local priority entirely — goes unaddressed, because the money it received was tied to something else and its own unconditional equalization grant was too small to cover it. The result is the worst of several worlds: money allocated but not delivering, local needs unmet despite funds being available, and the appearance of a well-funded local government that is in practice unable to convert its funding into outcomes.

The example illuminates several of this post's themes at once. It shows why the *balance* between conditional and unconditional grants matters — a system that ties most of the money flowing down leaves little room for local governments to address their own real priorities, undercutting the very local autonomy federalism promised. It shows why *capacity*, not just money, is the binding constraint at the local level — that transferring funds to a government that cannot effectively spend them does not produce services. And it shows why the rhetoric of decentralisation — power and money to the people — can outrun the reality, when the money comes with strings the locals cannot manage and the local tier lacks the capacity to use its autonomy well. None of this means federalism has failed or that the transfers are pointless; many local governments do use their funds to real effect, and the system is young and still building capacity. But it means that "we sent the money down to the local level" is not the same as "the local level delivered for its people," and a citizen who understands the difference — money transferred versus outcomes achieved — is equipped to judge fiscal federalism on its results rather than its intentions.

## For the technically minded: the transfers as a matrix, and an allocation sketch

For readers who work with structured data, the federal transfer system is naturally a *matrix* — tiers down one axis, kinds of transfer across the other. The single most telling comparison in the matrix is within the local-government column: about NPR 90.20 billion of unconditional equalization money against about NPR 206.08 billion of conditional, tied money. More than twice as much of what flows down to local governments comes with strings as comes free for local priorities — a ratio that quietly reveals how much real autonomy the system grants versus how much it retains for central direction.

Now a sketch of how a transfer is meant to be allocated — by *need and capacity*, not politics — which is the principle the independent fiscal commission is meant to enforce:

```text
# Fiscal equalization is meant to give MORE to tiers/places with greater need
# and weaker own-revenue capacity. Schematically:
allocate_equalization(unit):
    need_index     = function_of(population, poverty, remoteness, cost_of_services, development_gap)
    capacity_index = function_of(own_revenue_potential)   # how much the unit can raise itself
    # Greater need and LOWER capacity -> LARGER equalization grant:
    grant = base_pool * (need_index / total_need) * adjustment_for_low_capacity(capacity_index)
    return grant

# The integrity of federalism depends on this being driven by OBJECTIVE need and capacity
# data, recommended by the independent fiscal commission -- NOT by which tier or which
# local leader has the most political pull at the centre. When transfers drift toward
# political favouritism, "equalization" stops equalizing.
```

The lesson of the allocation sketch is the lesson of the whole post: a federal transfer system is only as fair as the *basis* on which it divides the money. Done well — driven by objective measures of need and capacity, as recommended by an independent commission — it channels resources to where they are most needed and brings government genuinely closer to the people. Done badly — driven by political pull, or tying so much of the money that local autonomy is hollow, or transferring funds to tiers that cannot spend them — it multiplies the cost of government without delivering the benefits. A citizen who understands the kinds of transfers, the conditional-versus-unconditional balance, and the principle that allocation should follow need rather than influence is equipped to ask the questions that test whether Nepal's fiscal federalism is working: Is the money reaching the places of greatest need? Is enough of it free for local priorities? And are the lower tiers actually able to convert it into services? Those questions, not the rhetoric of decentralisation, are how you judge the more than NPR 600 billion that flows down each year.

## How budgets deceive: decentralisation theatre

The technique of deception for this post is one that any government in a federal system can deploy, and it is especially seductive because it wraps itself in the language of empowerment: **decentralisation theatre** — the appearance of devolving power and money to the people, while the reality keeps control firmly at the centre.

Here is the move. "Decentralisation," "empowering local governments," "taking power to the people" — these are popular and virtuous-sounding goals, and a government gains political credit by appearing to advance them. So there is a temptation to *announce* large transfers of money to the lower tiers, and to *speak* the language of local empowerment, while the *structure* of those transfers quietly retains central control. The clearest mechanism, as we have seen, is the *balance between conditional and unconditional grants*: a government can transfer a large total sum downward — a figure it can trumpet as a great devolution of resources — while making most of that sum *conditional*, tied to central programmes and central rules, so that the lower tiers are not genuinely empowered to set their own priorities but are merely executing the centre's wishes with the centre's money. The headline says "power to the local level"; the structure says "the centre still decides, and the locals merely deliver." Other forms of the theatre include announcing devolved functions without the funds or the authority to perform them, or creating local bodies with grand titles but little real power. In each case the appearance of decentralisation is offered while the substance of central control is retained — and the public, hearing the empowering language and the large transfer figures, credits the government with a devolution that has not really occurred.

The defence is to look past the rhetoric and the headline transfer totals to the *structure* of the money and the power. When a government claims to be empowering local governments, ask: how much of the money flowing down is *unconditional* — genuinely free for the lower tiers to spend on their own priorities — versus *conditional*, tied to central direction? In this budget, recall, the conditional grants to local governments are more than double the unconditional ones, which means most of the money flowing to the local tier comes with central strings — a fact that sits uneasily with any claim of full local empowerment. Ask, too, whether the lower tiers have been given the *authority* and the *capacity* to match their responsibilities, or whether they have been handed duties without the power or the means to fulfil them. And ask whether the allocation of money is driven by objective need or by central political preference. A citizen who examines the structure rather than the slogans can see whether a government is genuinely dividing power and money across the tiers, or merely performing the appearance of doing so while keeping the real control at the centre. Decentralisation is a genuine and valuable goal; decentralisation theatre is its counterfeit, and telling them apart requires looking at the strings, not the sums.

## Where we go from here

We have followed the money down. You now understand what federalism is and why a country would choose it; that Nepal has three tiers of government — one federal, seven provincial, and seven hundred and fifty-three local — each a government in its own right; the fiscal imbalance that concentrates revenue at the top and responsibilities at the bottom, and the transfers that bridge it; the kinds of grants — unconditional equalization, conditional, complementary, and special — and what each does to local autonomy; revenue sharing; and the honest reckoning that federalism is genuinely expensive and genuinely beneficial, a trade-off to be weighed rather than a cause to be cheered or condemned.
