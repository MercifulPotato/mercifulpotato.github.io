## Part 1: Before We Talk About the Law, Let Us Talk About Nepal

Let's begin somewhere most explanations of financial legislation never begin: with the place itself.

If you are reading this and you already know Nepal well — its geography, its politics, its economics, its history — you may feel the early sections of this article are overly simple. That is intentional. This series is written for someone who has heard of Nepal, who perhaps knows it as "the country with Mount Everest," and who wants to understand a piece of legislation that is, in some ways, the most ambitious financial law the country has passed in a generation. To understand the law, you must first understand the country. And to understand the country, we must start from the beginning.

### What Nepal Is

Nepal is a landlocked country in South Asia. Landlocked means it has no coastline — it is entirely surrounded by other countries. To its north lies China (specifically the Tibet Autonomous Region). To its south, east, and west lies India. This geographic position is not a footnote; it is one of the most important facts about Nepal's economy. Nearly everything Nepal imports must come through India or China. Nearly everything Nepal exports must travel through one of those two countries to reach the world. When relations with either neighbor are tense, or when roads are blocked, Nepal's economy feels it almost immediately.

Nepal covers approximately 147,000 square kilometers. For comparison, that is slightly larger than the American state of Arkansas, or about the size of England and Wales combined. But within those 147,000 square kilometers lies one of the most dramatic terrain transitions on the planet. The southern strip of the country — called the Terai — is flat, fertile, and subtropical. It is where most of Nepal's agriculture happens and where a significant portion of its population lives. Moving north, the land rises through hills and middle mountain ranges, a zone of steep valleys, fast rivers, and dense settlements. Then, further north still, the land rises again to the Great Himalayan Range — the highest mountain chain on Earth, containing eight of the ten tallest peaks in the world, including Everest at 8,849 meters above sea level.

This terrain is not just scenery. It is an economic fact. Building a road in the Terai is straightforward. Building a road through the hills is expensive. Building infrastructure in the high mountains — electricity lines, water systems, tunnels, bridges — is extraordinarily expensive, often technically complex, and sometimes barely feasible with current technology. When we talk about infrastructure investment in Nepal, we are not talking about laying asphalt on flat ground. We are talking about engineering feats across wildly difficult terrain, at significant elevation, in zones prone to earthquakes, monsoon flooding, and landslides.

Nepal's population is approximately 30 million people. Demographically it is a young country — a large proportion of its population is under 30. Its capital and largest city is Kathmandu, located in a valley in the hills region, at roughly 1,400 meters elevation. The Kathmandu Valley is home to several million people and is the political, economic, and cultural center of the country. It also contains some of the worst traffic congestion, air pollution, and housing pressure of any city in South Asia — partly because its infrastructure was never designed for its current population, and partly because infrastructure investment has never kept up with urbanization.

### What Nepal's Economy Looks Like

Nepal is classified by international institutions as a lower-middle-income country. That classification comes from the World Bank, which divides the world's countries by their Gross National Income per capita — roughly speaking, the average income per person per year. As of recent years, Nepal's GNI per capita was approximately $1,200 to $1,400 per year. For context, the United States is around $76,000. India is around $2,300. China is around $12,000. Nepal is poor, but it is not the poorest country in the region — it ranks above some of its neighbors, and it has made significant progress in reducing extreme poverty over the past two decades.

The economy rests on several main pillars:

**Remittances.** This is the largest and arguably most important. A remittance is money sent home by someone working abroad. Nepal is one of the most remittance-dependent countries in the world. At times, remittances have equalled or exceeded 25 percent of the country's entire GDP (Gross Domestic Product — the total value of everything the country produces in a year). Hundreds of thousands of Nepali workers are employed in Gulf countries like Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE, as well as in Malaysia, South Korea, Japan, and beyond. They work in construction, domestic service, manufacturing, and other sectors, and they send money home to their families. This money is then spent on food, housing, education, and consumer goods. It sustains millions of households. But it also creates a structural problem: the economy is dependent on remittance income, which is vulnerable to global economic conditions and the labor policies of foreign governments, and which does not create productive assets inside Nepal itself.

**Agriculture.** A significant portion of Nepal's population — historically around two-thirds, though this is declining — is engaged in some form of agriculture. Most of it is subsistence or small-scale farming. The Terai produces significant quantities of rice, wheat, sugarcane, and maize. The hills produce tea, coffee, vegetables, and fruit. But agricultural productivity is constrained by lack of irrigation, lack of fertilizer, lack of mechanization, difficult terrain, and limited access to markets. Nepal imports more food value than it exports, despite being an agricultural country. This is a failure of infrastructure and investment, not of the land itself.

**Tourism.** Nepal's mountains attract trekkers, mountaineers, and cultural tourists. Trekking routes like the Annapurna Circuit and the path to Everest Base Camp are world-famous. Tourism generates significant foreign exchange earnings and supports a large hospitality and guiding industry. But tourism is fragile — natural disasters (Nepal suffered a devastating earthquake in 2015), political instability, and global events (the COVID-19 pandemic essentially eliminated Nepali tourism for two years) can collapse it overnight.

**Hydropower.** Nepal sits on some of the most powerful rivers in Asia. The Himalayan rivers — the Koshi, the Gandaki, the Karnali — descend rapidly from the mountains and carry enormous volumes of water and energy potential. Nepal has been estimated to have a theoretical hydropower potential of around 83,000 megawatts, with a practically exploitable potential of around 40,000 to 50,000 megawatts. As of recent years, Nepal's installed hydropower capacity was only around 2,000 to 3,000 megawatts — a tiny fraction of what is possible. The government has set a target, referenced explicitly in the legislation we will be studying, of 40,000 megawatts. This is an enormous aspiration that would require investment on a scale Nepal has never before attempted.

### What Development Means, and Why It Requires Money

The word "development" is used so often in discussions about countries like Nepal that it can lose all meaning. Let us be precise about what it means here.

Development, in the economic sense, refers to the process by which a country's economy grows more productive, more complex, and more capable of generating rising standards of living for its people. Development is not simply about GDP going up. It is about building the physical and institutional infrastructure that makes economic activity possible: roads, bridges, tunnels, airports, electricity grids, water systems, sewage systems, schools, hospitals, telecommunications networks, financial systems. It is also about building the human capital — the education, skills, health — of the population. And it is about building the institutional capital — trustworthy laws, reliable courts, transparent government, effective regulatory systems — that allows economic activity to happen at scale.

Every single one of these things requires money. Not just money to build them once, but money to maintain, operate, and expand them over time.

In a wealthy country, this money comes from multiple sources: government tax revenue, private investment by companies seeking profit, loans from banks and financial markets, and sometimes international aid or investment. The wealth of the country itself makes each of these sources relatively available. Tax revenues are large because incomes are high. Private investment is plentiful because there are profits to be made. Banks and capital markets are deep and sophisticated.

In a lower-income country like Nepal, all of these sources are constrained. Tax revenues are limited because incomes are low. Private investment is cautious because returns are uncertain and risks are high. Banks are smaller and less sophisticated. Capital markets are thin. International aid exists but is not unlimited and often comes with conditions. The result is what economists call an infrastructure gap: the difference between the infrastructure a country needs to develop and the infrastructure it actually has the resources to build.

### Nepal's Infrastructure Gap

The infrastructure gap in Nepal is not small. It is enormous.

The explanatory memorandum at the beginning of the Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 states explicitly that various studies suggest Nepal needs more than one trillion rupees (Rs. 1 trillion, or 100 billion rupees written as 1 kharba in Nepali, is a unit worth approximately $750 million to $800 million at recent exchange rates) in infrastructure investment over the coming decade. The memorandum notes that current annual infrastructure investment in Nepal is only around Rs. 200 billion to Rs. 300 billion — meaning the country is investing only about one-fifth to one-third of what is needed each year.

Let us be concrete about what that gap looks like in practice.

Nepal's electricity system is under-developed and unreliable. Load shedding — scheduled power cuts, often for many hours per day — was a normal feature of life in Kathmandu until relatively recently. Industrial operations are hampered by unpredictable power supply. Rural areas are still not fully electrified. The hydropower potential exists, but the transmission infrastructure to move power from generation sites in the mountains to population centers in the valleys and Terai is incomplete and inadequate.

Nepal's road network is improving, but vast areas of the country remain unconnected by all-weather roads. The Kathmandu Valley has no rapid transit system adequate for its population. There is one functioning railway line, small and limited, in the Terai. The ambitious mid-hill highway connecting the east and west of the country through the hills has been under construction for decades and is still not complete.

Nepal's airports are inadequate. The main international airport in Kathmandu — Tribhuvan International Airport — has been described by aviation experts as one of the most challenging airports in the world to operate, with a single runway, surrounded by hills, operating at capacity. A new international airport at Nijgadh in the Terai, planned for years, remains unbuilt due to financing and environmental questions. The existing regional airports are small, sometimes unpaved, and dependent on weather for operations.

Water supply and sanitation infrastructure is inadequate in many parts of the country, including Kathmandu itself, which depends on a water supply system built decades ago for a much smaller population.

And behind all of this lies a financing problem. Nepal's government budget is limited. International aid — grants and concessional loans from bilateral and multilateral donors — covers some portion of infrastructure spending but is not sufficient to close the gap. And historically, Nepal has not had effective mechanisms for mobilizing private investment at scale into infrastructure, or for tapping international capital markets.

### Traditional Finance Versus Alternative Development Finance

Before we can understand what the Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 actually does, we need to understand the distinction between traditional finance and what this law calls "alternative development finance."

Traditional public finance for development relies on a relatively small set of tools. The government collects taxes. It receives foreign aid and grants. It borrows from multilateral development banks like the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank, and the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, usually at concessional (below-market) interest rates. It borrows from bilateral creditors like India, China, Japan, and others. It allocates this money through the annual budget process to fund infrastructure projects and public services.

This system has real strengths. It is transparent — at least in principle. It is accountable — at least in principle. It is subject to parliamentary oversight. But it has severe limitations. Tax revenues are constrained. Aid is declining as a percentage of GDP as Nepal becomes less "aid-eligible." Concessional borrowing has limits, and excessive borrowing creates debt sustainability risks. The annual budget process is short-termist and politically vulnerable — projects get started, run out of money, stall, resume, and stall again.

Alternative development finance is a broad concept that encompasses a much wider range of tools for mobilizing money for development. The key insight behind it is this: there is money in the world — in pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds, private equity funds, high-net-worth individuals, and ordinary investors — that is looking for investment opportunities. If development infrastructure projects can be structured in a way that offers these investors acceptable returns at acceptable risks, that money can flow into infrastructure. This is called "mobilizing private capital" or "blended finance" — using a small amount of public money or government guarantees to "crowd in" a much larger amount of private investment.

Alternative development finance instruments include:
- **Bonds** issued specifically to finance projects (project bonds), including green bonds for environmental projects
- **Equity funds** where investors buy shares in infrastructure companies or projects
- **Guarantee mechanisms** where the government or a public institution promises to cover losses up to a certain level, reducing the risk for private investors
- **Blended finance structures** where grants, concessional loans, and commercial investment are combined in a single project
- **Remittance-linked investment vehicles** that encourage the Nepali diaspora to invest their earnings back into infrastructure at home
- **Asset monetization** — converting existing public assets (like roads that generate tolls, or electricity networks) into investable instruments

The Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 is Nepal's attempt to create a legal and institutional framework for all of these instruments — to give them legal standing, to create an institution to manage them, and to build the regulatory and governance structures that would make them credible to domestic and international investors.

## Part 2: Why This Law, Why Now

### The Sixteenth Plan and the 40,000 Megawatt Target

Nepal operates on a system of five-year development plans. These are documents produced by the National Planning Commission that set out the government's development objectives, targets, and investment priorities for a five-year period. The current plan — the Sixteenth Plan — sets out an extremely ambitious long-term vision that includes generating 40,000 megawatts of electricity.

To put that number in context: as of recent years, Nepal's total installed electricity generation capacity from all sources was around 2,000 to 3,000 megawatts. The target is to increase that by roughly 13 to 20 times. This is not an incremental improvement. This is a transformation of the country's energy system on a scale that would rank Nepal among the significant hydropower producers in the world.

The investment required to achieve this is staggering. Hydropower projects in the Himalayas cost hundreds of millions to billions of dollars each. Transmission infrastructure to connect generation sites to cities and export routes costs billions more. The total investment needed for the electricity sector alone — generation, transmission, and distribution — is estimated at trillions of rupees. And that is before considering roads, airports, railways, industrial parks, smart cities, and the other infrastructure the same plan targets.

No amount of tax revenue, foreign aid, and concessional lending from multilateral banks can fill this gap. It is simply not possible. The math does not work. The only way to approach these targets is to find ways to bring private capital — domestic and foreign — into infrastructure investment at a scale that has never happened in Nepal before.

This is the core problem the law is trying to solve.

### The Political Context: Who Made This Law and Why

The bill was introduced to the Federal Parliament of Nepal as a government bill. The explanatory notes at the front of the bill are signed by Vishnu Prasad Paudel, the Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister of Nepal at the time of drafting. This is significant. A government bill introduced by the Finance Ministry represents a formal policy commitment by the executive branch of government. It is not a private member's bill or a minor regulatory amendment. It is a major piece of financial architecture legislation.

Nepal's political system is a federal democratic republic with a bicameral parliament (two chambers — a House of Representatives and a National Assembly). The constitution of Nepal, adopted in 2015, established a federal system dividing the country into seven provinces, each with its own elected government and assembly. The central government retains responsibility for major infrastructure, defense, foreign affairs, and national-level economic policy.

The political history of Nepal over the past two decades has been turbulent. The country ended a decade-long civil war (the People's War, fought between Maoist insurgents and the state) in 2006. Since then, it has undergone a political transition from a constitutional monarchy to a republic, drafted and adopted a new constitution, experimented with several different coalition governments, and experienced considerable political instability with frequent changes of government. Infrastructure projects have often suffered because of this instability — decisions are delayed, projects are stalled when governments change, and corruption (a significant and acknowledged problem in Nepal's political economy) has at times diverted funds.

The Act 2081 is labelled as being from the year 2081 in the Bikram Sambat (BS) calendar system, which is the official calendar of Nepal. To convert to the Gregorian calendar, you subtract approximately 56 to 57 years. The year 2081 BS corresponds to approximately 2024-2025 CE. The bill received Cabinet approval on the 25th day of the 12th month of BS 2081, which corresponds to approximately late March or early April of 2025 CE. It was then submitted to the Federal Parliament.

### What Motivated This Now

Several factors converged to make this the right moment for such legislation.

The first is the electricity transformation that is already underway. Nepal has been building hydropower projects at an accelerating pace over the past decade, partly with Chinese investment, partly with Indian investment, partly with multilateral bank financing, and partly with domestic private investment. Some major projects are operational or under construction. But the financing toolkit for this expansion has been limited, and the government recognized that without new instruments, the pace of expansion would remain far below what the plan targets require.

The second is the growing sophistication of Nepal's capital markets. The Nepal Stock Exchange (NEPSE) has grown significantly in recent years. Domestic investors — individuals, companies, pension funds, and insurance companies — are increasingly looking for investment opportunities beyond bank deposits and equity in listed companies. The government saw an opportunity to channel some of this domestic savings into infrastructure investment.

The third is the global context of development finance. International development institutions — the World Bank, the International Finance Corporation, the G20 Infrastructure Working Group — have spent the past decade pushing the idea of "blended finance" and "private capital mobilization" as the solution to the global infrastructure gap. Instruments like green bonds, infrastructure bonds, and guarantee mechanisms have become standard tools in the development finance toolkit globally. Nepal was behind the curve in formalizing these instruments and the institutions to manage them.

The fourth is straightforwardly political economy. A law that promises more infrastructure, more jobs, and more economic growth is popular. And there are real constituencies — domestic investors, foreign investors with interest in Nepal's hydropower, the construction sector, the financial sector — who would benefit from the structures this law creates, and who have incentives to lobby for it.

## Part 3: What the Law Actually Claims to Do

At the highest level of abstraction, the Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 does three things.

First, it defines a category of financial instruments called "alternative development finance" and specifies the legal basis for their use in Nepal. This is important because, without a legal framework, many of these instruments — infrastructure bonds, equity funds, guarantee mechanisms — either cannot be issued at all or exist in a legal grey zone that makes investors nervous.

Second, it establishes a new public institution — the Alternative Development Finance Fund (ADFF) — to manage these instruments, to invest in infrastructure projects, and to act as an intermediary between the government and the private sector in infrastructure development.

Third, it specifies a governance framework for this institution: its ownership structure, its management, its accountability mechanisms, its financial oversight, and the conditions under which it can invest, lend, and guarantee.

Each of these elements deserves — and will receive — deep examination in subsequent articles in this series. But for now, the key point is this: this law is attempting to build a new financial architecture for Nepal's infrastructure development, one that goes beyond the traditional tools of government budgets and multilateral loans, and that brings in private capital at a scale the country has never previously managed.

Whether it will succeed depends on many things: the quality of the legislation itself, the quality of the institution it creates, the broader economic and political environment in Nepal, the credibility of the governance structures, and the willingness of domestic and foreign investors to trust a new institution in a country with a difficult history of institutional reliability. This series will examine all of these questions without assuming that the law is either perfect or doomed.

## Part 4: Why Should You Care — Whoever You Are

If you are Nepali, or have family in Nepal, or are interested in Nepal, the answer is obvious. This law, if implemented well, could accelerate the infrastructure development that determines the quality of life for 30 million people. If implemented badly, it could create new avenues for corruption, new liabilities for the government, and new ways for connected insiders to benefit at the expense of ordinary citizens.

If you are a developer, financial professional, or someone interested in institutional design, this law is a fascinating case study. It is attempting to transplant financial instruments and institutional models that have been developed and refined in wealthier markets — green bonds, blended finance structures, development finance institutions — into a lower-income country context with significantly different institutional capacity, political risk, and market depth. The tensions and trade-offs involved are instructive.

If you are a student of governance and political economy, the questions this law raises — about accountability, about conflict of interest, about the relationship between public institutions and private capital, about who benefits and who bears the risk — are questions that recur in every development finance context, from infrastructure bonds in Lagos to sovereign wealth funds in the Gulf to the development banks of postwar Europe.

And if you are a citizen of any democracy, anywhere, the questions of how governments mobilize private capital for public purposes — and who bears the risk when things go wrong — are fundamental to understanding how modern economies work and how they distribute their benefits and burdens.

## Part 5: What This Series Will Cover

Over the next nineteen articles in this series, we will work through every major element of the Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 in plain English.

We will cover:
- What Nepal's parliament is, how it works, and how laws are made there
- What a development finance institution is, how they have worked in other countries (for better and worse), and what makes them succeed or fail
- The specific financial instruments the law authorizes — bonds, equity funds, guarantee mechanisms, remittance funds, and more — explained from first principles without jargon
- The governance structure of the new institution — who sits on its board, how they are chosen, what conflicts of interest rules apply, and how accountability works
- The investment rules — what kinds of projects the fund can invest in, what it cannot invest in, and the reasoning behind each restriction
- The financial architecture of the fund — its authorized capital, its paid-up capital, its reserve funds, and how dividends flow
- The accountability and audit mechanisms — annual reports, quarterly disclosures, external audits, and parliamentary oversight
- The legal protections and enforcement mechanisms — what happens when someone lies to get a loan, and how the courts are supposed to handle it
- The specific infrastructure sectors targeted — energy, roads, airports, railways, industrial parks, and more
- The international context — how this compares to similar legislation in other developing countries
- The honest assessment of risks — what can go wrong, what has gone wrong in similar contexts elsewhere, and what safeguards exist

At each step, we will try to be honest about uncertainty. This law is new. Its institution has not yet been built. Its instruments have not yet been deployed. We cannot know with certainty how it will unfold. What we can do is give you the intellectual tools to follow its implementation, to evaluate its performance, and to hold its managers accountable — whether you are a Nepali voter, an international investor, a development professional, or simply a curious reader.
