# Blood, Stars, and a Stolen Silmaril: The Wars of Beleriand, the Coming of Men, and the Tale of Beren and Lúthien

*This is Part Two of the comprehensive in-universe chronological history of Middle-earth. Part One covered the creation of the world, the Ainulindalë, the Valar and Maiar, the awakening of the Elves, and the catastrophe of the Two Trees' destruction through the rising of the Sun and Moon. We continue now in the First Age proper, as the Noldor arrive in Beleriand and the long wars against Morgoth begin.*

---

## Part 1: Beleriand — The Land and Its People Before the Noldor Came

Before the Noldor arrived in their stolen ships and their grief and their terrible Oath, Beleriand was already an old land with its own history and its own peoples, and understanding its shape is essential to understanding everything that happened within it.

Beleriand was the westernmost region of Middle-earth — a great land bordered on the west and south by the sea, on the north by the iron range of mountains called Ered Engrin, the Mountains of Iron, behind which lay Morgoth's fortress of Angband, and on the east by the Blue Mountains, the Ered Luin. The land was divided by the River Sirion, which ran from north to south in a great course and was the dominant geographical feature of the whole region: West Beleriand and East Beleriand were the two halves, loosely defined by the Sirion and its tributaries.

In the north, between the highlands called Dorthonion — Taur-nu-Fuin in later days, the Forest Under Nightshade — and the Mountains of Iron, lay the great plain of Ard-galen, which means "Green Region" and was at this time exactly what its name promised: an enormous grassland of sweeping beauty, inhabited by great herds of horses and by the Elves who loved the open plain. Later — much later — it would be renamed Anfauglith, the Gasping Dust, after Morgoth poisoned it. But in the earliest days of the First Age, it was a place of light and wind.

South of Dorthonion lay the highlands of Himring, the Hills of the Hunters, the great forests of Brethil and Doriath, and finally, in the far south, the green and sheltered land of Nan-tathren and the mouths of the Sirion where they poured into the sea. The geography matters because the Wars of Beleriand were fought across this landscape, and every river, every pass, every forest played its strategic role.

The people already in Beleriand when the Noldor arrived were primarily the Sindar — the Grey Elves, those Telerin Elves who had not made the Great Journey to Valinor but had stayed in Middle-earth under the lordship of Elwë Singollo, whom they called Elu Thingol, meaning Greycloak. Thingol and Melian dwelt in Doriath, the protected forest realm surrounded by the Girdle of Melian. His people, the Sindar, were spread across Beleriand in many settlements: Círdan the Shipwright held the Falas, the long coastland with its two great havens of Brithombar and Eglarest; the Laiquendi, the Green Elves, dwelt in Ossiriand in the east, an extraordinary land of seven rivers. Various lesser lords held other regions.

The Sindar were not the same Elves as those who had gone to Valinor. They had not received the full teaching of the Valar, had not seen the Two Trees in their light. But they were not a lesser people for that — they had developed in their own way, building their own culture, their own music, their own modes of craft and governance. The Sindarin language — which would become the dominant language of Middle-earth for most of its recorded history, spoken by Elves and eventually by Men and Dwarves alike — was their tongue, evolved from the common Elvish speech during the long ages apart from Valinor.

The Dwarves had their own presence in the region. The great Dwarf-city of Nogrod lay in the Blue Mountains, as did Belegost. They were not friendly to the Elves in any warm sense, but they were not enemies either — there was trade between the two peoples, and Thingol commissioned great works from the Dwarves, and Celebrimbor's later career as a craftsman of rings owes much to the Dwarven traditions he learned in part from Beleriand contacts. The Dwarves called the Blue Mountains Ered Luin — the same name the Elves used — and they called themselves Khazâd, and they had their own ancient histories reaching back before even the Elves' awakening, because Aulë had made the Fathers of the Dwarves before the Elves came into being, though they were given true life by Ilúvatar and slept beneath the stone until after the Elves awoke.

There had already been wars in Beleriand before the Noldor came. When Morgoth fled Valinor after the destruction of the Trees, he returned to Angband with enormous speed and immediately began to consolidate his power. The Sindar of Beleriand had no warning. He sent out armies of Orcs — the first great Orc invasions since the long silent ages — and they descended on Beleriand in a two-pronged assault. This is the First Battle of Beleriand, sometimes called the Battle Under Stars because it was fought before the rising of the Moon.

The western host of Orcs drove straight south along the coast toward the Falas. The eastern host came down through Dorthonion into East Beleriand, seeking to cross the Sirion and outflank the defenders. Thingol and the Sindarin lords fought back. The sons of Finarfin — Finrod and his kin — had not yet arrived; this was entirely a Sindarin defence. Denethor, lord of the Laiquendi in Ossiriand, was slain and his people dispersed and hid among the trees and never again fought in open battle. Círdan's havens were besieged by the western host. Only the arrival of Oromë with his horns, riding from the west — the old hunter's instinct to come when there was a great hunt — gave the Sindar enough respite to stabilise.

This First Battle set the pattern for everything that followed. The Sindar had no great fortresses. They lacked the disciplined armies and the art of war-making that the Noldor had developed over long ages in Valinor. They fought bravely but could not hold effectively against the organised assault of Morgoth's hosts. They needed allies.

And then, in the light of the new Moon and the first dawn of the Sun, the Noldor came across the sea.

## Part 2: The Arrival of the Noldor — Two Hosts, One Grief

Fëanor and his sons landed first, on the northern shore of the Firth of Drengist, near the bay of Losgar. The burning of the Telerian ships lit the sky with a great fire, visible from Beleriand. The Sindar and even Morgoth's servants saw it and wondered. Fingolfin's host, completing their terrible crossing of the Helcaraxë at that moment, also saw it — saw the fire of their stolen transport burning, understood what it meant, and continued anyway.

Fëanor's arrival was immediately aggressive. He led his host east from Drengist into the highland region called Lammoth, the Great Echo, where sounds reverberate in disturbing ways, and there he was met by a large Orc host that Morgoth had sent to oppose the Noldor's landing. The Battle of the Lammoth, also called the Second Battle, was a decisive Noldorin victory. The Orcs were broken and fled north. Fëanor pursued them with all the speed and ferocity he possessed, which was considerable — he was the most gifted Elf who ever lived, and his sons were each formidable, and the Noldorin warriors who had come with him were among the best fighters in the world.

He drove the Orcs all the way north to the plain of Ard-galen and the shores of Lake Mithrim beneath the Mountains of Mithrim. The Second Battle, also called the Dagor-nuin-Giliath — the Battle Under Stars — was the first great formal engagement of the Noldorin war against Morgoth, and the Noldor won it comprehensively. The Orc armies were routed and destroyed. But in the pursuit, Fëanor rode too far ahead.

He was surrounded by Balrogs, the most terrible of Morgoth's servants. He fought them. He was one against several, and even Fëanor, the mightiest of the Noldor, could not prevail. He was mortally wounded by Gothmog, the Lord of Balrogs, and fell. His sons found him and carried him south, but the wounds were beyond any healing that the Elves of Middle-earth could provide. Before he died, he was carried to a high place so he could look north toward Angband, and in his last clear moments he cursed Morgoth three times with the last of his strength.

Then his spirit — his fëa — left his body, and his body burned, consumed in the fire that had always been part of his nature. There was nothing left of him to bury. He was the first of the Calaquendi — the Elves of the Light, those who had been in Valinor — to die in Middle-earth.

His sons gathered and kept the Oath between them. Maedhros, the eldest, tried to negotiate with Morgoth — offering his submission in exchange for the Silmarils, or at least one. It was a desperate attempt that nobody really expected to work. Morgoth summoned Maedhros to a parley under conditions of nominally equal terms, and then betrayed those terms, as everyone had suspected he would. He captured Maedhros and hung him from the face of the cliff of Thangorodrim, above Angband, from a chain fastened to his right hand. Maedhros hung there, unable to die because he was an Elf, unable to escape, as a demonstration of what it meant to challenge Morgoth.

He hung there for years.

## Part 3: The Merging of the Hosts — Fingon's Rescue

Then Fingolfin's host arrived in Beleriand. They came from the north, from the terrible Helcaraxë, and they were changed by what they had endured. They were thinner and harder than when they had left Valinor, stripped of any lingering sentimentality, and they were angry — at Fëanor (who was dead), at Fëanor's sons, at their situation, and at Morgoth who was the cause of all of it.

Fingolfin himself was a great lord — second among the three princes of the Noldor, mightier in terms of followers than Fëanor, and a king of exceptional military and personal quality. He led his people to the shores of Lake Mithrim, where Fëanor's sons had already camped. The two hosts initially camped on opposite shores of the lake, the enmity between them sharp and unresolved. But events forced reconciliation of a sort.

The reconciliation came through Fingon, Fingolfin's eldest son and most beloved. Fingon and Maedhros had been close friends before all the disasters — a friendship that Tolkien describes with genuine warmth, the kind of friendship between great warriors that is itself a form of devotion. When Fingon heard where Maedhros was — that he had been captured and hung from Thangorodrim — he went alone, at night, to attempt a rescue.

This is one of Tolkien's great heroic episodes. Fingon climbed the mountains alone. He had no plan for getting Maedhros down from the cliff face — the chain was too strong to break, the cliff too sheer to climb to where the chain was fastened. He found Maedhros alive, after years of torment, barely coherent. Fingon had a bow. He couldn't break the chain. He offered, out of mercy, to shoot Maedhros dead rather than leave him there.

Maedhros asked him to. He was beyond endurance.

Before Fingon could loose the arrow, he prayed to Manwë — Manwë, Lord of the Valar, King of Arda, to whom Fingolfin's host had special devotion. And Manwë heard, and sent one of the great Eagles: Thorondor, the Lord of Eagles, the greatest bird that ever lived, whose wingspan was thirty fathoms. Thorondor seized Fingon and carried him to where Maedhros hung, and Fingon cut off Maedhros's hand — the right hand, with the chain through it — and Thorondor carried them both back to the camp.

Maedhros healed in time. He remained the most powerful of Fëanor's sons, and he learned to use his left hand with the sword as well as any warrior had ever used their right. The great sword-story of Túrin Turambar in the next generation is in some ways a continuation of this tradition of the great swordsmen of the Noldor.

More practically: Maedhros acknowledged that Fingolfin, as the surviving senior lord of all the Noldor, was rightfully the High King. This was significant. Fëanor had claimed the High Kingship, and his sons had inherited his claim, but Fëanor's burning of the ships had forfeited whatever moral authority the claim carried. Maedhros's acknowledgement unified the Noldor, at least formally, under Fingolfin's kingship.

## Part 4: The Glorious Battle — Dagor Aglareb and the Siege of Angband

In the first centuries of the First Age, after the arrival of the Noldor, there was a period of extraordinary achievement and beauty. The Noldor spread across Beleriand, building great fortresses and kingdoms. Fingolfin's people held the northwest, with Fingolfin himself keeping court at Hithlum, a land of hills and mists north of the Mountains of Shadow. His son Fingon was given the surrounding lands. His daughter Aredhel would later be the cause of a chain of disasters beginning with her departure to seek the sons of Fëanor.

Turgon, Fingolfin's second son, was the one who built the greatest hidden city in the history of Middle-earth — Gondolin — but we will come to that in the next article. At this stage, he dwelt at Vinyamar by the sea.

To the east, the sons of Fëanor spread in a great line — Maedhros on Himring, Maglor in the plains between, Celegorm and Curufin in the Aglon Pass, Caranthir in Thargelion east of the Blue Mountains, the twins Amrod and Amras in the south near the mouths of the Gelion. They formed a great defensive arc across the east and north.

In the centre of Beleriand, in the south, Finrod Felagund — son of Finarfin and the wisest and most beloved of all the Noldorin lords — had built his own kingdom. He established Nargothrond, the great underground fortress-city in the caverns beside the river Narog, the largest and most powerful Noldorin stronghold in Beleriand. This required the help of the Dwarves of the Blue Mountains, and to repay the debt, Finrod wore the Nauglamír — the Necklace of the Dwarves — as a badge of friendship. Nargothrond would endure for nearly four centuries before falling to Glaurung the dragon.

Meanwhile, Morgoth in Angband was not idle. He sent out probing forces periodically, testing the Noldorin defences, and the Noldor repulsed them. Then, in approximately the year 60 of the First Age — the counting is not precise, Tolkien's text gives only rough intervals — Morgoth sent out a massive army: the Orcs in numbers that dwarfed the earlier assaults, with Balrogs to lead them, advancing south into Ard-galen.

The Battle of Sudden Flame — Dagor Bragollach, one of the great catastrophes — did not happen yet. What happened in year 60 was something different: the Glorious Battle, the Dagor Aglareb, in which the Noldor demonstrated their full military power and routed Morgoth's army completely. The Orc host was caught between the forces of Fingolfin coming from Hithlum and the sons of Fëanor sweeping in from the east. It was a catastrophic defeat for Morgoth, and afterward the Noldor instituted the Siege of Angband — a formal military containment of Morgoth's fortress that would last, off and on, for nearly four hundred years.

The Siege of Angband was remarkable. The Noldor maintained a ring of watchful forces around the passes from Angband, preventing any Orc army from breaking through into Beleriand in force. Morgoth could not attack effectively without his armies, and his armies could not get through the siege lines. For four centuries, Beleriand was relatively secure.

During this long peace — the Siege Years — the Noldor built and created and loved. Nargothrond grew mighty. The hidden city of Gondolin was begun and eventually completed. Thingol's Doriath flourished under Melian's protection. The arts of the Elves reached extraordinary heights: the music of Maglor was said to be the finest heard in Middle-earth since Lúthien herself sang; the smithcraft of Curufin and his son Celebrimbor was extraordinary; Finrod's wisdom and generosity drew people from across Beleriand to his court. These were the years that could have lasted forever — and did not.

## Part 5: The Coming of Men — The Edain Enter Beleriand

In the latter half of the Siege period, something remarkable happened: Men found their way over the Blue Mountains into Beleriand.

The first Men to arrive were the people of Bëor the Old — a company of wanderers from the east who crossed the mountains into Ossiriand and were found by Finrod Felagund. Finrod came across them encamped, and he sat among them and played music on a harp he had taken, and the music was so beautiful and so full of the things they had half-dreamed of that they woke and listened in wonder. Finrod played, and as he played he learned their language from them — that is Tolkien's somewhat magical description of what might more prosaically be called an intensive period of language learning. Finrod was exceptional even among the Noldor for his gift of friendship and his ability to understand other minds.

Finrod became the great friend and champion of Men — the Elves called him Nóm, "Wisdom," and his people called him Friend of Men, and he dedicated much of his life to the service of Men and their interests. When Beren came to Nargothrond in desperate need, Finrod's friendship would cost him his life.

The Men of the House of Bëor were followed by two other great Houses of Men. The House of Haleth came into Beleriand somewhat later and settled in the forest of Brethil, a position of great strategic importance between Doriath and the River Teiglin, which they guarded with fierce devotion. The House of Hador — or the House of the Third Folk, sometimes called Marach's people — were the largest and in some ways the most vital of the three Houses, golden-haired (unusual among Tolkien's Men) and of extraordinary vigour and spirit. The greatest of them were Húrin and his son Túrin, whose story is the subject of Part 3 of this series.

Collectively these three peoples — Bëor's folk, Haleth's folk, and Hador's folk — were called the Edain by the Elves, meaning "Second People" or "Men of the West" in the sense of those Men who had come into Beleriand. They were distinguished from the other Men of Middle-earth precisely by this: they had resisted Morgoth's early corrupting influence in the east and come west seeking the Elves and the light they had heard of. They were not pure or perfect — they had their own weaknesses and pride and tragedy, as Men always do — but they were genuinely good in the way that fighting people who have chosen a harder road when an easier one was available are genuinely good.

Among the Edain there was also an anomaly: the Drúedain, sometimes called Woses or Púkel-men. They were Men, but strange in appearance and manner, possessed of unusual gifts including a sensitivity to shadow and evil that was almost Elvish. They allied with the Edain and particularly with the House of Haleth. Their descendants are the Wild Men of the Drúadan Forest who appear, briefly but significantly, in The Return of the King, where Ghân-buri-Ghân leads Théoden's riders through a hidden path to the Pelennor Fields.

The relationship between Elves and Men in Beleriand was complex and often beautiful. Finrod and Bëor were the model of what it could be at its best: genuine friendship across the vast difference in nature between the two peoples, each learning from the other, each made greater by the connection. But there was also condescension from some Noldorin lords who regarded Men as short-lived and therefore somehow lesser. And there was the darkness of Morgoth's ancient corruption in the east, which had touched even some of the Edain's ancestors, and which made the relationships between eastern Men — who had not come to Beleriand — and the West a source of grief and conflict for the rest of the history of Middle-earth.

It was among the Edain of Beleriand that the great events of the next century would unfold: the breaking of the Siege, the catastrophe of the Nirnaeth Arnoediad, and the individual tragedies of Beren and Lúthien, of Húrin and Morwen, of Túrin Turambar.

## Part 6: Dagor Bragollach — The Battle of Sudden Flame

After nearly four hundred years, Morgoth found a way to break the Siege. He sent out rivers of fire from beneath the ground — a volcanic assault that preceded his armies and burned across Ard-galen in a single terrible night. The great plain was transformed from a place of grass and beauty into a smoking wasteland. This was the event that renamed it Anfauglith: the Gasping Dust, or more literally the Thirsty Sand, a place of ash and ruin where nothing would grow again in the history of Arda.

The fire swept across the Noldorin positions in the north. The companies guarding the pass of Aglon and the Highland of Dorthonion were overwhelmed. The great lords who had held these positions for centuries were killed, dispersed, or driven south. Angrod and Aegnor, the brothers of Finrod, were slain on the heights of Dorthonion, which became the haunted and darkened forest of Taur-nu-Fuin after the battle. Behind the fire came Morgoth's armies: Orcs in enormous numbers, a new kind of terror in the form of the great dragons (Glaurung, the first and father of all dragons, had appeared briefly before the Dagor Bragollach but had been driven back; now he came in his full power), and worse.

The Siege of Angband was broken. The Noldor were driven back across Beleriand in disorder. Fingolfin watched from his seat in Hithlum as the whole of the northern watch was overwhelmed, and he did what any great king in grief and rage might do — something desperate and magnificent and ultimately hopeless.

Fingolfin rode alone to the gates of Angband.

He rode on his great grey horse Rochallor, and he blew his silver horn at the gates of Morgoth's fortress, and he called Morgoth out to single combat. This was either the bravest or the most reckless act in all the Wars of Beleriand, and it was both. Morgoth came. He had to — the shame of refusing would have undermined his own authority among his servants, and whatever else Morgoth was, he was proud.

The duel between Fingolfin and Morgoth is one of Tolkien's most powerful episodes. Fingolfin was the greatest warrior among the Elves in Middle-earth at that moment — he had been fighting for four centuries, was in the full power of his immortal body, armed and armoured in the work of the best Noldorin smiths. Morgoth was the most powerful single being in Middle-earth, a Vala in corrupted form, but he had been wounded in spirit by his own evil — he was not what he had been in the Timeless Halls. He was slower than he might have been, more careful of himself, reluctant to take risks.

Even so, Morgoth was Morgoth.

Fingolfin struck him seven times. His sword Ringil, cold as winter, cut Morgoth's foot and his knee, and with each wound Morgoth roared and the mountains shook and the earth beneath cracked. Seven times Fingolfin wounded the Black Enemy. No Elf or Man had ever wounded Morgoth before, and none would again. But at the last, Morgoth pressed Fingolfin down with his shield, the shield of Grond that was called the Hammer of the Underworld, three times he drove Fingolfin to the ground, three times Fingolfin rose, and the fourth time he fell and could not rise.

Morgoth set his foot on Fingolfin's neck and broke it. And Fingolfin drove his sword into Morgoth's foot one last time, hamstringing him, so that Morgoth walked with a limp for the rest of his existence in Arda, and this wound was the most lasting hurt any Elf or Man ever dealt him.

Thorondor came, the Lord of Eagles, and carved Morgoth's face with his talons as he seized Fingolfin's body. Morgoth could not prevent it. The Eagles took Fingolfin's body to the mountains above Gondolin, and there Turgon, his son, built a cairn for him.

The High Kingship passed to Fingon, Fingolfin's eldest son, who was already one of the great heroes of the Noldor. His reign would be one of hardship and resistance, rebuilding what had been broken by the Dagor Bragollach, and it would end at the Nirnaeth Arnoediad — but that comes later.

## Part 7: The Wanderings of Húrin and Huor — and the Secret of Gondolin

In the years after the Dagor Bragollach, two young Men of the House of Hador — Húrin and his brother Huor — became lost in the wilderness and were found by Eagles and carried to Gondolin. This matters enormously because Gondolin was the most secret city in the world: its location was known to no one outside it, protected by Ulmo's guidance and Turgon's paranoid care. Turgon received them, and they dwelt in Gondolin for a year, and when they left, they swore oaths of secrecy — they would not reveal the city's location, and they did not, though the oath was eventually broken by other means.

Húrin carried the memory of Gondolin for the rest of his life. His son Tuor would later find the city and father Eärendil within it. The chain of connections between the House of Hador and Gondolin is one of the most important structural elements in the whole of the First Age's history.

Huor would die at the Nirnaeth Arnoediad, buying time for Turgon's retreat. Húrin would be captured by Morgoth and undergo one of the most terrible fates of any Man in Tolkien's world. Their story continues in Part 3.

## Part 8: Beren — A Man Walks Into Doriath

Now we come to the most important love story in Tolkien's mythology — the tale that Tolkien himself, in his letters, identified as the "kernel" of his whole mythology, the story he cared most deeply about. The tale of Beren and Lúthien. He wrote the names on his wife Edith's gravestone and his own, and she was Lúthien and he was Beren: these were not characters he had made at a distance. This was personal myth of the most intimate kind.

Beren son of Barahir was a Man of the House of Bëor — a kinsman of Finrod Felagund, who had saved his father Barahir's life during the Dagor Bragollach, and in gratitude Barahir had given Finrod his ring, and Finrod had sworn an oath of friendship to Barahir and his kin. This ring — the Ring of Barahir — became the heirloom of Beren's line and descended eventually through the generations of Númenor to Aragorn son of Arathorn, who wore it in the Third Age.

After the Dagor Bragollach, the survivors of the House of Bëor under Barahir were hunted in the Highland of Dorthonion, which had been overrun by Morgoth's forces and transformed into the haunted forest of Taur-nu-Fuin. A small outlaw band, never more than twelve, lived in hiding and fought where they could. One by one they were betrayed and killed. Barahir himself was betrayed by a man named Gorlim, whose grief for his lost wife had been turned into treachery by one of Morgoth's agents. The whole band was slain by Orcs save only Beren, who was away at the time, and who found his father's hand with the ring of Finrod still on it and knew what had happened.

Beren survived alone for years in Taur-nu-Fuin, hunted by Orcs and by the wolves of Morgoth and by creatures of shadow, living by his wits and his desperate courage. He was eventually driven south and crossed the Girdle of Melian. This crossing was impossible for anyone not invited by Melian — it was not a physical barrier but a ward of her power, and nothing that meant harm to Doriath could pass. That Beren passed was a sign: he meant no harm, and the Girdle let him through.

He came into Doriath in spring, and in the forest of Neldoreth he found Lúthien.

## Part 9: Lúthien Tinúviel — Who She Was

Lúthien Tinúviel, called the Nightingale — the most beautiful being who ever lived in Arda. This is not mere storytelling rhetoric; it is stated as fact in Tolkien's mythology. She was the daughter of Thingol and Melian — half-Elf, half-Maia — and her gifts reflected both. From Thingol she had the grace and the ancient strength of the Elves; from Melian she had powers that exceeded any mortal Elf's, powers of enchantment and creation and music that were of Ainurin quality. She was the only child of Thingol and Melian, loved and protected beyond measure, kept within Doriath and watched over with care that sometimes edged toward captivity.

She danced in the forest alone, and she sang, and the nightingales gathered to hear her and to learn from her. It is from this that the Elvish surname Tinúviel — "Daughter of Twilight," the nightingale — derives. Her given name, Lúthien, means various things in various interpretations of Tolkien's Elvish, but is generally associated with enchantment or flowering.

When Beren saw her, he was so stunned by her beauty that he could not speak. She saw him and fled into the trees, and he followed, called out to her with the name Tinúviel, and she turned, and their eyes met, and the thing that happened to Thingol when he met Melian in Nan Elmoth happened again, differently, between Elf and Man: they fell in love with each other in an absolute way that neither could resist or chose to resist.

They spent time together in the forests of Neldoreth, walking and talking and gradually coming to know each other. Their love was genuine and mutual — Tolkien is careful to make Lúthien's love for Beren as active and chosen as his for her. She was not a passive object of his devotion. She chose him.

When Thingol discovered this — discovered that his daughter, the most precious being in Doriath, the daughter of a Maia-queen, was in love with a mortal Man of no particular standing, a landless outlaw with nothing but courage and grief to his name — his response was what any deeply protective father's response might be, except that Thingol was a king and had the power to do something about it.

He summoned Beren to his presence. Beren came without weapons into the halls of Menegroth — the Thousand Caves, the great underground capital of Doriath — and stood before Thingol and Melian on their thrones. Thingol looked at this Man — this mortal, this small and short-lived creature by Elvish reckoning — and prepared to have him killed.

Melian, who could see more deeply than Thingol in such matters, cautioned him. And Lúthien's presence in the hall may also have influenced what happened next. Thingol made a challenge that he intended to be impossible: if Beren wished to have Lúthien as his bride, he would first have to bring Thingol a Silmaril from the Iron Crown of Morgoth.

It was a death sentence dressed as a quest. Everyone in the hall knew it. Thingol knew it. Beren knew it.

Beren accepted.

His acceptance speech — brief, calm, without bravado — is one of Tolkien's finest pieces of dialogue. He looked at Thingol and said that his hand was already stretched out toward a Silmaril, and that Thingol's pride had put it there. And he left.

---

## Part 10: Beren at Nargothrond — The Oath of Finrod

Beren went to Nargothrond, to Finrod Felagund, because the Ring of Barahir was his bond with Finrod and the oath of friendship Finrod had sworn to his father. He told Finrod what he needed. Finrod knew exactly what was being asked — he knew the task was essentially impossible for any ordinary means. But the oath was real and Finrod was the kind of person — the kind of Elf — who kept oaths at the cost of his life.

He called his people together to go with him. And at that moment, Celegorm and Curufin — two of the sons of Fëanor, who had taken refuge at Nargothrond after the Dagor Bragollach — rose against it. The Silmaril was the object of the Oath of Fëanor. Any attempt to take a Silmaril was, under the terms of that Oath, a matter that involved the House of Fëanor. Celegorm and Curufin argued that Finrod had no right to pursue a Silmaril and that following him in this enterprise would place Nargothrond in violation of their Oath.

It was a political argument dressed as a moral one. They wanted to prevent the quest, and they succeeded in preventing most of Nargothrond from joining it. Only ten companions remained willing to go with Finrod and Beren. Among them was Edrahil, Finrod's faithful servant. The ten were enough.

Finrod took off the crown of Nargothrond and placed it in the hands of his brother Orodreth, with instructions on how to govern until he returned. He expected to die. He accepted this.

The company of twelve — Finrod, Beren, and ten companions — set out from Nargothrond toward Angband.

---

## Part 11: Sauron's Isle — The Defeat and Capture

They came, in their journey north, to the territory of Sauron — who held at this time the island fortress of Tol Sirion, a great watchtower at the main pass into Beleriand, which the Elves had built and which Sauron had seized after the Dagor Bragollach. The island was set in the River Sirion and commanded the pass.

Finrod tried to pass the isle in disguise. He used his art to transform his company into the appearance of Orcs — a skill that was well within his power as a lord of the Calaquendi. But Sauron was one of the most skilled at seeing through such disguises. He saw through the disguise from the isle and captured the whole company.

What followed was a contest of song and power between Finrod and Sauron. Finrod sang a song of power — of opening doors and closing gates, of breaking bonds and shattering darkness. Sauron countered with songs of deception and lies, of darkness and betrayal. Finrod's contest with Sauron on Tol Sirion is the direct ancestor of all the great singing-contests in later Tolkien — including Lúthien's defeat of Sauron, which we are coming to. Finrod put every skill he possessed into it, his full power as a lord of the Noldor. It was not enough. Sauron was a Maia, and Finrod was an Elf, and though Finrod's nature was as noble as any being Tolkien ever described, the raw power difference was too great.

Finrod's disguise was broken. The company was imprisoned in Sauron's dungeons. One by one, they were taken and interrogated and killed when they would not yield their true names and purpose. Finrod's power kept them alive as long as it could — he used what remained of his strength to fight the wolf that came for Beren, and was mortally wounded doing so. He killed the wolf with his bare hands, and then died of his wounds. And so ended Finrod Felagund — the most generous, most beloved, most consistently admirable of all the Noldorin lords — killed fighting a wolf in a dungeon to protect the Man he had sworn to help.

He died as he had lived: in service to others, without self-pity, faithful to his oath.

But Lúthien had not been idle.

---

## Part 12: Lúthien Acts — The Defeat of Sauron

When Thingol discovered that Lúthien had been involved with a Man who had left for Angband on a suicide mission, he had her confined for her own protection in a house in a tall tree in the forest. This was not a prison exactly, but it was a confinement. It did not hold her.

Lúthien used the power of enchantment — her hair, which she cut and wove into a cloak and a rope with her arts — to climb down from the tree and escape. She went south, seeking Beren, seeking any help she could find. She met Celegorm and Curufin riding in the woods — these were the same sons of Fëanor who had sabotaged the quest at Nargothrond.

Celegorm and Curufin saw Lúthien and recognised her and were overcome by her beauty. Celegorm wanted her for himself — the thought that this most beautiful of all beings might be his wife was intoxicating to him, and he calculated that if he could present Thingol with the fait accompli of Lúthien at Nargothrond, the political advantages might also work in his favour. He and Curufin brought her to Nargothrond, not to help her but to confine her and use her as a political piece.

This was not the last evil that Celegorm and Curufin would do. They were the worst of Fëanor's sons in terms of their willingness to use people as means rather than ends. The Oath made them all capable of terrible things, but Celegorm and Curufin seemed to find the Oath a convenient excuse for evils they might have committed anyway.

Lúthien was confined at Nargothrond. But Huan was there too.

Huan was the great hound of Oromë, given to Celegorm as a gift in Valinor. He was not simply a dog — he was a creature of extraordinary intelligence and power, a hound of divine origin whose nature was such that no wolf or werecreature could defeat him except one: the greatest wolf that would ever exist. He was faithful to Celegorm, but Celegorm was not faithful to himself, and Huan could see that. When Lúthien came to Nargothrond and Huan saw her, he chose her. He helped her escape. He brought her back to Sauron's isle.

On Tol Sirion, Sauron still held the remaining prisoners — including Beren, who was the last one left alive, waiting for the wolf that would come for him. Lúthien and Huan came to the isle, and Sauron sent out his greatest werewolves one by one, and Huan killed them all. Then Sauron himself took wolf form — a form so vast and terrible that even Huan was in danger — and Huan and Lúthien together defeated him. Lúthien held him in an enchantment, pinned, and demanded the keys of the tower and the mastery of Tol Sirion as the price of releasing him.

Sauron yielded. He resumed his fair form and surrendered the isle and fled south, eventually finding refuge in Taur-nu-Fuin in the form of a vampire — a great bat-shape of darkness. This was a profound humiliation for the greatest of Morgoth's servants. Sauron was not destroyed; he would return, in the Second Age, more terrible than before. But in this moment, he was defeated by a Maia's daughter and a divine hound, and he knew it.

The tower fell, the dungeons opened, and Beren walked out into the light. He found Finrod's body and buried him with his own hands, and the green mound was never defiled in the long years of Beleriand's history, though all else fell.

---

## Part 13: The Quest Resumes — Toward Angband

After their reunion, Beren and Lúthien debated what to do. Beren, true to his promise to Thingol, wished to continue to Angband alone and attempt the quest without Lúthien. She refused to be left. She had already defeated Sauron; she was the one with the power necessary for what lay ahead. Beren's courage and love were undeniable, but Lúthien was the one who could actually do this.

They were joined again by Huan, who had definitively left Celegorm's service. And Lúthien used her arts to clothe herself in the skin of Draugluin, the greatest werewolf, and Beren in the skin of a similar beast, so that they appeared as great wolves — Sauron's messengers, perhaps, returning to Morgoth's fortress.

The journey through the Gates of Angband — past the great iron gates, past the guardians, down into the black depths of Morgoth's fortress — is narrated with restrained horror in the Silmarillion. They passed where lesser travellers could not, because Lúthien's enchantment and the wolf-skins held against the scrutiny of Morgoth's guards. And finally they came before Morgoth himself.

Morgoth sat on his black throne with the Iron Crown on his brow. Set in the Crown were the three Silmarils. In the hall around him were wolves and Orcs and great servants of darkness, and the air was thick with heat and the smell of metal and ash. The Black Enemy looked at the two wolves who had come into his presence unbidden, and he was suspicious — because Morgoth was always suspicious, because his own psychology of treachery made him see treachery in everything.

Then Lúthien threw off the wolf-skin and stood before him in her own form. She sang.

The song Lúthien sang in Morgoth's throne room is described in the Silmarillion with deliberate restraint, because Tolkien understood that some things are beyond description. She sang of the birth of time, of joy and grief mingled, of Valinor and the Two Trees and all the beauty that had been made in the world, and she sang in a way that was compounded of her Elvish nature and her Maia-blood and her love for Beren and her grief for the world, and it was the most powerful enchantment any mortal being ever performed in Arda.

The wolves slept. The Orcs slept. The great servants in the hall slept. And Morgoth himself — even the Black Enemy, the Vala whose will had marred the world — was drowsed, his eyes heavy, his guard down. He tried to seize Lúthien himself, attracted by her beauty and her power in the way that darknesses are always attracted to light they wish to possess. But as he reached for her, she used the last of the enchantment and he fell from his throne and slept.

The Iron Crown fell from his head and rolled across the floor.

Beren had a knife — Angrist, made by Curufin's craft and able to cut iron. He cut one Silmaril from the Iron Crown. It rested in his hand and burned — but he was worthy, or perhaps love made him worthy, and the burning was bearable. He tried to cut a second Silmaril. The knife broke. A fragment struck Morgoth's cheek and he stirred.

They ran.

At the gates of Angband, Carcharoth was waiting. Carcharoth — the Red Maw, the greatest wolf who had ever lived, bred by Morgoth from the beginning of time for exactly this purpose, fed on Elvish and Mannish flesh until he was enormous, beyond any power to stop. This was the wolf that was fated to defeat Huan, and Huan was not there — he had waited outside.

Beren held up the Silmaril against Carcharoth, thinking the holy light would repel the wolf as it repelled all evil things. Carcharoth bit off his hand. The Silmaril — and the hand — vanished into the wolf's belly.

And the wolf ran mad.

The holy light of the Silmaril burned in the wolf's belly in the same way that the Ring would burn in Gollum's hand in a later age, and Carcharoth ran howling south into Beleriand, destroying everything in his path, driven insane by the holy light he had swallowed.

The Eagles came — Thorondor himself and two great companions. They lifted Beren and Lúthien and carried them out of the north. The quest was, in a technical sense, complete: Beren had held a Silmaril in his hand, and it had been there, and Thingol's condition — "a Silmaril in your hand" — was met. The Silmaril was no longer there, but it had been there.

---

## Part 14: The Return to Doriath — The Hunt of Carcharoth

Beren and Lúthien came back to Doriath. Beren, weakened by the loss of his hand and his ordeal, lay near death for a time. Lúthien healed him. He woke. And they went before Thingol.

What happened in Thingol's court when Beren arrived again is one of the most emotionally rich scenes in the Silmarillion. Beren, missing his right hand, stood before the same king who had sent him to his death. Thingol asked where the Silmaril was, perhaps hoping the quest had failed and Beren was here to admit it. Beren raised his arm and said that his hand held a Silmaril at that moment — it was inside Carcharoth, who was inside Beleriand.

Thingol's attitude changed. He saw what had actually happened — what this Man had actually done, and what Lúthien had done alongside him. He recognised the love between them as something real and enormous, and he gave his blessing, and Beren became his son-in-law.

But Carcharoth was still ravaging Beleriand. The Hunt of Carcharoth was necessary. Thingol himself went, with Mablung and Beleg, two of the greatest Elvish hunters, and Beren and Huan. They ran the wolf to bay in the woods of Doriath, and in the great battle at the end, Carcharoth killed Beren — he sprang on him and crushed him — and Huan killed Carcharoth. And Huan died of the wounds, because Carcharoth was the wolf he was fated to die against.

The Silmaril was recovered from Carcharoth's belly, and it burned the one who opened the wolf but was borne safely by Mablung to Beren's dying hand.

Beren died in Doriath, held by Lúthien, the Silmaril in his hand. He called her by the old name — Tinúviel — and said farewell, and passed into the realm of Mandos.

---

## Part 15: The Song of Lúthien — Death Transcended

Lúthien did not accept this. She lay in Doriath and faded — not because she was physically ill but because she was literally dying of grief, her life-force going out of her. This is how Elves die when the will to live fails. And then she came before Mandos.

No one, Elf or Man, had ever come before Mandos in this way — in the body, alive, walking into the Halls of the Dead to argue with their Keeper. Mandos was the most implacable of the Valar. He did not grant requests. He kept the dead. He pronounced dooms. He did not reverse them.

Lúthien sang to him. She sang of the grief of the world, of love and loss, of the marring of Arda and the longing that ran through all things. She sang what she had felt, what Beren had been to her, what the world was without him. And Mandos wept.

Tolkien is explicit: it is said that Mandos wept, and he was not moved to pity before that time. The implication is that it was unique — that no being in the whole of Arda's history had moved Mandos to pity before. The song of Lúthien in the Halls of Mandos is the greatest song ever sung in the world, greater even than what she had sung in Morgoth's throne room, because it was entirely true and entirely from the heart and had no purpose other than to express love.

Mandos could not grant what she asked — the return of Beren. Only Ilúvatar himself had that authority. Mandos took the matter to Manwë, and Manwë took it to Ilúvatar. And Ilúvatar said yes — but at a cost.

Lúthien had two choices. She could go to Valinor, where her spirit would eventually be released from the weariness of the world and she would live there in bliss until the ending of Arda. Beren, as a Man, would not be there — Men's fate was beyond the world. She would lose him regardless. Or she could return to Middle-earth as a mortal — genuinely mortal, giving up her Elvish immortality and her Maia-portion — and live out the rest of her life with Beren on borrowed time, before they both died the death that mortals die and went wherever Ilúvatar takes Men after death.

She chose mortality. She chose Beren and the Road that goes ever on, even though that road ends.

They lived for a time in the land of Ossiriand, in a forest called Tol Galen, in a period of gentle happiness that the texts describe but do not dwell on in detail. They had a son: Dior Eluchíl, the Heir of Thingol. And after a time, Beren died the death of Men, truly and finally. And Lúthien, no longer immortal, followed him. They passed beyond the world, and their fate is not recorded in any text because it is the fate of Men: unknown, a mystery, kept by Ilúvatar.

What they left behind was the Silmaril, given to Thingol, which would eventually set off another catastrophe. And Dior, their son, half-Elf and half-Maia and half-Man in an impossible fraction — the most diverse heritage of any being in Middle-earth. And a story that would be told and retold for as long as Middle-earth existed, in every language, by every people: that a mortal Man and the most gifted daughter of the Elves had gone into Angband, had stolen a Silmaril from Morgoth himself, had defeated Sauron, had moved even the stoniest of the Valar to tears.

---

## Part 16: The Silmaril at Doriath — Thingol's Fate

The Silmaril came to Doriath, and Thingol possessed it, and he loved it. He commissioned the Dwarves of Nogrod to set it in the Nauglamír — the great necklace of dwarvish craft that Finrod Felagund had given him long before — in a mounting worthy of the gem. The Dwarves made it. And then they wanted to keep it.

The Silmaril had that effect. Its beauty was such that whoever possessed it fell somewhat under its spell, and the Dwarves who had worked on the Nauglamír and handled the Silmaril were not immune. When Thingol refused to pay them their full price, there was a quarrel, and in the quarrel the Dwarves killed Thingol — the greatest of the Grey-elves, the lord who had dwelt in Beleriand since before any other people had come there — and took the Nauglamír and fled.

They were caught. Most of them were killed. A few escaped to Nogrod with the Silmaril, and brought news of Thingol's death back to Nogrod. The Dwarves of Nogrod raised an army and invaded Doriath. The Girdle of Melian was down — Melian, in her grief at Thingol's death, had withdrawn from the world and returned to Valinor, and with her went the Girdle. Doriath was ravaged. The Silmaril was taken.

But it was recovered. Beren and Lúthien's son Dior had become king of Doriath, and he had children — among them a daughter named Elwing, who would become important beyond measure. He recovered the Silmaril in battle against the Dwarves. He wore it. And the sons of Fëanor heard about it.

The Oath of Fëanor was still in force. A Silmaril was in Doriath, held by someone who was not a son of Fëanor. They demanded it. Dior refused to yield what had been won by Beren and Lúthien at such cost. The sons of Fëanor attacked Doriath.

The Second Kinslaying was the murder of Doriath — the great realm that had survived everything Morgoth had thrown at it, protected by Melian for hundreds of years, now destroyed from within by Elves killing Elves over a Silmaril. Dior was killed. Two of his young sons were captured by the sons of Fëanor and left to die in the forest — a cruelty that even Tolkien's text describes with revulsion, and for which Maedhros later expressed grief. Elwing escaped with the Silmaril and some refugees, fleeing south to the Mouths of Sirion.

The Oath was driving the sons of Fëanor to exactly the tragedies Mandos had promised it would. They were not yet done with their crimes.

---

## Part 17: The Nirnaeth Arnoediad — Tears Unnumbered

We have been moving somewhat out of chronological order to trace the story of Beren and Lúthien to its end. Let us return to the main thread of the Wars of Beleriand, which was proceeding alongside these events.

After the Dagor Bragollach, the situation of the Noldor in Beleriand was grave but not hopeless. Fingon as High King worked to rebuild alliances and defences. And a great plan emerged: a massive coordinated attack on Angband from east and west simultaneously, intended to catch Morgoth's forces in a pincer. The architect of this plan was Maedhros, and it is called the Union of Maedhros — a genuine attempt at strategic coordination between all the free peoples of Beleriand.

The plan had strengths. The forces assembled were the largest the Noldor and Edain and Sindar had ever gathered. Maedhros commanded the eastern wing; Fingon and the sons of Fëanor anchored the west. The Dwarves of Belegost came — the only Dwarves to fight in the Wars of Beleriand — bringing their iron masks that would prove essential against dragonfire. Men of the Edain fought alongside their Elvish allies. Even the Eagles came.

The plan had weaknesses. It depended on precise coordination. It excluded Thingol's Sindar, because Thingol was angry at the Noldor for the Kinslaying at Alqualondë, which had killed Telerin Elves — cousins of his own people. The coordination was disrupted before it even began by treachery: Morgoth sent Men who had been corrupted in the east — Easterlings, Men who had never come to Beleriand and who were not Edain — to serve as agents, and their presence in the alliance poisoned its trust.

And when the battle was joined, Morgoth sent Glaurung the dragon with a host of Balrogs, and Gothmog led the vanguard, and they broke the eastern wing before the western wing had engaged. The coordination — the whole point — was destroyed. And then the western wing engaged anyway, because Fingon could not stand by while his allies were broken, and the battle became a general catastrophe.

Fingon was killed by Gothmog, Lord of Balrogs. He was the third High King of the Noldor, and his death left the kingship to his cousin Turgon, lord of Gondolin. Huor, brother of Húrin, was killed covering Turgon's retreat, pierced by a poisoned arrow through the eye, and as he died he turned to Turgon and spoke words of prophecy: that out of Gondolin would come the hope of Elves and Men when all seemed lost. Turgon fled and the gates of Gondolin closed behind him.

Húrin fought on alone. He was the mightiest warrior among Men of his age, and in the wreckage of the Nirnaeth Arnoediad he stood and fought alone while the last of his people fled, holding back the tide of Morgoth's army through sheer skill and will. He killed Orcs and trolls and wolves, and in his rage he would not stop, and he was eventually taken alive by Morgoth's servants — Morgoth had ordered that he not be killed, because Morgoth wanted to know the location of Gondolin and believed Húrin knew it.

He did. He had been there in his youth, he and Huor. He would not tell.

Húrin was brought before Morgoth. There was a conversation between them that Tolkien renders as one of his great set pieces of moral confrontation: Morgoth arguing that all resistance was futile, that everything was determined, that Húrin's courage was mere pride; Húrin arguing back that Morgoth lied and that even if Morgoth won, he had already lost something by being what he was. Húrin would not yield. Morgoth cursed him.

Morgoth set Húrin on a stone seat on the heights of Thangorodrim — a chair of torment, like Maedhros's chain in a different form — and cursed him with a doom of second sight: he would watch, through Morgoth's eyes, everything that happened to his wife and children, with Morgoth's interpretation twisted into every sight. He would see Morwen his wife endure her long years of widowhood. He would watch his son Túrin move from catastrophe to catastrophe, each disaster magnified by Morgoth's perspective into something still worse. He would be unable to help. He would be unable to look away.

He sat there for twenty-eight years.

The fate of Húrin and his children — of Morwen and Nienor and Túrin Turambar — is the subject of the next article in this series. It is perhaps the most sustained tragedy in Tolkien's work, a story so dark and so complete in its darkness that Tolkien set it apart as the Children of Húrin, a separate extended telling.

---

## Part 18: The State of Beleriand After the Nirnaeth

The battle that the Elves called Unnumbered Tears — the Nirnaeth Arnoediad — was the decisive defeat of the First Age. After it, Morgoth controlled almost all of Beleriand. Hithlum was given to the Easterlings who had served him. Dorthonion and the Highland north of Brethil were entirely under his shadow. The sons of Fëanor were scattered, their great eastern holdings overrun.

What remained:

Gondolin, hidden and secret, still stood. Its gates had never been found.

Nargothrond still stood, though it would fall within decades.

Doriath still stood, protected by Melian's Girdle.

The Havens of the Falas still stood, Círdan's great mariners holding two fortified cities on the coast.

And the forest of Brethil still held, the remnant of the House of Haleth fighting as forest guerrillas in their home territory.

But the free peoples of Beleriand were now islands in an ocean of darkness. They could not coordinate. They could not take the offensive. They could only hold, and wait, and hope for something — some miracle, some unexpected reversal of fortune — that would change what seemed like an inevitable end.

That miracle was coming. It would take the form of a half-Elf, half-Man child born in Gondolin to Tuor of the House of Hador and Idril Celebrindal, Turgon's daughter. The child's name would be Eärendil. But first, more darkness.

## Part 19: The Significance of Beren and Lúthien in the Larger History

Before we close Part Two, it is worth pausing to reflect on what the tale of Beren and Lúthien means in the larger structure of Tolkien's mythology — because it is not simply a love story, however beautiful. It is a hinge point in the history of Middle-earth, a place where several different threads of the story permanently change course.

The Silmaril recovered by Beren and Lúthien eventually came to Eärendil the mariner, son of Tuor and Idril. Eärendil wore it on his brow when he sailed to Valinor to plead for the Valar's aid against Morgoth, and it was the light of the Silmaril that allowed him to find the way that was otherwise hidden. Without the Silmaril — without Beren and Lúthien's quest — Eärendil could not have made his voyage. Without Eärendil's voyage, the Valar would not have come to fight the War of Wrath. Without the War of Wrath, Morgoth would not have been defeated, and the darkness of the First Age would have continued until Beleriand was completely consumed.

The Silmaril that Eärendil wore is still in the sky. It is the star that Tolkien's Elves call Gil-Estel, the Star of High Hope. When Sam Gamgee sees the Evening Star burning bright above Mordor in The Two Towers, he is seeing the Silmaril that Beren cut from Morgoth's crown and Lúthien wore against the darkness. When Galadriel gives Frodo the Phial of Galadriel — the light of Eärendil's star caught in water — she is giving him Lúthien's light, refracted through ages of time, to carry into the darkest place in the world.

The bloodline is also important. Lúthien and Beren's son Dior married Nimloth of Doriath, and their daughter Elwing married Eärendil. Eärendil and Elwing's sons were Elrond and Elros — the two Half-elven brothers who were given a choice between Elvish and Mannish fates. Elros chose to be mortal and became the first King of Númenor, father of all the Númenórean kings and ultimately the ancestor of Aragorn. Elrond chose to be Elvish and became the great lord of Rivendell, the counsellor of ages, the father of Arwen. 

Aragorn is thus a direct descendant of Beren and Lúthien. His love for Arwen — an Elf-woman of extraordinary beauty and wisdom who gives up her immortality for him — is in a direct line of echo from the original love story. Tolkien did not do this accidentally. The love of Mortal Man and Immortal Elf, repeated through generations, is the signal that keeps running through the history. It says something about what Ilúvatar intended for the two kinds of Children: that they were meant to love each other, that their different natures were complementary rather than opposed, and that when they mixed their fates together, something extraordinary and permanent resulted.

Arwen's acceptance of mortality for Aragorn's sake is Lúthien's choice made again, thousands of years later, with full knowledge of what it cost.

## Part 20: Character Portraits of the First Age

This article would be incomplete without a more detailed accounting of some of the supporting figures who gave the Wars of Beleriand their human — and Elvish — texture.

**Maedhros**, son of Fëanor, called Maitimo, "Well-shaped," in Quenya. He was the most noble and the most tragic of Fëanor's sons. He had his father's pride but not his father's cruelty; he had genuine strategic and political gifts; he maintained the fragile unity of the sons of Fëanor despite enormous pressures. The loss of his right hand changed him — made him, in some ways, more patient, more careful, less the prince of casual confidence and more the king of earned dignity. He knew the Oath was destroying his people. He could not renounce it. He drove himself and his brothers to crimes he did not want to commit, and he hated himself for it, and he kept going anyway. At the very end of the First Age, after the War of Wrath, he threw himself into a volcanic chasm with the Silmaril he had stolen, because the jewel burned his hand — the hands of the Fëanoreans could no longer hold the holy things their Oath had been sworn to recover. He could not live with the loss; he could not hold what he had sworn for; he chose destruction. It is the Oath's final price.

**Maglor**, second son of Fëanor, called Kanafinwë, "Commander by his Voice." He was the greatest singer among the Noldor after Lúthien — the Elves said that his lament for the Two Trees was the most beautiful music ever made in Middle-earth, and they called it the greatest of laments. He was also the one who, with Maedhros, carried out the Third Kinslaying at the Havens of Sirion. He could not justify what he did. He knew it was wrong. He did it anyway because the Oath. Afterward — when Elwing had escaped with the Silmaril and her children were found — Maglor took Elrond and Elros, the sons of Eärendil, as foster children. He raised them with genuine care and love. It was not enough to balance the scales, but it was real. When he too could not hold the Silmaril at the end of the Age, he threw it into the sea and walked along the shore singing, and the Elves who heard him said his lament was heard for ages afterward on certain nights when the sea wind came right. He is never said to have died. He may still be walking somewhere.

**Beleg Cúthalion**, the greatest bowman of the Elves, captain of Thingol's marches. He was Túrin Turambar's closest friend and companion, and his death — killed accidentally by Túrin's hand in a moment of confusion in the dark — was one of the most wasteful and heartbreaking of the First Age's many tragedies. He will be central to Part Three.

**Círdan the Shipwright** was perhaps the oldest Elf in Beleriand and possibly the oldest Elf in Middle-earth — he was among those who had set out on the Great Journey with the Teleri but had stayed on the western shore, watching for a sign that never came, and became the greatest mariner of the Elves. He held the Falas through every disaster of the First Age, retreating only when it became impossible to hold, eventually taking his people to the Isle of Balar where they waited out the darkest years. He survived the First Age, the Second Age, the Third Age. He was the keeper of Narya, the ring of fire, one of the Three Rings of the Elves, and he gave it to Gandalf when Gandalf arrived in Middle-earth in the Third Age. He is one of the few beings in Tolkien's world whose wisdom is unimpeachable and whose service never wavered.

## Conclusion to Part Two

The Wars of Beleriand are a sustained tragedy — a story of extraordinary people doing extraordinary things in circumstances that make success essentially impossible, and finding meaning and beauty and love in the doing of them anyway. The darkness of Morgoth is real; his victories are real; the suffering is real. But so is the light. Finrod Felagund and his death in the dungeon. Beren, missing his hand, telling Thingol that a Silmaril was in his grasp. Lúthien in Mandos's halls, singing the most beautiful and most grief-laden song in the history of the world. Fingolfin, on horseback, at the gates of Angband.

These are not simply exciting plot events. They are Tolkien's argument about the nature of courage and love in a world where evil is real and powerful and where the odds are almost always against the good. You act anyway. You love anyway. You sing anyway. Because the song is real, even if everything else burns.

The Third Age will have its own version of this argument, repeated with hobbits instead of Elves and Men, on a smaller scale but no less real in stakes. Everything that happens in The Lord of the Rings is, in some sense, a consequence and continuation of what happened in the First Age. The Ring is Sauron's One Ring — the craft of an Aulëan Maia expressed as total domination. The Elves who remain in Middle-earth in the Third Age are the survivors and descendants of the Beleriand exiles. Aragorn's claim to the throne of Gondor is built on the bloodline of Beren and Lúthien. Galadriel was in Beleriand; she remembers.

It is one story.
