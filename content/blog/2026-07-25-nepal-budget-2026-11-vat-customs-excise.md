In the last post we examined income tax — the tax you can see, the one with a form and a filing and a visible bite out of a salary. In this post we turn to the taxes you cannot see: the ones built silently into the price of nearly everything you buy, paid without a form, without a filing, often without a moment's awareness that you are paying tax at all. These are the **consumption taxes** — value-added tax, customs duty, and excise duty — and they are, collectively, the *largest* source of the government's revenue, far larger than income tax. The taxes you never notice are the ones that fund most of the state.

This matters enormously, and not only because of their size. Consumption taxes have a property that income tax does not, a property that determines who really bears the cost of government: they fall on rich and poor *alike* when they spend, which, as we will see, means they fall *harder* on the poor as a share of income. So the question of how much of Nepal's revenue comes from consumption taxes versus income tax is, at bottom, a question about who pays for the government — and this budget, which cut income tax (benefiting the middle and the wealthy) while leaning on consumption taxes (which burden everyone), may have quietly shifted that answer. By the end of this post you will understand how each of these invisible taxes works, what this budget changed, the new measures it introduced, the new "Green Tax" it created, and — crucially — why consumption taxes are *regressive*, and what that word means for the fairness of the whole system.

## What is a consumption tax?

Begin with the basic distinction we drew in the last post. An income tax is levied on what you *earn*. A **consumption tax** is levied on what you *spend* — it is built into the price of goods and services, so that you pay it as part of the purchase, automatically, every time you buy. The defining feature of a consumption tax is that it is *triggered by spending*, not by earning, which means it is paid by anyone who buys the taxed good, regardless of their income, their wealth, or whether they pay any income tax at all. This is why consumption taxes are so productive for a government and so invisible to citizens: nearly everyone spends, so nearly everyone pays, and because the tax is embedded in the price, most people never see it as a tax at all — they just see a price. A great share of the government's revenue is collected this way, quietly, a few rupees at a time, from every transaction in the economy.

Nepal relies on three main consumption taxes, and we will take them in turn: value-added tax, customs duty, and excise duty. Together they form the bulk of the government's revenue, and this budget makes significant changes to all three.

## Value-added tax (VAT)

The most important consumption tax, in Nepal and in most countries, is **value-added tax**, or VAT. Nepal's standard VAT rate is **13 percent**. Let us understand what it is and how it works, because it is more clever — and more pervasive — than most people realise.

VAT is a tax on the sale of goods and services, charged as a percentage of the price. When you buy most things in Nepal — from a shop, a restaurant, a service provider — 13 percent of the price is VAT, which the seller is legally required to collect from you and pass on to the government. You, the buyer, ultimately bear it; the seller merely collects it on the government's behalf. (Some essential goods may be exempt or zero-rated, and the precise list should be taken from the VAT Act and the budget; the standard rate on most things is 13 percent.) That is the simple version: a 13 percent tax on what you buy.

The "value-added" part refers to the clever mechanism by which it is collected along the chain of production, and it is worth understanding because it explains why VAT is so hard to evade and so productive. Goods pass through many hands before reaching you — a raw material becomes a component, the component becomes a product, the product passes through a wholesaler to a retailer to you. At each step, value is added, and VAT is designed to tax only the value *added* at each step, with each business along the chain charging VAT on its sales but able to reclaim the VAT it paid on its purchases, so that the tax is not charged repeatedly on the same value. The net effect, after the whole chain, is that the full 13 percent is borne by the final consumer — you — while being collected in pieces along the way. The genius of this design, from the government's point of view, is that each business in the chain has an incentive to demand proper VAT invoices from its suppliers (so it can reclaim its own VAT), which creates a paper trail that makes the tax self-policing and hard to evade. Hold this "paper trail" idea, because two of this budget's new measures — the digital-payment refund and the VAT-bill lottery — are precisely attempts to strengthen it, by giving *consumers*, too, a reason to demand the invoices that bring transactions into the taxed, recorded economy.

A crucial point about *where* Nepal's VAT is collected, which connects to the revenue fragility we discussed in Part 7: because Nepal imports so much of what it consumes, a very large share of its VAT is collected *at the border, on imported goods*, as they enter the country. So Nepal's VAT revenue, like its customs revenue, rises and falls with imports — and imports, recall, rise and fall with the remittance-fuelled consumption of Nepali families. This is part of why the whole revenue base is so import-dependent and so exposed to forces abroad. VAT may be a tax on Nepali consumption, but a great deal of it is collected as Nepali consumption crosses the border in the form of imports.

## Customs duty

The second consumption tax is **customs duty** — also called tariffs — which is tax charged on goods as they are *imported* into the country, at the border, calculated as a percentage of the value of the goods. Customs duty does two jobs at once, and the tension between them is at the heart of what this budget changed. Its first job is *revenue*: because Nepal imports so much, taxing those imports at the border is a major and reliable source of government money. Its second job is *protection*: by making imported goods more expensive, customs duties shield domestic producers from foreign competition, giving local industries a price advantage in their own market. This protective function is why customs duties are often set at different rates for different kinds of goods — typically higher on finished products (to protect domestic manufacturers of those products) and lower on raw materials and machinery (which domestic manufacturers need to buy in order to produce). The structure of customs rates thus encodes a whole industrial policy, deciding which industries are protected and which inputs are made cheap.

This budget makes two significant changes to customs. The first is *simplification*: it reduces the number of customs duty *tiers* — the distinct rate bands — from eleven to seven. A tariff system with many bands is complex, hard to administer, prone to disputes over which band a particular good falls into, and vulnerable to the kind of manipulation where goods are misclassified into a lower band to dodge duty. Collapsing eleven bands into seven is a genuine simplification that, in principle, makes the system easier to administer, harder to game, and more transparent — a real and defensible reform. The second change is a *reduction in duty on raw materials*: the budget cuts customs duty on 273 categories of industrial raw materials, with the stated principle that the duty on raw materials should remain at least one band below the duty on the corresponding finished goods. The logic here is industrial: by making the inputs that domestic manufacturers need cheaper, while keeping finished imports more expensive, the policy aims to give domestic industry a cost advantage — cheaper inputs, protected outputs — and thereby encourage local production rather than the mere importing of finished goods. This is a coherent pro-industry strategy, and it has real merit. But, as always, there is another side: lowering customs duties reduces customs *revenue*, in a budget already running a large deficit; and the protective structure, while it may help domestic producers, also keeps prices higher for consumers than free competition would, a hidden cost borne by everyone who buys the protected goods. The simplification is good; the trade-offs between revenue, protection, and consumer prices are real and worth seeing.

## Excise duty

The third consumption tax is **excise duty** — a tax levied on the production or import of *particular* goods, typically those the government wishes to tax heavily for revenue or to discourage for reasons of health or policy. The classic targets of excise are alcohol, tobacco, and vehicles — goods whose consumption governments often wish to discourage (for health or environmental reasons) and which also, conveniently, generate substantial revenue because people buy them anyway. Excise is thus partly a "sin tax," discouraging consumption of harmful goods, and partly simply a heavy levy on goods that are easy to tax. Unlike VAT, which applies broadly to most goods and services, excise is *selective* — it applies only to the specific goods the law designates.

This budget makes a notable change: it *abolishes* excise duty on 360 goods. The stated rationale is presumably simplification and the reduction of the tax burden on a range of products — removing excise from many goods reduces their cost and simplifies the tax system. This is, on its face, relief, and it may reduce prices on the affected goods and ease the administrative burden. But again the trade-offs deserve scrutiny: abolishing excise on 360 goods reduces excise *revenue*, adding to the same fiscal pressure as the income-tax and customs cuts; and to the extent that any of the goods losing their excise were goods that excise was meant to *discourage* (on health or environmental grounds), abolishing the excise removes that discouragement, which may or may not be desirable depending on what the goods are. The precise list of the 360 goods matters here, and should be examined, because abolishing excise on, say, industrial inputs is a different matter from abolishing it on goods whose consumption carries social costs. As reported, the abolition is a broad reduction in the excise net; its wisdom depends on exactly which goods left the net and why.

## The new VAT measures

Beyond changing the rates and structures of the existing taxes, this budget introduces several *new* consumption-tax measures, and they are interesting because they reveal the government's strategy of using the tax system to change behaviour — to formalise the economy, to nudge people toward recorded transactions, and to raise revenue in new ways. Let us take them in turn, weighing each.

The first is a **10 percent instant VAT refund for digital payments**. The idea is that when a consumer pays for a purchase digitally — by electronic means rather than cash — they receive an instant refund of a portion of the VAT, on the order of 10 percent of the VAT paid. Why would a government give money back like this? Because it serves the formalisation goal we discussed: cash transactions are invisible and easy to keep off the books, escaping VAT entirely, whereas digital payments are recorded and traceable. By rewarding digital payment, the government encourages consumers to pay in ways that create a record, which pulls transactions into the formal, taxed economy and makes evasion harder — the consumer's small reward is the government's lever for visibility. This is a clever and broadly sensible measure: it costs some revenue in refunds but may gain more by bringing previously-untaxed cash transactions into the net, while also advancing the broader goal of digitising the economy. Its success depends on the refund being large enough to change behaviour and on the digital-payment infrastructure being widely available — and the benefit naturally flows more to those who already use digital payments, who tend not to be the poorest.

The second is a **5 percent VAT on electricity consumption above 50 units**. Here is a measure with a genuine contradiction at its heart, and it deserves careful examination. The measure applies a 5 percent charge (a reduced VAT rate, below the standard 13 percent) to electricity consumption *above* 50 units, leaving the first 50 units — the basic consumption of the smallest, poorest users — untaxed. The design protects the smallest consumers, which is sensible and progressive as far as it goes. But step back and notice the contradiction: Nepal's whole development strategy, as we will see in the post on energy, rests heavily on *expanding* electricity use — electrifying homes, powering industry, substituting clean domestic hydropower for imported fuel. Electricity consumption is something the country is, in general, trying to *encourage*. And yet here the budget *taxes* electricity consumption above a threshold — which, by making electricity more expensive, *discourages* the very thing the broader strategy aims to promote. This is the tension: a measure that raises revenue and is designed to spare the poorest, but that sits awkwardly against the national goal of expanding electricity use, taxing consumption the country elsewhere says it wants to grow. Whether the revenue and the protection of small users justify the disincentive to electricity use is a real question, and the contradiction between taxing electricity and promoting electrification is exactly the kind of internal inconsistency a careful reader looks for in a budget.

The third is a **5 percent VAT on ride-hailing services** — the app-based transport services that have grown rapidly in Nepal's cities. This extends the tax net to a newer part of the economy that may previously have been lightly taxed or untaxed, bringing it into line with other services and raising revenue from a growing sector. It is part of the general formalisation push — taxing new economic activity that the formal system had not yet captured — and is defensible on grounds of fairness (why should app-based transport be untaxed when other services are taxed?) and revenue, though it will raise costs for the users of these services.

The fourth is a **universal VAT-bill lottery**. This is perhaps the most ingenious of the measures, and it directly targets the "paper trail" mechanism we discussed. The problem VAT faces is that *consumers* have little incentive to demand a proper VAT bill (an invoice) for their purchases — and without the consumer demanding a bill, the seller may not record the sale, keeping it off the books and escaping VAT. The lottery solves this by turning every VAT bill into a *lottery ticket*: consumers who obtain proper VAT bills for their purchases are entered into a lottery with prizes. Suddenly the consumer has a reason to demand the bill — the chance to win — and every demanded bill forces the transaction to be recorded, pulling it into the taxed economy. It is a low-cost, behaviourally clever way to enlist millions of consumers as enforcers of the VAT system, strengthening the paper trail from the consumer end. Many countries have used such lotteries with real success in improving compliance. It is, on its face, one of the smarter formalisation measures in the budget.

Taken together, these four new VAT measures reveal a coherent strategy: use the tax system not just to raise revenue but to *formalise* the economy — to reward recorded transactions, enlist consumers in enforcement, and bring cash and new sectors into the net. This strategy is genuinely sound in principle, and it is the most credible part of the government's answer to the puzzle from Part 7 (how to collect more revenue while cutting rates): broaden the base by formalising activity that previously escaped tax. Whether the measures deliver enough new revenue to offset the rate cuts, however, is uncertain, because formalisation is slow and the gains are hard to predict — so the strategy is promising but unproven, and a citizen should watch whether the formalisation revenue actually materialises.

## The Green Tax

This budget also introduces a new levy it calls the **Green Tax**, and it is worth examining closely, because the name does a great deal of work and a skeptical citizen should ask whether the work it does is honest. According to the budget, the Green Tax *consolidates* two existing levies collected at customs — an infrastructure development tax (historically a levy on imported petroleum products) and a road maintenance fee — into a single, renamed charge. Let us be precise about what this is and is not.

What it *is*, in substance, appears to be largely a *consolidation and rebranding* of existing levies — taking charges that already existed (on fuel and for road maintenance) and combining them under a new name. Consolidation can be a genuine simplification: fewer separate levies, collected together, is administratively cleaner. And to the extent that the consolidated tax falls on petroleum products, there is a *coherent environmental logic* available — taxing fossil fuels does discourage their use and is a standard tool of climate policy, so a tax on fuel can legitimately be called "green" in the sense that it raises the cost of carbon-emitting consumption. So the name is not entirely without justification: a levy on petroleum does have an environmental dimension.

But here is where skepticism is warranted, and it is the deception we will formalise at the end of this post. Calling a tax "Green" invokes the moral authority of environmental responsibility — it suggests the government is taxing pollution for the planet's sake, a virtuous act. Yet if the Green Tax is, in substance, mostly a *renaming and consolidation of existing fuel and road levies* that were already being collected for revenue and road maintenance, then the "green" label may be doing more *marketing* than *environmental* work — dressing a continuation of existing revenue collection in the fashionable clothing of climate policy. The questions a careful citizen should ask are: Is the Green Tax actually *higher* than the levies it replaces, or merely a consolidation at a similar level — because a genuine green tax intended to discourage emissions would presumably set the rate to reflect environmental harm, not merely continue existing collection? Is the revenue it raises actually *directed* to environmental ends, or does it flow into general revenue and road maintenance as before? And does the consolidation change anything about the environmental incentive, or merely the name on the charge? If the answers reveal that the Green Tax is substantially the old levies under a new, virtuous-sounding name, with the revenue going where it always went, then the "green" branding is largely cosmetic — a revenue measure wearing an environmental costume. This is not to say the tax is bad; taxing fuel is defensible. It is to say that the *name* claims an environmental virtue that the *substance* may not fully support, and that a citizen should judge the tax by what it actually does, not by the reassuring adjective attached to it.

## Why consumption taxes are regressive — and why it matters

We come now to the most important idea in this post, the one that determines who really bears the cost of government, and it requires a word we must define carefully: **regressive**.

Recall from the income-tax post that a *progressive* tax takes a *larger share* of income from those who earn more — the rich pay a higher *proportion*. A **regressive** tax is the opposite: it takes a *larger share* of income from those who earn *less* — the poor pay a higher *proportion*, even if the rich pay more in absolute rupees. And here is the crucial fact: *consumption taxes are inherently regressive.* Understanding why is essential.

The reason lies in how spending relates to income across rich and poor. A poor person spends nearly *all* of their income, because they must — every rupee goes to food, fuel, transport, and the necessities of survival, with little or nothing left to save. A rich person spends only a *portion* of their income, because they have far more than they need for necessities, and the rest they save or invest. Now apply a consumption tax, which is paid only on what is spent. The poor person, spending all their income, pays the consumption tax on essentially *all* of it — so the tax consumes a large share of their total income. The rich person, spending only a portion of their income, pays the consumption tax only on that portion — so the tax consumes a *small* share of their total income, the rest of which, being saved rather than spent, escapes the consumption tax entirely. The result is that a consumption tax takes a *larger share of income from the poor than from the rich* — it is regressive — precisely because the poor spend a larger fraction of their income than the rich do. The 13 percent VAT on a sack of rice is a far heavier burden, relative to income, on the family that spends most of its income on food than on the wealthy family for whom food is a trivial fraction of a much larger income.

This is why the *balance* between consumption taxes and income tax matters so profoundly for the fairness of the whole system, and why it connects directly to the income-tax cut we examined in the last post. Income tax, being progressive, falls more heavily on the better-off. Consumption taxes, being regressive, fall more heavily, in proportion, on the poor. A tax system that relies heavily on consumption taxes and lightly on income tax is therefore a system in which the poor bear a relatively heavy share of the cost of government — and Nepal, like many developing countries, relies far more on consumption taxes than on income tax. Now recall the previous post: this budget *cut* income tax, which benefits the middle and the wealthy, while *leaning* on consumption taxes (which it has reshaped but which remain the largest revenue source, and which it has even extended in new directions). The net effect, viewed across both posts, may well be a *shift* in who bears the cost of government — lightening the load on the income-tax-paying middle and wealthy, while the regressive consumption taxes that burden the poor remain the backbone of revenue. This is the deeper, system-level question that neither tax change reveals on its own and that only emerges when you see them together: has this budget, in cutting income tax while relying on consumption taxes, quietly tilted the tax burden away from the better-off and toward the ordinary consumer? It is exactly the kind of question a citizen armed with the word "regressive" can ask, and that the comforting language of "relief" and "simplification" is designed to keep from being asked.

## A worked example: a household's monthly bill

Make the regressivity concrete by following the consumption taxes through a single household's month, because seeing the taxes pile up on ordinary spending is the best way to feel their weight.

Imagine a family of modest means. Over a month they buy groceries, much of it including 13 percent VAT (and some of it imported, having paid customs duty at the border that is built into the price). They fill a cooking-gas cylinder, whose price includes the taxes levied on it. They buy fuel for a motorcycle, whose price includes customs duty, the consolidated Green Tax, and other levies — a large share of the pump price of fuel in Nepal is tax. They pay their electricity bill, now including the new 5 percent charge on consumption above 50 units if they exceed that threshold. They take a few app-based rides, now bearing the new 5 percent VAT. They buy clothes, household goods, a phone top-up — VAT on nearly all of it, customs duty embedded in the imported items. At no point in the month did this family fill out a tax form or consciously "pay tax." Yet tax was embedded in nearly every transaction, a few rupees or many at a time, adding up over the month to a substantial sum — a meaningful fraction of everything the family spent, which, because they are of modest means and spend nearly all they earn, is a meaningful fraction of everything they *earned*.

Now imagine a wealthy household making the same kinds of purchases. They too pay VAT, customs, and the various levies on what they buy — and in absolute rupees they may pay more, because they buy more and more expensive things. But the wealthy household spends only a fraction of its income; the large remainder is saved or invested, untouched by consumption taxes. So the consumption taxes they pay, however large in rupees, are a *small* share of their total income. The same taxes that consumed a meaningful slice of the modest family's entire income consume only a small slice of the wealthy household's income, because so much of the wealthy household's income was never spent and therefore never taxed by consumption. There, in the comparison of the two monthly bills relative to the two incomes, is the regressivity made visible: the consumption taxes weigh far more heavily, in proportion to income, on the modest family than on the wealthy one. And these are the taxes that fund most of the government — which means the cost of government, to the extent it rests on consumption taxes, weighs more heavily on families like the first than on households like the second. That is the distributional reality beneath the invisible taxes, and it is exactly what the income-tax cut, benefiting the better-off while the consumption taxes endure, leaves untouched or even worsens.

## For the technically minded: the indirect taxes as data, and an invoice calculation

For readers who work with numbers, here are the budget's consumption-tax changes as a structured summary, followed by a sketch of how these taxes actually stack on a purchase — which reveals how much of a price is tax.

| Change | Tax affected | Direction | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard VAT rate (13%) | VAT | unchanged | The backbone; much collected at the border on imports |
| Customs tiers reduced 11 → 7 | Customs | simplification | Easier to administer, harder to game; reduces some revenue |
| Duty cut on 273 raw materials | Customs | reduction | Pro-industry; cheaper inputs, protected outputs; revenue cost |
| Excise abolished on 360 goods | Excise | reduction | Relief and simplification; revenue cost; check which goods |
| 10% instant VAT refund (digital) | VAT | targeted reduction | Formalisation nudge; rewards recorded payment |
| 5% VAT on electricity > 50 units | VAT (reduced rate) | new charge | Spares smallest users; contradicts electrification goal |
| 5% VAT on ride-hailing | VAT | new/extended | Brings a new sector into the net |
| Universal VAT-bill lottery | VAT (compliance) | base-broadening | Enlists consumers as enforcers; strengthens paper trail |
| Green Tax (IDT + road fee consolidated) | Customs-collected levy | consolidation/rebrand | Largely existing levies renamed; "green" label questionable |

Now the invoice calculation, showing how taxes stack on an imported good and how much of the final price is tax:

```text
# How an imported good's price builds up, layer by layer.
import_value      = 1000.0     # NPR, the value of the good at the border (illustrative)
customs_rate      = 0.10       # customs duty (depends on the good's tariff band)
excise_rate       = 0.00       # excise (0 if among the 360 abolished, else its rate)
vat_rate          = 0.13       # standard VAT 13%

# Customs duty is charged on the import value:
after_customs = import_value * (1 + customs_rate)            # 1100.0
# Excise (if any) is typically charged on the value-plus-customs:
after_excise  = after_customs * (1 + excise_rate)            # 1100.0 (excise abolished here)
# VAT is charged on the value INCLUDING the prior duties -- tax on tax:
final_price   = after_excise * (1 + vat_rate)               # 1243.0

tax_in_price  = final_price - import_value                   # 243.0
tax_share     = tax_in_price / final_price                  # ~0.196 -> ~20% of the price is tax

# Note: VAT applied AFTER customs means the consumer pays VAT on the customs duty too --
# a "tax on tax" that raises the effective burden. And ALL of it -- ~20% here -- is paid by
# the final consumer, regardless of their income: the regressive heart of consumption taxes.
```

The calculation reveals two things this post has stressed. First, *how much of a price is tax* — in this illustrative case about a fifth of the final price, and for heavily-taxed goods like fuel or vehicles far more — all of it invisible to the consumer, who sees only a price. Second, the *tax-on-tax* effect, where VAT is charged on the price that already includes customs duty, quietly raising the effective burden. And underlying both is the regressive truth: this entire embedded tax burden is paid by the final consumer in proportion to what they *spend*, which means in heavier proportion by those who must spend most of their income. A developer or auditor who builds up prices this way sees exactly how the state collects most of its revenue — silently, transaction by transaction, from everyone who buys, weighing hardest on those who can least afford it.

## How budgets deceive: simplification that shifts the burden, and green labels on revenue

This post offers two related techniques of deception, because the consumption-tax changes display both: **"simplification" that quietly shifts the burden**, and **virtuous labels on ordinary revenue measures**.

The first: *simplification as cover for a burden shift.* "Simplifying the tax system" is an unambiguously good-sounding goal — who could object to making taxes simpler? So a government gains easy credit by framing tax changes as simplification. But the word can serve as cover for changes whose real effect is to shift *who bears the tax burden*. Cutting income tax (which the better-off pay) while relying on or extending consumption taxes (which everyone pays, and the poor pay hardest in proportion) can be presented as a package of "relief" and "simplification" — and each piece may indeed simplify something — while the *net* effect is a regressive shift of the burden from the wealthier income-tax-payers toward the ordinary consumer. The deception is not in any single change but in the *framing of the whole as benign simplification*, which discourages anyone from asking the distributional question: after all these "simplifications," who pays more and who pays less, as a share of their income? The defence is to refuse to evaluate tax changes one comforting piece at a time, and instead to ask of the *whole package*: taking the income-tax cuts and the consumption-tax measures together, has the burden shifted toward or away from the poor? Simplification that leaves the distribution alone is genuinely good; "simplification" that quietly shifts the burden onto those least able to bear it is regressive policy in reassuring clothing, and only looking at the whole package reveals which it is.

The second: *virtuous labels on ordinary measures*, of which the "Green Tax" is the textbook case. Attaching a morally attractive name — "green," "development," "welfare," "reform" — to a tax or a spending measure borrows the moral authority of the named virtue, discouraging scrutiny and inviting approval. A "Green Tax" sounds like the government taxing pollution to save the planet; who could object? But if the substance is largely a consolidation and rebranding of existing fuel and road levies, collected and spent much as before, then the "green" label is doing marketing rather than environmental work — the virtue is in the name, not necessarily in the deed. The defence is to *judge every measure by its substance, not its label*, and specifically to ask, of any virtuously-named tax or programme: what does it actually do, is it different from what came before, where does its money actually go, and would it deserve its virtuous name if the name were stripped away and you saw only the substance? A genuine green tax set to discourage emissions, with revenue directed to environmental ends, earns its name; a renamed fuel levy whose revenue flows into general spending does not, however green its title. The same test applies to anything labelled "development," "welfare," or "reform": the label is a claim, and the substance is the truth, and a citizen who judges by substance cannot be swayed by an adjective.

## Where we go from here

We have illuminated the invisible taxes. You now know what a consumption tax is and why it is so productive and so unseen; how VAT works, with its 13 percent rate and its clever value-added paper-trail mechanism, much of it collected at the border on imports; how customs duty serves both revenue and protection, and what the simplification to seven tiers and the cuts on 273 raw materials do; how excise targets particular goods, and what the abolition on 360 goods means; the new measures — the digital-payment refund, the electricity VAT with its contradiction, the ride-hailing VAT, and the ingenious VAT-bill lottery — and the formalisation strategy behind them; the Green Tax and the questions its label invites; and, most importantly, why consumption taxes are *regressive*, falling hardest in proportion on the poor, and how that connects to the income-tax cut to raise the system-level question of whether the budget has shifted the tax burden toward ordinary consumers. You have met two deceptions — simplification that shifts the burden, and virtuous labels on ordinary measures — and the discipline of judging whole packages and actual substance.

We have now examined how this budget taxes individuals (income tax) and consumption (VAT, customs, excise). In Part 12 we turn to how it taxes and treats *businesses and investment* — corporate tax rates, the incentives for exporters, the reforms to company and insolvency law, the promises to foreign investors and a one-stop investment system — and we will ask the recurring Nepali question of whether a budget full of business-friendly reforms will translate into actual investment, or whether it is another round of reform-by-announcement against binding constraints that no budget can wish away. The consumption taxes are understood. Now we turn to business.

## A Note on Numbers and Assumptions

All US dollar figures use our single assumed rate of **1 US dollar = 150 Nepalese rupees**, an approximation close to the Nepal Rastra Bank reference rate in mid-2026; the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee at 1.60 and drifts daily, so treat dollar figures as intuition, not accounting. All rupee figures are nominal unless stated otherwise.

The tax changes described — the 13 percent standard VAT, the reduction of customs tiers from 11 to 7, the duty cuts on 273 raw materials, the abolition of excise on 360 goods, the 10 percent digital-payment VAT refund, the 5 percent VAT on electricity above 50 units, the 5 percent VAT on ride-hailing, the VAT-bill lottery, and the Green Tax consolidating the infrastructure development tax and road maintenance fee — are from the Finance Bill 2083 and should be confirmed against the Finance Act, the VAT and Excise Acts, the Customs Tariff, and IRD circulars; the precise rates, exemptions, and lists of affected goods should be taken from those sources. The invoice calculation uses illustrative round numbers to show the mechanism, not actual rates for any specific good. The characterisation of the Green Tax as largely a consolidation of existing levies, and the questions about its "green" label, are based on its reported structure and should be tested against the detail of where its revenue is directed.

And the assumption beneath the whole series: the most important effects of tax changes are distributional, and the most important deceptions are framings — "simplification," "relief," "green" — that discourage the distributional question. Consumption taxes are regressive; they fund most of the government; and whether a budget that cuts income tax while leaning on them has shifted the burden onto ordinary consumers is a question you can now ask, and that the comforting labels are designed to keep you from asking. Judge the whole package, and judge by substance, not by adjective.
