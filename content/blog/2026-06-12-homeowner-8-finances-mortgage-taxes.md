## Part 1: Money Is the Other Half of the Job

Every post in this series so far has been about the physical house — its systems, its maintenance, its exterior. But your relationship with your home is equally financial. The mortgage is probably the largest debt obligation you will ever have. The property taxes are a bill that never goes away. The insurance is a product you pay for hoping never to use. The equity is the wealth that builds slowly, invisibly, and powerfully if you manage it well.

Most first-time homeowners understand their mortgage payment — they wrote the check at closing. But they do not fully understand how the mortgage works mathematically, what levers they have to reduce its cost, or what the tax implications of home ownership are. Many do not understand what their homeowner's insurance actually covers until they try to make a claim and discover the gap.

This post covers all of it.

## Part 2: Understanding Your Mortgage

A mortgage is a loan secured by your home. If you stop making payments, the lender has the legal right to foreclose — to take ownership of the home. Understanding how the loan is structured is foundational.

### Amortization — How Your Payment Is Split

Most residential mortgages are amortizing loans: each fixed monthly payment consists of two components — interest and principal. Over time, as the principal balance decreases, the interest portion decreases and the principal portion increases.

This relationship is highly non-linear. In the early years of a mortgage, the vast majority of each payment is interest. In the later years, the majority is principal.

**Example:** A $350,000 mortgage at a 7.0% fixed rate, 30-year term:
- Monthly payment (principal + interest): approximately $2,329
- First payment: roughly $2,042 goes to interest, only $287 goes to principal
- After 10 years (120 payments): the loan balance is approximately $306,000. You have made $279,480 in payments, of which roughly $235,000 was interest and only $44,000 reduced the principal.
- Only in year 21 does the principal portion of each payment exceed the interest portion.

This is why the 30-year mortgage is so expensive in total interest paid. At 7.0%, a $350,000 mortgage will cost approximately $488,400 in interest over 30 years — more than the original loan amount.

### How to Pay Off Your Mortgage Faster

**Extra principal payments:** Any extra money you pay toward the mortgage goes directly to principal reduction, which reduces future interest charges. Even small additional principal payments, made consistently, can dramatically reduce the loan term and total interest cost.

On the $350,000 at 7.0% example above, adding $200 per month in extra principal:
- Shortens the loan from 30 years to approximately 24 years and 7 months
- Saves approximately $93,000 in interest

Adding $500 per month extra:
- Shortens the loan to approximately 20 years and 8 months
- Saves approximately $173,000 in interest

When making extra payments, specify in writing (or using the lender's online payment system) that the extra amount is to be applied to principal — not a future payment. Many lenders, if not instructed otherwise, will apply overpayments to advance your next due date rather than reducing the principal.

**Bi-weekly payments:** Instead of 12 monthly payments per year, make a payment every two weeks (half your monthly payment each time). This results in 26 bi-weekly payments = 13 full monthly payments per year — one extra payment annually. Over a 30-year mortgage, this typically shortens the loan by 4 to 6 years and saves tens of thousands in interest.

**Refinancing:** If current interest rates are significantly lower than your mortgage rate, refinancing to a lower rate reduces your monthly payment and total interest cost. The rule of thumb is to refinance if you can reduce the rate by at least 1% and plan to stay in the home long enough to recover the closing costs through the payment savings.

Closing costs on a refinance are typically 2% to 5% of the loan amount ($7,000 to $17,500 on a $350,000 loan). If your monthly savings are $200 per month and closing costs are $8,000, your break-even is 40 months — 3 years and 4 months. If you plan to stay for more than that, refinancing makes financial sense.

**Shortening the loan term:** A 15-year mortgage has a higher monthly payment than a 30-year mortgage, but because the term is half as long and the rate is typically lower (lenders charge less for shorter-term loans because they take less risk), the total interest paid is dramatically lower. A 15-year mortgage at 6.5% on a $350,000 loan:
- Monthly payment: approximately $3,052 (compared to $2,329 on the 30-year)
- Total interest over 15 years: approximately $199,400 (compared to $488,400 over 30 years)
- Savings: approximately $289,000

That $723 more per month for 15 years saves $289,000 in interest and builds equity twice as fast.

### Fixed Rate vs. Adjustable Rate Mortgages

A **fixed-rate mortgage (FRM)** has an interest rate that does not change for the life of the loan. Your principal and interest payment is the same on month 1 and month 360. The predictability makes budget planning straightforward.

An **adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM)** has a fixed rate for an initial period (commonly 5, 7, or 10 years — hence "5/1 ARM," "7/1 ARM," etc.) and then adjusts annually based on a benchmark index (typically the Secured Overnight Financing Rate, SOFR, plus a margin). The initial rate on an ARM is typically lower than a fixed rate, but after the initial period, the rate can increase significantly.

ARMs can make sense if you are certain you will sell or refinance before the adjustment period begins. They carry risk for those who stay in the home past the adjustment date and rates have risen substantially.

### Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI)

If you made a down payment of less than 20% of the purchase price, your lender required private mortgage insurance. PMI protects the lender (not you) against default and typically costs 0.5% to 1.5% of the loan amount per year — added to your monthly payment.

PMI is not permanent. The Homeowners Protection Act (HPA) of 1998 requires lenders to automatically cancel PMI when the loan balance reaches 78% of the original purchase price (based on your payment schedule). You can request cancellation when you reach 80% of original value — with good payment history and, in some cases, with an appraisal showing sufficient equity.

If home values have risen since your purchase, you may be able to request PMI cancellation based on current appraised value rather than original purchase price — though lenders have different policies on this. Contact your servicer to understand their PMI cancellation process.

## Part 3: Property Taxes — Understanding, Managing, and Appealing

### How Property Taxes Are Calculated

Property taxes are calculated by multiplying the assessed value of your property by the local tax rate (mill rate).

**Assessed value** is determined by the local assessor's office. In most jurisdictions, the assessed value is a percentage of the market value — 100% in some places, 50% to 80% in others. The assessor uses mass appraisal methods (comparing your property to similar properties) rather than conducting individual appraisals.

**The tax rate (mill rate)** is set by the taxing jurisdictions — typically a combination of the municipality, the county, the school district, and sometimes special districts (fire, water, sanitation, etc.). One mill equals $1 of tax per $1,000 of assessed value. A property assessed at $300,000 in a jurisdiction with a total mill rate of 25 mills pays $7,500 per year in property taxes.

### When Taxes Increase

Property taxes increase when:
- The assessed value of your property increases (through a reassessment cycle or when improvements are made)
- The tax rate increases (a decision by the governing bodies in your jurisdiction)
- Both

In rapidly appreciating real estate markets, property tax increases can be dramatic. Some states have caps on how much assessed value can increase per year (California's Proposition 13 is the most well-known example, capping increases at 2% per year for existing owners). Know your state's rules.

### Property Tax Exemptions

Many jurisdictions offer property tax exemptions that reduce your tax bill:

**Homestead exemption:** The most common. Reduces the assessed value of your primary residence by a fixed amount, reducing the tax on it. Available in most states; the amount varies widely. Apply for the homestead exemption immediately after closing — there is typically a deadline (often December 31 of the year you purchase) for the exemption to apply to your first tax year.

**Senior exemptions:** Many jurisdictions offer additional reductions for homeowners above a certain age (typically 65) or with income below certain thresholds.

**Veterans and disabled veterans exemptions:** Often significant — sometimes eliminating property tax entirely for 100% disabled veterans.

**Circuit breaker programs:** State programs that cap property taxes as a percentage of income for qualifying homeowners (typically lower-income or fixed-income households).

You must typically apply for these exemptions — they do not apply automatically. Contact your local assessor's office to understand what you qualify for.

### Appealing Your Assessment

If you believe your property has been over-assessed, you can appeal the assessment and potentially reduce your tax bill. This is more common and more successful than most homeowners realize.

**When to consider an appeal:**
- Your assessed value is higher than what you paid for the house recently
- Your assessed value is higher than the sale prices of comparable properties (comparable = similar size, age, condition, and location)
- Your assessment increased significantly in a year when the market did not clearly support it
- There are errors in the property record (wrong square footage, extra bathroom recorded that does not exist, etc.)

**The appeal process:**
1. Review your assessment notice carefully when you receive it. It will specify the appeal deadline — missing the deadline typically means waiting until the next assessment cycle.
2. Request the property record card from the assessor's office. Review it for errors in the property description.
3. Research comparable sales (comparable properties that sold in the recent period covered by the assessment) using public records, Zillow, Realtor.com, or a real estate agent's comparative market analysis.
4. File a formal appeal with the local Board of Assessment Review (or equivalent) within the deadline. You typically appear at a hearing and present your evidence.
5. If the local board does not provide adequate relief, most jurisdictions allow appeal to a state board or tax court.

Property tax appeal firms work on contingency (they take a percentage of the tax savings in the first year). They can be worth it for properties with significant over-assessment but you can do this yourself for smaller amounts.

## Part 4: Homeowner's Insurance — What It Covers and What It Doesn't

Homeowner's insurance is one of the least understood financial products most people own. Most homeowners do not read their policies until they have a claim, at which point they discover the gaps.

### The Standard Policy (HO-3)

The most common homeowner's insurance policy is the HO-3 (Special Form). It provides:

**Dwelling coverage (Coverage A):** Covers damage to the physical structure of your home from "all perils except those specifically excluded." This is "open peril" or "all-risk" coverage for the structure. Exclusions are specifically listed and include the perils described below.

**Other structures coverage (Coverage B):** Typically 10% of Coverage A. Covers detached garages, fences, sheds, and other structures on the property.

**Personal property coverage (Coverage C):** Covers your belongings — furniture, clothing, electronics, appliances — but typically on a "named peril" basis (only the specific perils listed are covered). Also typically subject to sub-limits for high-value items.

**Loss of use coverage (Coverage D):** Pays for additional living expenses (hotel, meals, storage) while your home is uninhabitable due to a covered loss.

**Liability coverage (Coverage E):** Protects you against claims that someone was injured on your property or that you caused property damage to others. Typically $100,000 to $300,000 — often not enough for serious incidents. Consider an umbrella policy for additional liability protection.

**Medical payments (Coverage F):** Pays medical bills for guests injured on your property, regardless of fault. A small coverage ($1,000 to $5,000) intended for minor incidents.

### What Standard Policies Do NOT Cover

**Flooding:** Standard homeowner's insurance does not cover flood damage — water that enters the home from the ground up (surface flooding, overflow from rivers, storm surge). Flood insurance is a separate policy, available through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or private insurers. If your home is in a FEMA-designated Special Flood Hazard Area (the 100-year floodplain), your lender will require flood insurance. Even if not required, flood insurance is worth considering in many areas.

**Earthquake:** Standard policies do not cover earthquake damage. Separate earthquake insurance is available in most states, though premiums can be very high in high-risk zones (California, Pacific Northwest, parts of the South).

**Sewer backup:** Water that backs up through floor drains or toilets from the municipal sewer system or a failed private septic system is not covered by standard homeowner's insurance. A sewer backup endorsement (rider) is available from most insurers for a modest additional premium ($50 to $150 per year) and provides coverage for these events.

**Gradual damage and neglect:** Insurance covers sudden and accidental damage. A slow plumbing leak that has been dripping inside the wall for two years, causing mold and wood rot, is typically not covered — it is considered maintenance neglect. This is why regular inspections matter: catch problems early, when they are still sudden-and-accidental rather than gradual.

**Mold:** Most standard policies either exclude mold entirely or limit coverage to a small sublimit ($5,000 to $10,000) and only when the mold resulted from a covered water loss.

**Power failure:** Damage from a power outage (food spoilage, frozen pipes from a failed furnace) may not be covered or may be subject to limited coverage.

**Insects and vermin:** Termite damage, rodent damage, and other pest damage is specifically excluded. Pest control is a maintenance issue, not an insurable event.

**Normal wear and tear:** Insurance covers damage, not aging. A 25-year-old roof that is simply worn out is not insured — its replacement is the homeowner's capital expense.

### Actual Cash Value vs. Replacement Cost Coverage

This is one of the most important things to understand about your policy.

**Actual Cash Value (ACV):** Pays the depreciated value of damaged property. A 10-year-old roof that would cost $15,000 to replace might have an ACV of $6,000 (factoring in 20 years of depreciation on a 25-year roof). You would receive $6,000 and need to come up with $9,000 yourself.

**Replacement Cost Value (RCV):** Pays the cost to replace the damaged property with new material of like kind and quality, without depreciation. You receive $15,000 for the roof replacement. RCV policies are more expensive but dramatically better when you have a claim.

Verify whether your dwelling coverage is ACV or RCV. If it is ACV, consider upgrading to RCV — the premium difference is typically modest and the claim outcome difference is enormous.

### Ensuring Adequate Coverage

**Insure to rebuild, not to purchase:** Your Coverage A (dwelling) should be sufficient to cover the cost of completely rebuilding your home — not its market value, which includes land that does not burn down or flood. In areas where construction costs are rising rapidly (which is nearly everywhere since 2020), many homes are significantly underinsured. Ask your insurer to run an independent replacement cost estimate annually.

**Extended replacement cost coverage:** A rider that extends Coverage A by 20% to 50% beyond the stated limit, protecting against situations where reconstruction costs have risen since the policy was written. Given construction cost inflation, this is worth considering.

**Personal property endorsements:** Standard personal property coverage has sublimits for jewelry ($1,000 to $2,500 typically), cameras, musical instruments, bicycles, silverware, firearms, and other categories. If you have valuables in these categories, schedule them individually for their appraised value for an additional premium.

### Shopping for and Maintaining Your Policy

- Get quotes from at least three insurers when purchasing or renewing
- Review your policy annually — make sure coverage limits still reflect current replacement costs
- Report significant home improvements (additions, major renovations) to your insurer — they may require a coverage increase and you may need it
- Maintain your home — insurers can cancel or non-renew policies for properties with deferred maintenance that represents high risk (old roofs, damaged electrical systems, uncapped chimneys)
- Document your possessions: photograph and video the contents of each room and store the documentation in cloud storage or off-site. This documentation is essential for personal property claims.

## Part 5: Building and Using Home Equity

Equity is the difference between what your home is worth and what you owe on it. If your home is worth $400,000 and you owe $280,000, your equity is $120,000.

Equity builds in two ways:
1. **Principal paydown:** Every mortgage payment that reduces the loan balance increases equity
2. **Appreciation:** An increase in the market value of your home increases equity

### The Wealth-Building Case for Home Ownership

Over long periods, residential real estate has historically appreciated at roughly 3% to 4% per year nationally, though with significant regional variation and cyclical volatility. This appreciation, combined with the leverage of a mortgage (you control a $400,000 asset with, say, a $80,000 down payment), produces returns on investment that are often higher than equivalent-risk financial investments.

The forced savings aspect of home ownership is also significant: every mortgage payment forces you to save (via principal paydown). Most renters do not invest the difference between renting and owning — home ownership creates automatic, forced equity building that many owners would not achieve through voluntary savings.

### Home Equity Loans and Home Equity Lines of Credit

Once you have meaningful equity, you can borrow against it:

**Home equity loan (HEL):** A lump-sum second mortgage at a fixed interest rate and fixed term. You receive the money at closing and repay over 5 to 30 years. Interest rates are typically higher than first mortgage rates but lower than personal loans or credit cards. Useful for a specific large expense: a major renovation, college tuition, debt consolidation.

**Home equity line of credit (HELOC):** A revolving credit line secured by your home. You can draw money as needed (during the "draw period," typically 5 to 10 years), repay, and draw again, like a credit card. After the draw period, a repayment period follows (typically 10 to 20 years). Interest rates are usually variable, tied to the prime rate. More flexible than a HEL; appropriate for ongoing expenses or projects with uncertain costs.

**Critical warning:** Both HELs and HELOCs are secured by your home. Failure to repay can result in foreclosure. Do not use home equity to finance consumption (vacations, cars, lifestyle) — use it only for investments that either produce income or increase the home's value. Using your home's equity to fund a lifestyle you cannot otherwise afford puts your housing security at risk.

### The ROI of Home Improvements

Not all home improvements add equal value. Some significantly increase resale value; others are purely personal preference with little to no return. This topic is covered in detail in Post 9, but the general principle: functional improvements that bring the home to market standard (updated kitchen and bathrooms, replaced roof, new HVAC) tend to have good returns. Luxury improvements beyond what the neighborhood supports (a $100,000 kitchen in a $300,000 neighborhood) typically do not.

## Part 6: The Homeowner's Personal Finance Framework

Home ownership fundamentally changes your personal finance situation. Here is a framework for managing it well.

### The Hierarchy of Financial Priorities

For a new homeowner, prioritize in roughly this order:

1. **Emergency fund (3 to 6 months of expenses, plus a separate home emergency fund of $5,000 to $10,000):** Non-negotiable. The home emergency fund replenishes after use.

2. **Employer match on retirement accounts:** If your employer offers a 401(k) match, contribute enough to get the full match before anything else. The match is an immediate 50% to 100% return on investment.

3. **High-interest debt elimination:** Any consumer debt at 7%+ interest (credit cards, personal loans) should be paid off aggressively. The guaranteed return of eliminating 20% credit card debt is better than almost any investment.

4. **Other retirement savings (IRA, additional 401(k)):** Contributing to tax-advantaged retirement accounts — Roth IRA if eligible, traditional IRA or additional 401(k) if not.

5. **Home maintenance and capital planning fund:** Set aside the 1% to 2% of home value annually for maintenance and capital replacements. Keep this separate from your emergency fund.

6. **Mortgage extra principal payments:** If steps 1 through 5 are addressed, extra principal payments on the mortgage are a guaranteed return equal to your mortgage interest rate. At 7%, paying down the mortgage is equivalent to a 7% guaranteed, tax-free (if your mortgage interest is deductible) return.

7. **Taxable investment accounts:** If all of the above are funded, additional savings go into taxable investment accounts.

### Federal Tax Benefits of Home Ownership

**Mortgage interest deduction:** Homeowners who itemize deductions can deduct mortgage interest on up to $750,000 of acquisition debt (loans used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home). The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 reduced this from $1 million and also dramatically increased the standard deduction, meaning many homeowners no longer itemize and thus do not directly benefit from the mortgage interest deduction.

To benefit from itemizing, your total itemized deductions (mortgage interest, state and local taxes, charitable contributions, etc.) must exceed the standard deduction ($29,200 for married filing jointly in 2024; $14,600 for single filers, though these amounts are indexed for inflation and will change). Run the math for your situation.

**Property tax deduction:** State and local taxes (SALT) — including property taxes — are deductible up to $10,000 per year ($5,000 for married filing separately) if you itemize. The SALT cap significantly limits this deduction for homeowners in high-tax states and counties.

**Capital gains exclusion on home sale:** When you sell your primary residence, up to $250,000 of capital gain ($500,000 for married couples filing jointly) is excluded from federal income tax. This exclusion applies if you have owned and used the home as your primary residence for at least 2 of the 5 years before the sale. This is one of the most valuable tax benefits available to individuals and is a significant reason why home ownership builds wealth more efficiently than renting for many people.

**Energy efficiency credits:** The Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (Section 25C) provides a credit of up to 30% of qualifying home energy efficiency improvements, including heat pumps, insulation, windows, doors, and energy audits. There is an annual cap of $3,200 (with sub-limits for specific items). This was discussed in Post 3 for heat pumps and heat pump water heaters.

## Part 7: Property Tax Escrow — How It Works

Most mortgage lenders require an escrow account for property taxes and insurance premiums. Each month, a portion of your total payment (PITI = Principal + Interest + Taxes + Insurance) is deposited into the escrow account. When property taxes and insurance premiums come due, the lender pays them from the escrow account.

**Escrow account analysis:** Each year, your lender does an escrow analysis — comparing what was deposited and paid against what is projected for the next year. If taxes or insurance premiums increased, you may have an escrow shortage, which results in either a one-time payment or an increase in your monthly escrow portion. If deposits exceeded payments, you receive a refund.

If your property taxes are appealed successfully and reduced, your escrow will be adjusted downward in the next annual analysis.

## Part 8: What Happens When You Sell

Eventually, most homeowners sell. Understanding the financial mechanics of a sale helps you maximize the outcome.

**Capital gains calculation:** Your gain is the sale price minus your adjusted basis. Your adjusted basis is what you paid for the home plus qualifying improvements (additions, major renovations — not maintenance) minus any depreciation you took (relevant only if you used the home as a rental). Keep records of all qualifying improvements throughout ownership — these reduce your taxable gain.

**Closing costs on sale:** Sellers typically pay 5% to 7% of the sale price in closing costs: real estate agent commissions (traditionally 5% to 6%, though this is changing following the 2024 NAR settlement), title transfer taxes, attorney fees, and other costs. On a $400,000 sale, selling costs may be $20,000 to $28,000.

**Timing the sale:** The capital gains exclusion requires 2 years of primary residence use. Selling before 2 years may result in taxable capital gain. There are exceptions for certain qualifying circumstances (job relocation, health, unforeseen events) that allow a prorated exclusion even before 2 years.
