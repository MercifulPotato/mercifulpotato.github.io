## Part 1: Nothing Is Free, Itemized

Seventeen days of this series have built, layer by layer, a genuinely strong case for OpenTelemetry: the fragmentation problem it solved, the architecture that makes it neutral, the concrete payoff of correlating a log line to a full trace. Today's post exists specifically to argue the other side, as sincerely and thoroughly as this series has argued every other position, exactly as the original brief for this series demanded from the outset.

Every piece of telemetry this series has built, from the very first span in Part 6 onward, costs something real, and it is worth itemizing those costs plainly rather than leaving them as a vague, unexamined caveat. There is runtime overhead: creating a span, recording a measurement, or writing a structured log record all consume a small amount of CPU time and memory on the machine actually doing the work, and this overhead, while genuinely small per individual operation, is not zero, and it compounds at the volumes Part 16 quantified. There is network and storage cost, covered honestly across Parts 16 and 17: every span transmitted and stored costs bandwidth and disk space, at whatever rate a chosen backend charges. And there is a cost this series has flagged before but which deserves to be named as the largest line item of all: engineering time — not the time to install a package, but the ongoing time to design a sensible instrumentation strategy, tune sampling correctly, keep a Collector fleet healthy, and actually build the operational muscle to use any of this well during a real incident.

## Part 2: The Cardinality Bomb, Detonated in Full

Part 8 of this series introduced the cardinality problem as a warning, worked out with a modest, three-attribute example that multiplied to seventy-two time series. It is worth detonating that warning fully here, as a complete case study, because this is one of the single most common and most expensive real mistakes teams make with OpenTelemetry in production.

Imagine a well-intentioned engineer, wanting richer diagnostic detail, adds a single new attribute to an existing, already-deployed metric: a customer identifier, attached directly to a request-duration histogram, so that any specific customer's own performance could in principle be isolated and inspected. For a service with even a modest ten thousand distinct customers, this one addition does not add ten thousand data points to an existing time series — it multiplies the existing metric into ten thousand entirely separate time series, each independently stored, indexed, and billed by whatever backend receives it. A single well-meaning line of code, merged in good faith on an ordinary Tuesday, can turn a modest, healthy metrics bill into a shockingly large one by the end of the same month, and the backend's own bill, arriving weeks later, is very often the first anyone actually notices. The guardrails this series has already covered — the Views mechanism from Part 8, attribute filtering applied centrally at a Collector of the kind built in Part 13, and a standing habit of naming review before any new attribute reaches production — exist specifically because this mistake is common enough to deserve them.

## Part 3: Complexity as a Tax, in the Project's Own Words

This series has repeatedly cited OpenTelemetry's own public self-criticism as a uniquely credible source, and this is the post where that source deserves to be quoted at full length and full weight, because a project's own governing body criticizing its own complexity is about as strong a piece of evidence as this kind of claim can have.

In a public announcement from its own Governance Committee, OpenTelemetry stated plainly that the project has grown into a massive, complex ecosystem, supporting four different telemetry signals across more than a dozen programming languages, each with its own runtime requirements — and that this complexity creates real barriers to adoption. The same announcement states, in language worth quoting directly rather than paraphrasing away its force, that organizations ready to deploy OpenTelemetry in production encounter unexpected roadblocks: configuration that breaks between minor versions, performance regressions that only appear at scale, and the challenge of coordinating rollouts across hundreds or thousands of services — and that many teams end up delaying or scaling back their OpenTelemetry deployments as a direct result.

This is not a hostile critic's assessment. It is the project's own leadership, in its own words, describing exactly the pattern this post exists to warn about. The same announcement goes further, naming a specific, concrete symptom of this complexity tax: subtle differences in configuration and telemetry output between different versions of instrumentation libraries, Collector receivers, and SDKs can cause real difficulty for adopters, and the project's own rapid release cadence, particularly around the Collector, has proven genuinely difficult for large organizations whose own deployment and upgrade processes move far more slowly and deliberately than the project's own shipping pace.

## Part 4: Maturity Is Uneven, Audited Against This Series' Own Findings

This series has tracked signal-by-signal and language-by-language maturity carefully throughout, and it is worth assembling those individual findings into one consolidated audit here, because the pattern, seen all at once, is more striking than any single instance of it was alone. Tracing reached full, stable, long-term-supported status, verified in Part 4. Logs reached stable status as a specification, but Part 9's own verification against the project's current documentation found genuinely uneven per-language implementation — stable in C#/.NET, C++, Java, and PHP, but still at a development level in JavaScript, Python, Ruby, and Swift. Profiles, examined honestly in Part 4 and cross-checked again in Part 14, remains the newest and least mature signal by a wide margin. And browser and client-side telemetry, flagged explicitly by Microsoft's own documentation back in Part 5 and revisited in Part 12's honest accounting of this very publication's own limitations, remains meaningfully behind the server-side story this series has spent most of its time building.

Applying the four-stage ladder from Part 4 — specified, implemented, stable, adopted — as a genuine audit checklist rather than a one-time explanation: a reader evaluating OpenTelemetry for any specific, real project should check every one of these four stages, for their own specific language and their own specific signal, rather than assuming that "OpenTelemetry supports this" uniformly implies the same level of production-readiness this series demonstrated for C# tracing in Part 12.

## Part 5: When Not to Use OpenTelemetry, Argued Sincerely

Here is the section this post exists most directly to deliver: a sincere, non-strawmanned case for situations where the correct answer is genuinely not to adopt OpenTelemetry, argued with the same care this series has given every position it has examined.

The single-server application whose entire log output fits comfortably in one file, read directly with ordinary command-line tools, gains essentially nothing from distributed tracing, because there is no distribution to trace — Part 6's entire payoff depends on a request crossing service boundaries, and a monolith running on one machine has no such boundaries to cross. The two-person team, already stretched thin building the product itself, faces a genuine opportunity cost in spending scarce engineering time on the SDK wiring, Collector configuration, and sampling tuning this series has spent eighteen days building, when that same time might be better spent elsewhere entirely. The short-lived prototype, explicitly built to be thrown away once it has answered its one specific question, gains nothing from an investment whose value compounds specifically over a long operational lifetime. The team already content on an existing, working vendor agent, with no active pain from lock-in and no genuine multi-cloud plans on the horizon, faces a real migration cost for a portability benefit it may never actually need to exercise, echoing the honest, undecided framework Part 5 built rather than resolved. And the regulated environment, where telemetry itself becomes a data-governance liability rather than a pure asset — personal information inadvertently captured in a span attribute, an exception message, or a log body — has a genuine, serious obligation to treat instrumentation as something requiring active redaction and careful review, not a footnote to bolt on afterward.

## Part 6: Failure Stories, Told Honestly

This series has committed, from Part 1 onward, to never inventing evidence, and that commitment applies here with particular force, because failure stories are exactly the kind of material a less careful account might be tempted to embellish for effect. The honest, general pattern, well documented across the wider industry rather than attributed to any single unverified incident, includes telemetry pipelines that themselves became a source of outages — a Collector exhausting its own memory under an unexpected traffic spike, exactly the failure mode the `memory_limiter` processor from Part 13 exists specifically to guard against; and runaway cardinality bills, exactly the mechanism detonated in full in Part 2 of this very post, discovered only when a monthly invoice arrived far larger than anyone had budgeted for.

This series will not manufacture a specific, named, dated incident it cannot verify simply to make this section feel more concrete. The pattern itself, however, is genuine, widely reported across the industry, and worth taking seriously exactly as seriously as the positive case this series has built for the preceding seventeen days.

## Part 7: How to Spot the Lie — ROI Theater

A specific category of claim deserves this post's own closing test, because it inverts the direction of every prior "spot the lie" section in this series: rather than a vendor overselling a capability, this is the internal, self-directed claim that observability investment simply, automatically pays for itself, presented with enough confidence to shut down further scrutiny of its own underlying assumptions.

An "observability ROI calculator," whether built internally or supplied by a vendor, is doing real, substantive work in exactly the assumptions it asks you to plug in, and those assumptions deserve the same scrutiny this series has applied to every other confident-sounding number throughout. What dollar value was assigned to a minute of downtime, and was that figure genuinely representative of this specific application, or borrowed wholesale from a much larger, much more critical system's own economics? What counterfactual detection time was assumed for the "without observability" baseline — a plausible, honestly estimated number, or a deliberately pessimistic one chosen specifically to make the "with observability" comparison look as favorable as possible?

This returns, in its final and most important form, to the inputs-versus-outcomes distinction Part 1 introduced on this series' very first day and which has quietly anchored nearly everything since: spending money and engineering time on telemetry is an input. Correctly, quickly diagnosing the next real incident is the outcome that spending was supposed to buy. An ROI calculation that only ever measures the input — dollars spent, terabytes ingested, spans instrumented — while never actually verifying the outcome improved, has not demonstrated a return on anything. It has simply restated the cost as though restating it were the same thing as justifying it.
