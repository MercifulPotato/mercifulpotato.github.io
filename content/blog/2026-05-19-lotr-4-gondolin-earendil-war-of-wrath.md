# The Last Light of the Elder Days: The Fall of Gondolin, the Voyage of Eärendil, and the War of Wrath

*This is Part Four of the comprehensive in-universe chronological history of Middle-earth. Part Three told the story of Túrin Turambar and the Children of Húrin — the most sustained tragedy of the First Age. We close now the First Age itself: the fall of the last great city, the third and final Kinslaying, the voyage that changed everything, and the war that ended an age of the world at the cost of the land itself.*

---

## Part 1: Gondolin — The Hidden City and Its Making

Of all the great works of the Noldor in Beleriand, none was more extraordinary than Gondolin. To understand its fall, we must first understand what it was — what it had taken to build it, how it had survived while everything else broke around it, and why its loss mattered in a way that transcended mere military defeat.

Turgon son of Fingolfin had been guided in a dream by Ulmo, Lord of Waters, before the Dagor Bragollach. Ulmo moved through all the waters of the world, including the underground streams of Beleriand, and he had foreseen that the wars against Morgoth could not be won by direct confrontation, that something would have to survive the coming defeats. He sent a vision to Turgon of a hidden valley in the Encircling Mountains — the Echoriath, a ring of peaks surrounding a broad flat-topped plateau through which ran the river Sirion — and told him that there he should build a city.

Turgon found the valley, which the Elves called Tumladen, and he walked it alone and told no one. Then over many years — secretly, in small groups, in ways that would not betray their purpose — he moved his people from their settlement at Vinyamar on the coast to the hidden valley, and there they built.

The building of Gondolin took roughly fifty-two years. This is a span that sounds long to human ears but is a short time for Elves undertaking a great construction, and the construction was great indeed. Gondolin — whose full name was Ondolindë, the Rock of the Music of Water, later called in Sindarin Gondolin, the Hidden Rock — was built on the flat top of a hill called Amon Gwareth, which rose in the centre of the valley. The city was surrounded by walls of white stone, had towers and fountains, gardens and libraries, smithies and music-halls. It was, in the judgment of those who saw both, more beautiful than even Tirion upon Túna in Valinor — which is saying something that defies ordinary superlatives.

The Great Gates of Gondolin were set in the encircling mountains, seven gates of different materials and different powers, and the road through the gates was long and guarded at every step. No one could find the entrance without a guide who already knew it. The Eagles of Thorondor watched the skies above the Echoriath and prevented any approach by air. Ulmo's influence in the water sources of the valley confounded any attempt at magical scrying.

Turgon had twelve houses of noble warriors — the twelve great families of Gondolin — and each house had its own emblems and traditions and fighting style. The most famous of these were: the House of the King, bearing the image of the sun and moon; the House of the Wing, whose lord was Tuor; the House of the Mole, whose lord was the treacherous Maeglin; the House of the Heavenly Arch; the House of the Swallow; the House of the Harp; the House of the Pillar; the House of the Tower of Snow; the House of the Tree; the House of the Golden Flower; the House of the Fountain, whose lord was Ecthelion; and the House of the Hammer of Wrath. Tolkien's early writings — the Book of Lost Tales — describe the Fall of Gondolin in extraordinary detail, naming individual warriors and their deaths in a manner that reads like the Iliad of Middle-earth. The later Silmarillion condensed this, but the texture of a great city with living institutions is present even in the condensation.

Turgon had one daughter: Idril Celebrindal, Silver-foot, who was the most beloved person in all of Gondolin. Her mother had died on the Helcaraxë. She was, like Lúthien before her, a half-divine figure in Tolkien's sense — not Maia-blooded, but possessed of an instinctive perception that exceeded ordinary Elvish foresight. She saw Maeglin clearly. She saw the future clearly. She prepared, as best she could, for what she saw coming.

Gondolin received no visitors. No one came in and no one went out, except by Turgon's strict command. The world beyond the mountains could burn — and it did burn — and Gondolin held. For nearly four centuries, it held.

## Part 2: Aredhel and Eöl — The Shadow That Began the End

The beginning of Gondolin's end was not Morgoth. It was a chain of personal decisions that started with Turgon's sister Aredhel — called Ar-Feiniel, the White Lady — who grew restless in the hidden city and asked to leave.

Aredhel was not made for enclosure. She was a rider and a hunter, accustomed to the wide spaces of the world, and Gondolin's beauty — however genuine — felt like a cage to her. She asked Turgon's permission to go out and visit the sons of Fëanor, with whom she had been friends before Gondolin's founding. Turgon was reluctant — every person who left Gondolin was a risk, because even the best-intentioned traveller could be captured and the city's location tortured out of them. But he could not keep his sister against her will, and eventually he let her go with three companions, with firm instructions not to go south (where the main Morgoth-controlled territory lay) but only east.

She went south anyway. Her companions refused to follow her into danger and returned to Gondolin without her. She passed alone through Nan Dungortheb — the Valley of Dreadful Death, between Doriath and the northern mountains, a place of monstrous spiders descended from Ungoliant — and came out alive, which itself speaks to her quality. And then she entered the forest of Nan Elmoth.

Nan Elmoth was the same forest where Thingol had stood enchanted by Melian. It had its own dark quality — a place where light never entered fully, where the trees were ancient and close, where the Dark Elf Eöl had chosen to live in deliberate isolation.

Eöl was one of the most interesting and least sympathetic figures in the Silmarillion. He had been a friend of the Dwarves of the Blue Mountains before the Noldor arrived in Beleriand, and from the Dwarves he had learned much of metalcraft. He made two things of lasting importance: the two swords Anglachel and Anguirel, forged from a single mass of meteoric iron, each containing something of their maker's dark will; and Galvorn, a black metal of his own devising, flexible as leather but hard as steel. He wore Galvorn armour himself and was essentially invulnerable to ordinary weapons.

He drew Aredhel into Nan Elmoth by enchantment — the forest responded to his desire — and she dwelt with him, seemingly willingly, though the Silmarillion is deliberately ambiguous about how willing. They had a son: Maeglin. Maeglin is one of the great tragic-villainous figures of Tolkien's mythology, a man whose evil was not chosen freely but grew from a poisoned origin and was then exploited by Morgoth at exactly the right moment.

Eöl's name for his son was Maeglin, meaning "Sharp Glance" — the boy had his father's dark intensity and his mother's grace, and he had a gift for seeing into the nature of things, including the nature of ore and metal and stone. He was potentially as great a craftsman as his father. He loved Aredhel, who taught him the language and the lore of the Noldor in secret. And he developed, in the closed world of Nan Elmoth, an obsessive love for his cousin Idril Celebrindal that would ultimately destroy Gondolin.

When Maeglin was grown, Aredhel decided to go home. She left with Maeglin while Eöl was away. Eöl pursued, caught them at the gates of Gondolin — Maeglin had found the gates, because his mother had told him enough of Gondolin's direction that his sharp perception could locate them — and was brought before Turgon. The law of Gondolin was simple: none who found the city could leave it, because the city's location could not be allowed to spread. Eöl accepted this, in the way that very dangerous people accept terms they intend to find a way around.

He was given a place in Gondolin. He loathed it. At a gathering, he threw a javelin at Maeglin — apparently in the belief that if he could not have his son, no one would — and hit Aredhel instead, who stepped in front. The javelin was poisoned. She died that night. Turgon had Eöl brought before him and sentenced to death. They took him to the walls and threw him off, and as he fell he cursed Maeglin: that Maeglin would die as he died, thrown from the walls of Gondolin, and that everything Maeglin built here he would lose in the end.

Maeglin remained in Gondolin. He became one of its great lords — the lord of the House of the Mole, master of the mines, the greatest smith of the city after Turgon himself. He was valued and trusted, insofar as Turgon trusted anyone. And his love for Idril, his cousin, remained and curdled over the decades into something dark, because Idril did not love him. She saw into him — that perception of hers — and what she saw she did not trust, though she could not name what specifically troubled her.

What she saw was not yet betrayal. It became betrayal when Morgoth found a way to reach into Gondolin.

## Part 3: Tuor and the Message of Ulmo

Tuor son of Huor — nephew of Húrin, of the House of Hador — was born in Gondolin's shadow without knowing it. His father Huor had died at the Nirnaeth Arnoediad, and Tuor himself had been hidden with the remnant of Húrin's people in Dor-lómin, where he had grown up essentially enslaved by the Easterlings who occupied the region. He had escaped, lived as an outlaw in the wild for several years, and then been guided by Ulmo himself — the Lord of Waters made his will known to Tuor more directly than to almost anyone else — to the coast, to a hidden sea-grotto where armour and a harp waited for him, and then to the city of Círdan at the Havens.

Círdan received him and gave him a boat and a companion: Voronwë, an Elf of Gondolin who had been sent on one of the rare ships that Turgon dispatched to Valinor seeking aid (the Valar had not responded to these appeals; the time was not yet). Voronwë's ship had been destroyed in a great storm sent by Ossë — who served Ulmo, but with less mercy than his master — and Voronwë alone had survived, cast up on the shore where Tuor found him. Tuor carried Ulmo's message: that Turgon should abandon Gondolin while there was still time, that the city's secrecy could not hold forever, that Ulmo's power to conceal it was diminishing as Morgoth's power grew.

Voronwë brought Tuor to Gondolin. They made the long journey through the wilderness — through Nan Dungortheb, as Aredhel had gone before them — and found the gates. Tuor was the first mortal Man to enter Gondolin.

Turgon received Ulmo's message from Tuor and rejected it. This was Turgon's great failure, the one decision of his life that he would not be able to take back: he had built Gondolin with everything he had, had spent centuries perfecting it, had kept his people safe within it while the rest of Beleriand burned. He could not believe it was not enough. He believed — and Tolkien implies this was a kind of pride as much as hope — that Gondolin could hold indefinitely, that its walls and its secrecy were sufficient protection against anything Morgoth could bring.

He was wrong. And his being wrong cost everything.

What Turgon gave Tuor was not the response Ulmo had requested, but something else: he received Tuor with extraordinary honour, gave him a place among the lords of Gondolin, and gave him his daughter.

Idril loved Tuor. This surprised the people of Gondolin — a mortal Man and the daughter of the king — but Tolkien makes clear it was genuine and mutual and right in the way that Beren and Lúthien had been right. Tuor was good in the specific sense that Tolkien means when he uses the word about Men: brave, humble enough to hear instruction, loyal to things larger than himself, capable of love that did not possess. He was, in short, everything that Maeglin was not.

The marriage of Tuor and Idril enraged Maeglin beyond what his self-control could contain. He had wanted Idril since his youth. A mortal Man — a guest, a newcomer — had what he could not have. And it was at this point, some time after the marriage, that Maeglin was captured by Morgoth's servants.

He had gone outside the walls — against Turgon's law — into the mountains, to seek new ore deposits. The exact manner of his capture by Morgoth's Orcs is not fully detailed, but the outcome is: he was brought to Angband, and Morgoth interrogated him. Morgoth was the master of breaking minds, and Maeglin's mind, already twisted by obsessive love and resentment, was not difficult to break. He gave Morgoth what Morgoth had spent nearly four centuries seeking: the location of Gondolin and the means of reaching it.

In exchange, Morgoth promised him Idril — which was the kind of promise Morgoth made to those whose desires he exploited, the kind he had no intention of keeping. Maeglin returned to Gondolin and waited, and said nothing, and prepared to be ready when the attack came to secure Idril for himself.

He waited years. Morgoth also waited, assembling the force and preparing the approach in absolute secrecy. The assault on Gondolin was the most carefully planned military operation of the First Age, and it required patience that even Morgoth found difficult.

## Part 4: Eärendil — The Child of the Two Kindreds

While these events were being prepared in shadow, Tuor and Idril had a son. He was born in Gondolin during the years of its last peace, and he was given the name Eärendil — "Lover of the Sea" or "Devoted to the Sea," from roots in Old English and the older Elvish that Tolkien used for the name. Tolkien has said in letters that Eärendil was the first character he invented for his mythology — that the whole of the Silmarillion grew, in a sense, from a single image: the Mariner who sailed with a light on his brow to Valinor, the Evening Star.

Eärendil was golden-haired like his father — the inheritance of the House of Hador — and had his mother's bright eyes and something of her foresight. He was a child of extraordinary heritage: on his father's side, the House of Hador, greatest of the Edain; on his mother's side, the Noldorin royal line of Fingolfin stretching back to Valinor. He was, technically, three-quarters Elvish — Tuor was fully Man, but Idril was Noldorin Elf, and Idril's grandmother Elenwë had no Mannish blood. The precise accounting of the fractions mattered because when Eärendil and his wife Elwing had children — Elrond and Elros — the question of which fate they would choose (Elvish immortality or Mannish mortality) depended on the fact that both sides of their heritage were present.

Eärendil grew up in Gondolin knowing the sea only from his father's stories and from Ulmo's presence in the springs and fountains of the city. He dreamed of the sea constantly. He was not made for walls, even beautiful ones. He wanted to sail.

## Part 5: The Fall of Gondolin

Morgoth sent his army against Gondolin in the dead of night, on the high feast of the Gates of Summer — the midsummer festival, when all the people of the city would be gathered on the walls looking east for the dawn. This was Maeglin's intelligence: not just the location of the gates, but the disposition of the people and the timing most likely to find them at their least militarily ready.

The force Morgoth assembled was the greatest he had sent against a single target: Balrogs, and Orcs in numbers beyond count, and — most terrible of all — dragons. Fire-drakes that Glaurung had been the first of, now in greater numbers and of greater power, led by Gothmog Lord of Balrogs. The army came over the Encircling Mountains through gaps in the peaks, having bypassed the seven gates by going over the mountains themselves rather than through the passages. The Eagles did not catch them in time — or perhaps the sheer scale of the force overwhelmed the Eagles' ability to provide warning, since the Eagles were few however mighty each was.

The battle of Gondolin was the greatest battle ever fought within a single city in the history of Middle-earth. It lasted through the night and into the following day. The twelve houses fought their positions with extraordinary courage: the House of the Fountain under Ecthelion held the central square; the House of the King fought around the citadel; the other houses defended the walls and the gates and the great squares. Individual acts of heroism multiplied beyond counting.

Tolkien's early account — in the Book of Lost Tales, written around 1916-1917, and published in the History of Middle-earth Volume 2 — is the most detailed rendering of the battle ever written, and it is worth considering some specific moments because they tell us something about the texture of the First Age that the condensed Silmarillion cannot fully convey.

Ecthelion of the Fountain killed Gothmog, Lord of Balrogs, in single combat in the great square — but was himself killed in the doing, grappling the Balrog and driving both of them into a fountain so that Gothmog was drowned and Ecthelion's armour weighed him down so he could not rise. The death of the Lord of Balrogs — the same being who had killed Fëanor and Fingon — in a fountain, by an Elf who chose to drown himself to drown the demon — is one of the most extraordinary deaths in all of Tolkien.

Glorfindel — the lord of the House of the Golden Flower — fought a Balrog on the mountain path during the escape, and killed it, and fell with it into the abyss. This Glorfindel was reembodied in Valinor in the Second Age and returned to Middle-earth in the Third Age, and is the same Glorfindel who rescues Frodo on the road to Rivendell in The Fellowship of the Ring and whose presence sends the Nazgûl fleeing. The name is identical because it is the same person, returned. The rider on the white horse who carries the wounded Frodo and cries "Noro lim, noro lim, Asfaloth!" has killed a Balrog. He is not simply a noble Elf-lord; he is one of the veterans of the First Age.

Maeglin, during the battle, seized Idril. He attempted to carry out Morgoth's promise to him — or perhaps his own obsession regardless of any promise. Tuor fought him. They fought on the walls of the citadel, and Tuor took Maeglin and threw him from the walls, and as he fell, Eöl's curse was fulfilled: Maeglin died as his father had died, thrown from the walls of Gondolin.

Turgon died in the citadel, refusing to abandon his city, buried in the ruin of his tower.

The city burned. The fountains were choked with ash. The gardens of Gondolin, which had been among the most beautiful things in the world, were destroyed.

But Idril had prepared. This is the importance of her foresight — that she had not trusted completely in the city's safety, that she had spent years quietly cutting a secret passage out through the northern side of the valley, a tunnel that bypassed the main gates and emerged in the mountains. She had told almost no one about it. She had told Tuor. She had not told Maeglin.

Tuor gathered what people he could find in the chaos of the battle — a remnant of the twelve houses, perhaps a few thousand out of a city that had held tens of thousands — and led them through Idril's tunnel. They emerged into the mountains and took the high path toward the south, the pass of Cirith Thoronath — the Eagle's Cleft — a narrow and terrible route above the clouds.

The Balrogs and Orcs caught them at Cirith Thoronath. It was there that Glorfindel died. It was there, according to the earliest tradition, that Thorondor himself came and the Eagles fought the Orcs and Balrogs to buy time for the refugees. And finally, the broken remnant of Gondolin descended into the valley of the Sirion and began the long walk south.

Gondolin was gone. The most beautiful city of the First Age was ash and ruin. Of the seven cities and great strongholds of the Noldor in Beleriand — Hithlum, the sons of Fëanor's eastern holdings, Nargothrond, the Falas, Dorthonion, the great open lands — only the refugee camps at the Mouths of Sirion now remained. Everything else had fallen.

## Part 6: The Mouths of Sirion — Eärendil Grown

The survivors of Gondolin came south to the Mouths of Sirion, where the great river spread into a delta before reaching the sea. Here they found others: the survivors of Doriath after the Second Kinslaying, including Elwing daughter of Dior, who had the Silmaril of Beren and Lúthien. Círdan's people were there, keeping small boats and maintaining a last foothold on the western shore.

This was the last community of the free Elves in Middle-earth. They were a remnant, a population of survivors living in a delta, with no great fortress and no great lord except for what local governance they could maintain. But they were alive. And among them, Eärendil came to his full gifts.

He built a ship. Of course he built a ship — he had dreamed of the sea his whole life. The ship was called Vingilot, "Foam-flower," and it was the finest ship ever made in Middle-earth, built with Círdan's guidance and Eärendil's own skills. He sailed along the coast, and then out to sea, and he sailed looking for Valinor.

He could not find it. Valinor was hidden — after the Noldorin exile, the Valar had taken steps to prevent the uninvited from reaching Aman. The Straight Road — the path between Middle-earth and Valinor — was hidden from those who did not know the way, and Eärendil did not know the way. He sailed west and found only open ocean, and east and found only the coasts of Middle-earth, and south and found nothing.

He returned from his voyages to find disaster waiting.

## Part 7: The Third Kinslaying — The Havens of Sirion

The sons of Fëanor knew about the Silmaril at the Mouths of Sirion. They had known since Elwing fled Doriath with it after the Second Kinslaying. They had sent messages demanding it — the Oath required them to demand it. Elwing had refused to give up what Beren and Lúthien had won and Dior had died to protect.

The remaining sons of Fëanor — Maedhros and Maglor and the twins Amrod and Amras — attacked the Havens of Sirion in Eärendil's absence. It was the Third Kinslaying, the last and in some ways most terrible of the three: they attacked a community of refugees, already broken by Gondolin's fall and Doriath's ruin, a people who had lost everything else. The people of the Havens fought back, and many were killed on both sides. The twins Amrod and Amras were killed in the fighting. Maedhros and Maglor survived.

But they did not get the Silmaril.

Elwing, when she saw the attack coming and understood that all was lost, took the Silmaril and threw herself into the sea. She was not a warrior; she could not fight; but she could refuse to let the jewel be taken. She leaped with it from the cliff into the water.

Ulmo saved her. He raised her from the sea in the form of a white bird — a great seabird, transformed by the Vala's power — and she flew west, following the track that Eärendil's ship had taken, and found him at sea.

She came to him as a bird and settled on his ship, and when she regained her form she was there with him, with the Silmaril on her breast, and she told him what had happened. The Havens were destroyed. Their children — Elrond and Elros, who had been young boys when the attack came — had been taken by Maedhros and Maglor rather than killed. (Maglor raised them, and genuinely loved them, as he had tried to make some amends for his crimes; this is the same Maglor who wandered singing on the shore at the Age's end.) Elwing and Eärendil's home was gone.

There was nothing left. There was nowhere left to go in Middle-earth.

## Part 8: The Voyage to Valinor — The Only Hope Remaining

Eärendil made the decision that no living person had made in the history of the world up to that point: he would sail to Valinor. Not to seek it as he had before, tentatively, hoping to find it — but to go deliberately, to use everything available to him to find the Straight Road, to reach the Blessed Realm and plead for the aid of the Valar.

He knew the penalty. Any living Elvish or Mannish soul who set foot on Valinor unbidden faced judgment and possibly death — the Valar had been clear, after the Noldorin exile, that the way was shut to those who had not been invited. He was going anyway. He was going because there was no alternative. Beleriand was almost entirely under Morgoth's control. Gondolin was gone. The sons of Fëanor were burning what little remained. The Men of Beleriand were scattered or destroyed. If something did not change — if the Valar did not intervene — Morgoth would complete his dominion over Middle-earth, and the Children of Ilúvatar would be enslaved or destroyed.

Elwing wore the Silmaril around her neck. Eärendil wore it on his brow, set in a band of mithril, when they approached Aman — the Silmaril that Beren had cut from Morgoth's crown, that Lúthien had helped recover, that had burned through the death of Carcharoth and the ruin of Doriath and the Third Kinslaying. The light of it was the light of the Two Trees, the oldest and purest light in the world, and perhaps it was that light that finally showed Eärendil the Straight Road. Perhaps it was Ulmo's guidance. Perhaps both. He found the way.

He beached Vingilot on the shores of Aman and walked alone to Tirion upon Túna, the city of the Elves on the hill, and found it empty — the Elves were gathered elsewhere, or perhaps this is one of Tolkien's ways of indicating that the moment is sacred and solitary, that this confrontation with destiny had to be faced without witnesses. He walked through the empty streets and called out.

The Valar heard.

What passed between Eärendil and the Valar is told in the Silmarillion with considerable economy, because the full deliberations of the Valar are beyond mortal or Elvish comprehension. What we know is that Eärendil pleaded for mercy — for Elves and Men both, not for himself alone. He pleaded in the name of all who had suffered, all who had struggled and died. He wore the Silmaril and he spoke, and the Valar heard.

They also judged him. Eärendil had come to Aman unbidden, which was forbidden. He and Elwing were both Half-elven — mixed heritage, neither fully Elvish nor fully Mannish. The question of what to do with them was unprecedented. The Valar took the matter to Ilúvatar, or so it is implied — the decision they made has the character of a divine accommodation rather than a simple rule applied.

The judgment: Eärendil and Elwing and all the Half-elven (including Elrond and Elros, wherever they were in Middle-earth) were given a choice. They could choose which fate to belong to — the fate of the Eldar, immortal, bound to the world; or the fate of Men, mortal, passing beyond. Each could choose individually, and the choice would be permanent.

Eärendil would have preferred to return to Middle-earth. He was a mortal Man at heart, or a Man who had become something between. But Elwing wished to be among the Eldar, and for her sake he accepted the same choice, and they both chose to be of Elvish kind. His son Elrond later made the same choice. His son Elros chose to be mortal, and became the first King of Númenor.

And Eärendil himself was given a task: he would sail across the sky. The Silmaril on his brow became a star — the brightest star in the sky, the Evening Star and the Morning Star, what we call Venus. He sails still, in Tolkien's mythology, across the heavens, and his light is the Silmaril that Beren cut from the crown of Morgoth, carried now at the height of the sky where it can be seen from all the world.

The Elves named that star Gil-Estel, the Star of High Hope. It was the first new light after the long dark, and when the free peoples of Middle-earth saw it rise in the East, they knew something had changed.

## Part 9: The Host of Valinor Sets Sail — The Beginning of the War of Wrath

The Valar had been petitioned before — by the Eagles, by Ulmo's promptings, by the prayers of the Elves and Men in Beleriand. They had not acted directly. The reasons are complex: the Valar had a deep reluctance to intervene in the affairs of the world with their full power, partly because of the damage they had already done to the world's physical fabric in the First Battle, and partly because Ilúvatar's design seemed to require that the Children develop and struggle and learn without having everything decided for them by beings of overwhelming power.

But Eärendil's plea was different from what had come before. He had come himself, at personal risk, wearing the Silmaril — the most holy object in the world, the last remnant of the Two Trees' light. He had come as the representative of both kindreds, Elf and Man. And the plea he made was not for himself but for others. The Valar heard in it what they had perhaps been waiting for: the genuine voice of the Children, freely offered, asking for help not out of pride but out of love.

Manwë gave the order. The Host of Valinor would sail to Middle-earth.

The host that assembled was unlike anything since the War of the Powers at the beginning of the Age. It included the Vanyar — the Firstborn Elves, those who had come first to Valinor and had never left and had never been part of the Noldorin exile or the wars of Beleriand. The Vanyar had weapons and skills of their own, different from the Noldor's, and they had lived in Valinor at the feet of the Valar for the entire history of the First Age. They came prepared for war in a way that the free peoples of Beleriand, worn down by centuries of fighting, could only imagine.

The Noldor of Valinor also came — those who had not gone into exile with Fëanor, the kinfolk who had remained. And the Teleri of Valinor brought their ships. They had refused to help Fëanor steal ships for the exile — had been killed by Fëanor at Alqualondë for refusing — and now they came freely, on their own terms, for a purpose they had chosen.

Eönwë, Herald of Manwë, led the host. This was the same Eönwë who would later summon Sauron to face judgment.

They sailed east. They came to the shores of Middle-earth. And they began what the Elves called the Great Battle, the War of Wrath: Dagor Dagorath is a different thing — the Last Battle at the end of the world — but the War of Wrath was itself so decisive that it dwarfed everything before it.

## Part 10: The War of Wrath — The Breaking of the World

The War of Wrath lasted forty-two years. This number, appearing in Tolkien's texts, should give an idea of the scale: this was not a single campaign or a sequence of battles in a season. It was a sustained war across the whole of northern Beleriand, fought against the full power of Morgoth's fortress and armies.

Morgoth sent out everything he had. He had been preparing for centuries, building his power, breeding his creatures, waiting. The Orcs came in their greatest numbers ever. Balrogs — there had been many in Angband, bred and maintained over the long ages — came with them. Glaurung was dead, but there were other fire-drakes. And then, at the last, Morgoth sent the winged dragons.

The winged dragons were his final weapon, held in reserve until this moment. Ancalagon the Black was the greatest of them — a beast of such enormous size that when he fell, in the last day of the war, his body crushed the towers of Thangorodrim. These were not the ground-crawling drakes like Glaurung; they flew, they breathed fire from the air, they were weapons of aerial attack that nothing the Host of Valinor had prepared for could counter.

For a time, even the Host of Valinor was driven back.

Then Eärendil came from the west.

He came in Vingilot, his ship made of mithril and of the birch-wood of Nimbrethil — a ship that had been hallowed in Valinor and set to sail the skies rather than the seas, and on his brow the Silmaril blazed. He came like a star descending into the battle, and the winged dragons turned to face him. He fought Ancalagon — the greatest of all the winged dragons — from his sky-ship, through the night and into the first dawn. And at the dawn he cast Ancalagon down, and the dragon fell on the three peaks of Thangorodrim and broke them.

The towers of Morgoth's great fortress, built over the immeasurable pits of Angband, built atop the roots of the Ered Engrin, were shattered by the body of a falling dragon. This tells us something about the scale of Ancalagon. It also tells us something about the scale of Eärendil: that a Half-elven Mariner, sailing in a hallowed ship with a Silmaril on his brow, could kill the greatest dragon in the history of the world.

After Ancalagon's fall, Morgoth's forces broke. The remaining dragons fled into the wastelands or were killed. The Balrogs were destroyed almost entirely — a few may have survived by hiding in the deep places of the earth, which would account for the Balrog in Moria in the Third Age, but Tolkien was uncertain about this, and different texts suggest different things. The Orc armies were routed, destroyed, scattered to the dark places. Angband itself was broken open, its pits uncovered, its dungeons emptied of whatever prisoners still lived — there were few.

Morgoth fled into the deepest pit. The Host of Valinor dug him out. He was taken and his Iron Crown was beaten into a collar for his neck, and the two remaining Silmarils were taken from the Crown and kept by Eönwë. And Morgoth was thrust through the Door of Night — the gate at the world's edge, beyond the Walls of Night, into the Void beyond Eä. He was removed from the world. Not destroyed, because the Valar had not the power to destroy a fellow Ainu in the way that material things can be destroyed — his spirit existed, somewhere in the Void, diminished and impotent. But he was gone from Arda, permanently. The First Age was over.

## Part 11: The Cost — Beleriand Beneath the Sea

The physical cost of the War of Wrath was the land of Beleriand itself.

The weapons used in this war — the power of the Vanyar and the Noldor of Valinor, the instruments of the Valar themselves, the total release of whatever power Morgoth had accumulated and was expending in his final defence, the fall of Ancalagon — shook the world to its roots. The land that had been Beleriand was broken apart. The great chain of the Blue Mountains was sundered. The sea rushed in.

Most of Beleriand sank beneath the waves. The land west of the Blue Mountains, from the shore of the Great Sea eastward to the Ered Luin, simply ceased to exist as land. The river Sirion, the heart of Beleriand's geography, flowed into an ocean that had not been there before. The mountains of Hithlum, the forests of Brethil, the ruins of Gondolin in the valley of the Echoriath, the grave-mound of Túrin on the heights of the Teiglin, the Fords of Arossiach, the Guarded Plain — all of it was gone under the sea.

What remained east of the Blue Mountains was the northwestern coast of Middle-earth as it would exist into the Second and Third Ages: a new coastline, formed by the sundering, with the Ered Luin running north-south as a mountain barrier and the Gulf of Lune at its southern end, where the river Lhûn ran through the mountains to the sea. The grey havens of Mithlond — Círdan's new harbours, built in the Second Age — would be in this gulf. The lands of Eriador — the region between the Blue Mountains and the Misty Mountains — were untouched, and that is where the Second Age's story of Middle-earth begins.

The physical destruction of Beleriand is one of Tolkien's most powerful pieces of world-building. It explains why none of the great kingdoms of the First Age are findable in the maps of The Lord of the Rings. They are underwater. The world of the Second and Third Ages is genuinely a different world than the world of the First Age — reshaped by catastrophe, its geography altered, its history accessible only through song and legend and the long memories of the Elves.

---

## Part 12: The Fate of the Sons of Fëanor — The Last Act of the Oath

After the War of Wrath, Eönwë called all the survivors of the Noldor and Edain in Middle-earth to gather. The Noldorin exiles were offered pardon — the Doom of the Noldor was lifted, they could return to Valinor if they chose. Many did. The Edain were offered a gift: a new land, an island raised from the sea by the Valar, between Middle-earth and Valinor but closer to Valinor — not in sight of the Blessed Realm, but blessed with a longer lifespan and with gifts of knowledge from the Valar. This was Númenor, and its first King was Elros son of Eärendil.

But the sons of Fëanor — Maedhros and Maglor, the two survivors — would not come to the gathering without the Silmarils. The Oath still bound them. Eönwë had the two remaining Silmarils — recovered from Morgoth's Crown. He refused to give them to the sons of Fëanor. He said the judgment of their use belonged to the Valar.

Maedhros and Maglor stole them. In the night, they crept into Eönwë's camp and took one Silmaril each. Eönwë, according to some versions of the story, chose not to prevent this — either because he could not, or because he recognised that the Oath could not be broken except by its holders, and the Oath would now break them.

Both Silmarils burned their bearers. The three Silmarils were holy — they had been hallowed by Varda herself before they left Valinor — and no evil thing could touch them without pain. And the hands of Maedhros and Maglor, after all the Kinslayings and all the murders and all the crimes committed in the Oath's name, could no longer hold them.

Maedhros, in agony, threw himself into a volcanic chasm with his Silmaril. He died and took the jewel into the earth. The Silmaril is there still, in the fires under the ground, the light of Telperion and Laurelin burning in the deep rock.

Maglor walked to the sea and threw his Silmaril into the waves. It is there still, somewhere in the deep ocean, the light of the Two Trees moving with the tides. And Maglor walked along the shore, singing a lament of grief so beautiful that the Elves who heard it said it was the greatest of laments, and he has not been heard from since. He did not go to Valinor. He is still out there, perhaps, walking the beaches of the world.

The three Silmarils: one in the sky (Eärendil's star), one in the earth, one in the sea. Sky, earth, and water. The lights of the Two Trees are distributed through all the elements of the world, inaccessible, unrecoverable, and present. Tolkien found that a fitting end to the Age.

---

## Part 13: The Survivors — Who Remained and What They Carried

Not all of the free peoples chose to go to Valinor or to Númenor. The Sindar of Beleriand — those who had survived — had never been to Valinor and did not desire it. Many of them went east, over the Blue Mountains into Eriador, and some went still further east, crossing the Misty Mountains. The Grey Elves who went furthest eventually became the Wood-elves of Greenwood the Great — the Mirkwood of the Third Age — and their traditions would shape the culture of Thranduil and his people for the entire Third Age.

Among the survivors of the First Age who remained in Middle-earth and whose presence shapes the Third Age directly:

**Galadriel** — Galadriel daughter of Finarfin, who had gone into exile with the Noldor and had dwelt in Beleriand through the entire First Age, including a long period in Doriath where she had learned from Melian and where she had met Celeborn, whom she married. Galadriel refused the pardon. She was the only Noldorin exile to do so, and the reasons are complex — Tolkien changed his mind about them several times. In the final version of his thinking, she had come to Middle-earth partly out of a desire to rule, to have a realm of her own, and she recognised that accepting the pardon without having yet renounced that desire would be a kind of dishonesty. She stayed. She would eventually receive one of the Three Rings of the Elves and rule Lothlórien, and she would be offered — and would refuse — the One Ring when Frodo offered it to her in the Third Age. Her refusal of the Ring was the final act by which she earned the right to go to Valinor, and she sailed on the last ship at the end of the Third Age.

**Elrond** — Son of Eärendil and Elwing, foster-child of Maglor, who chose to be counted among the Elvish kindred. He founded Rivendell, Imladris, in the Second Age and dwelt there through the entire Third Age as its lord and counsellor. He carried the ring Vilya, mightiest of the Three. He was Bilbo's host and the counsellor of the Fellowship.

**Círdan** — The Shipwright, the oldest Elf in Middle-earth, who had held the Havens through every disaster. He would hold the Grey Havens of Mithlond through the Second and Third Ages and would be the one to build the Last Ship and sail with the Ring-bearers at the end of the Third Age.

**Gil-galad** — Ereinion Gil-galad, whose parentage is a matter of inconsistency in Tolkien's texts (Tolkien settled on him as the son of Fingon, Fingolfin's son, though other texts say he was Orodreth's son), became the last High King of the Noldor in Middle-earth. He had survived because he had been sent to the Havens as a child before the Nirnaeth, kept safe by Círdan. He ruled in Lindon through the Second Age and died at the foot of Mordor in the War of the Last Alliance. His ring was Vilya, which he gave to Elrond.

## Part 14: The Theological Meaning of Eärendil

Tolkien was a Catholic, and his mythology inevitably carries resonances with Christian thought, though he was always careful to say that his mythology was not allegory. The figure of Eärendil, however, carries a particular weight that invites reflection.

Eärendil is a mediator — someone who stands between two parties and pleads on behalf of one to the other. He comes before the Valar representing Elves and Men, bearing the holiest object in the world, and asks for help that has been withheld, that seems to have been permanently withheld. He is not powerful enough, by himself, to make the plea effective by force; he can only ask. And the asking, done rightly, with the right object and the right spirit and the right words, is enough.

There is in this something of the intercessory tradition — the idea that certain beings stand between the human and the divine and speak on behalf of those who cannot speak directly. Tolkien himself was aware of this resonance; he wrote to Milton Waldman that the mythology was in some sense a completion of the Anglo-Saxon's mythology as he wished it had been, and the Elvish mythology has something of the pre-Christian Northern world's sense of divine powers who can be appealed to and who sometimes respond.

The Silmaril itself, in this context, is not merely a beautiful jewel. It is the light of the world's first purity, the light of before the Fall — before Melkor's first discord, before the Trees were killed, before any of the catastrophes of the Elder Days. It is, in some sense, innocence and beauty preserved against entropy. Eärendil carries it to Valinor as proof — proof that something of what had been made was still worth saving, that the world had not been entirely destroyed by the darkness, that there was still something in it worth the Valar's effort to protect.

And the Valar agree. They come. They end the Age.

## Part 15: The Shape of What Came Next

The destruction of Morgoth did not destroy Sauron. Sauron had not been at Angband for the War of Wrath — his location during the final years of the First Age is not specified in the texts, but he escaped the destruction of Morgoth's fortress and survived into the Second Age. Eönwë summoned him to come before the Valar and face judgment; Sauron came, appeared penitent, and then when it became clear that the judgment would include returning to Valinor for assessment, he refused. He fled back into Middle-earth.

This is the seed of everything that follows: Sauron's refusal to be judged, his return to Middle-earth, his gradual rebuilding of power in the Second Age, his creation of the Rings of Power, the corruption of Númenor, the Downfall, the Last Alliance, the One Ring. Everything in the Second and Third Ages traces back to this moment: Sauron refusing the summons of Eönwë on the shore of Middle-earth after the War of Wrath, turning and going back into the east.

He was not yet the Lord of the Rings. He was not yet Sauron the Deceiver, the Necromancer, the Eye. In the early Second Age, he was still capable of appearing fair — he had not yet been forced into the permanent hideous form that the destruction of Númenor would eventually impose on him. He was a craftsman, a Maia of Aulë, brilliant and patient and utterly without the will to repent that Eönwë had hoped for.

The Second Age is the age of his greatest triumph and greatest defeat. That is the subject of Part Five.

## Part 16: A Complete Account of the First Age's Character — What the Elder Days Were

Having told the full story of the First Age from the creation through the War of Wrath, it is worth stepping back and considering what kind of age it was — what its characteristic qualities were, what it added to the world, and what it left behind that shaped everything after.

The First Age was the age of Elves — in the specific sense that it was the age in which the Eldar were at the height of their power and their gifts, before the long diminishment that the later ages would bring. The Noldor of Beleriand were the most skilled craftspeople in the history of the world, the greatest warriors, the most powerful singers of songs of power. Fëanor's Silmarils were the greatest work of craft ever achieved. Lúthien's song in Mandos was the greatest music ever made. The buildings of Gondolin were the most beautiful architecture. These were not modest achievements — the First Age made things whose equal was never attempted again.

But the First Age was also the age of the Oath and the Kinslayings, of the Doom of the Noldor, of the catastrophic pride that brought great people to terrible ends. The Noldorin exiles were magnificent and doomed in roughly equal measure, and the doom came from within — from the Oath that Fëanor swore, from the pride that made them unable to accept help or counsel, from the conviction that their own gifts were sufficient for any challenge. They were not. No Elvish gifts were sufficient for Morgoth without the aid of the Valar. Morgoth was too powerful.

Men in the First Age were represented by the best of their kind — the Edain, who had resisted Morgoth's corruption and sought the light. They were also mortal, and their mortality meant that however brave and loyal they were, they could not sustain the long wars that the Elves could. Húrin endured for decades on Thangorodrim; a mortal Man cannot do what an immortal Elf might have done. This is not a criticism of Men; it is simply the structure of their existence. Their gift is not endurance but presence — they are fully in each moment in a way that immortal beings perhaps cannot be, because each moment is not one of an infinite series but one of a finite and precious few.

The great love stories of the First Age — Beren and Lúthien, Tuor and Idril — are all stories of mortal and immortal joining across the difference in their natures. This is not coincidence. Tolkien seems to have believed that the two kinds of Children were meant for each other in some way, that their differences were complementary — that what Men brought in vitality and presence, Elves brought in depth and memory, and together they made something greater than either could be alone. The children of these unions — Eärendil, Dior — were the bridges between the ages.

The language legacy of the First Age is also important. The Elvish languages developed in Beleriand into their classical forms: Quenya, the High-elven tongue of Valinor, became the Latin of the Elvish world, used in ceremony and high poetry; Sindarin, the language of the Grey Elves, became the common tongue of the Elves who remained in Middle-earth, the language in which Aragorn speaks to the Elves of Rivendell, the language of Frodo's cry "A Elbereth Gilthoniel." When Tolkien constructed his Elvish languages, he was building something that would encode the entire history of this lost world — every word in Sindarin carries in it the memory of the First Age, of Beleriand under the stars, of a people who loved language and made it live.

---

## Conclusion: The First Age Is Over

With the War of Wrath, the First Age of the Sun ended. Morgoth was gone. Beleriand was gone. The survivors were scattered: some to Valinor, some to the new island of Númenor, some remaining in Middle-earth east of the Blue Mountains. The world had been remade by catastrophe for the second time (the first being the fall of the Lamps of the Valar), and the map of the Second Age was drawn in part by the ocean that had swallowed an entire civilisation.

The three Silmarils were in the sky, the earth, and the sea.

The Elves who remained in Middle-earth began the long process of learning to live in a world without Beleriand, without the great kingdoms, without the focused pressure of Morgoth's presence. For a few centuries, it was almost quiet.

And then Sauron remembered what he was, and began.
