In the last post we followed Nepal's money to its largest source — revenue, the roughly NPR 1.4 trillion the government plans to raise, mostly from taxes tied to imports driven by the wages of migrant workers. But that revenue covers only about two-thirds of what the government plans to spend. The total budget is NPR 2,124.34 billion; revenue is NPR 1,405.31 billion; even adding the foreign grants the government expects, a large gap remains. That gap is the subject of this post, and it is one of the most important and most misunderstood quantities in any budget. It is called the **deficit**, and the way a government fills it — by borrowing — creates the **debt** that will shape the country's finances for years and decades to come.

This is where many citizens' eyes glaze over, because "deficit" and "debt" sound like the dry preserve of economists. But these ideas are not difficult, and they are not optional to understand, because the decision to borrow is among the most consequential a government makes: it reaches into the future and commits the country's children to repay, with interest, the choices made today. By the end of this post you will know exactly what a deficit is, the difference between a grant and a loan and between borrowing at home and abroad, what public debt is and when it becomes dangerous, who Nepal owes its money to, and how the interest on past borrowing quietly devours a growing slice of every year's budget. We will not tell you that deficits are sinful or that debt is wicked; both can be wise or foolish depending on what the borrowed money does. We will tell you how to judge which.

## What is a deficit?

Start with the plain idea, using the household we keep returning to. Imagine a family that earns a certain amount each month but chooses to spend more than it earns. The gap between what it spends and what it earns is its deficit, and it must be filled somehow — by borrowing, or by drawing on savings, or by receiving a gift. The family cannot spend money it does not have without getting that money from somewhere, and the deficit is precisely the measure of how much it must get from somewhere beyond its own earnings.

A government's deficit is the same idea at national scale. It is the gap between what the government plans to *spend* and what it expects to *raise from its own revenue and from grants*. In this budget, the government plans to spend NPR 2,124.34 billion (about USD 14.16 billion). It expects to raise NPR 1,405.31 billion (about USD 9.37 billion) in revenue, and to receive NPR 61.74 billion (about USD 0.41 billion) in foreign grants. Add the revenue and the grants together — about NPR 1,467 billion — and subtract that from the total spending, and you are left with a gap of roughly **NPR 657 billion**, about **USD 4.4 billion**. That gap is the deficit: the amount the government plans to spend that is covered neither by its own revenue nor by gifts, and that must therefore be *borrowed*. Stated as a fraction, the government plans to borrow to cover nearly a third of everything it spends. That is the headline of this post, and everything else explains what it means and whether it should worry you.

A crucial first clarification: a deficit is not the same as debt, and confusing the two is one of the most common errors in public discussion. The **deficit** is a *flow* — the gap in a single year, the amount borrowed *this year*. The **debt** is a *stock* — the total accumulated amount the government owes, built up from all the deficits of all the past years that have not yet been repaid. A useful image is a bathtub: the debt is the water already in the tub, and each year's deficit is the additional water flowing in from the tap (minus whatever drains out as old debt is repaid). A government can reduce its deficit — slow the tap — while its debt still rises, because the tap is still adding more than the drain removes. Keep the flow and the stock distinct: the deficit is this year's borrowing; the debt is the total owed.

## Grants versus loans: the difference that matters most

The gap can be filled, in part, by money from abroad, and here we meet a distinction that is fundamental and that governments sometimes blur: the difference between a *grant* and a *loan*.

A **grant** is a gift. It is money given to Nepal, typically by foreign governments or international institutions, that does *not* have to be repaid. When a donor provides a grant, the money is Nepal's to keep and use; it adds to the resources available without creating any obligation for the future. Grants are, in this sense, the best kind of external money: they help fund spending without burdening the country with debt. This budget expects NPR 61.74 billion (about USD 0.41 billion) in foreign grants — a real contribution, but, you will notice, a relatively small one, far too small to fill the deficit on its own. Grants help at the margin; they do not, for Nepal, come close to covering the gap.

A **loan** is borrowed money that *must be repaid*, and repaid with **interest** — an extra charge, a percentage of the borrowed amount, paid for the use of the money over time. This is the crucial difference: a loan creates an obligation for the future. Every rupee borrowed today is a rupee that must be paid back tomorrow, plus interest, out of future budgets — which means out of future taxpayers' pockets, including those of people not yet born or not yet earning. When a government borrows, it is, in a real sense, bringing forward spending from the future to the present, and committing the future to pay for it with interest. This is not automatically wrong — as we will discuss, borrowing to build something that generates future returns can be entirely sensible — but it is never free, and the interest cost, in particular, becomes a permanent charge on future budgets, a point we will return to with some force. The vast majority of the gap in this budget is filled not by grants but by loans, which means this budget, like most, is substantially funded by commitments against the future.

## Foreign borrowing and domestic borrowing

The loans that fill the deficit come from two distinct places, and the distinction between them matters because they have different costs and different risks. This budget plans to borrow **NPR 247.28 billion (about USD 1.65 billion) from foreign lenders** and **NPR 410 billion (about USD 2.73 billion) from within Nepal itself**.

**Foreign borrowing** — also called external borrowing — is money borrowed from outside the country: from foreign governments, and especially from international institutions such as the World Bank and the Asian Development Bank. The important feature of much of Nepal's foreign borrowing is that it is **concessional** — meaning it is lent on terms much more favourable than commercial markets would offer: very low interest rates, and long repayment periods stretching over decades. Concessional lending is a form of support for poorer countries, and it is far less burdensome than borrowing at commercial rates. This is genuinely advantageous: it allows Nepal to borrow large sums cheaply and to repay them slowly. But foreign borrowing carries a particular risk that domestic borrowing does not — it is usually denominated in *foreign currency*, typically dollars, which means it must eventually be repaid in dollars. If the rupee weakens against the dollar over the long repayment period, the rupee cost of repaying a dollar loan rises, even though the dollar amount owed has not changed. This *currency risk* is a real, if often slow-moving, danger of foreign borrowing, and it is one reason a country must be careful about how much foreign-currency debt it takes on relative to its ability to earn the foreign currency to repay it.

**Domestic borrowing** — also called internal borrowing — is money the government borrows from *within* Nepal: principally by issuing government bonds and treasury bills, which are essentially IOUs that domestic banks, financial institutions, and others buy, lending their money to the government in exchange for a promise of repayment with interest. Domestic borrowing avoids the currency risk of foreign borrowing, because it is in rupees and repaid in rupees. But it carries a different danger, one with a memorable name: **crowding out**. The pool of money available to be lent within Nepal is finite. When the government borrows heavily from that domestic pool, it competes with private businesses and individuals for the same finite funds. This competition can push up interest rates and leave less money available for the private sector to borrow — so that the government's borrowing "crowds out" private borrowing and investment. A government that borrows too much domestically can thus starve the very private economy whose growth it depends on, by soaking up the available credit and making it scarcer and dearer for everyone else. So each kind of borrowing has its hazard: foreign borrowing risks the currency; domestic borrowing risks crowding out the private sector. This budget uses a great deal of both.

There is a subtlety in the domestic-borrowing figure worth flagging, because it illustrates the deficit-versus-debt distinction in action. The NPR 410 billion of gross domestic borrowing is not all *new* debt added to the pile. A large part of it — about NPR 245.89 billion (around USD 1.64 billion) — is needed simply to *repay the principal* on past domestic loans that are coming due this year. In other words, the government is borrowing afresh in part to pay back old borrowing, a practice called *rolling over* debt. The genuinely *new* domestic debt added this year — the net increase in the domestic debt stock — is the difference: about NPR 164.11 billion (around USD 1.09 billion). This is exactly the bathtub at work: NPR 410 billion flows in from the tap, but NPR 245.89 billion drains out as old debt is repaid, so the water level rises by only NPR 164.11 billion. A government can thus report a large gross borrowing figure while the net addition to its debt is considerably smaller — and, conversely, a government rolling over a lot of maturing debt must borrow a lot just to stand still. Both the gross figure (how much it must raise) and the net figure (how much its debt actually grows) tell you something, and a careful reader distinguishes them.

## What is public debt, and when does it become dangerous?

We have spoken of debt as the accumulated stock of past borrowing. Let us now look at that stock directly, because the question every citizen ultimately wants answered is: how much does Nepal owe, and is it too much?

**Public debt** is the total amount the government owes, accumulated from all its past borrowing — domestic and foreign — that has not yet been repaid. It grows each year by the amount of new net borrowing (this year's deficit financed by new debt) and shrinks as old debt is repaid. Over the past decade or so, Nepal's public debt has grown substantially, both in rupee terms and as a share of the economy. To judge whether a debt is large or dangerous, the figure economists watch most is the **debt-to-GDP ratio** — the total debt measured against the size of the economy (the GDP we met in Part 1), exactly as a sensible lender judges a household's debt not by its absolute size but against the household's income. A debt of a given size is far more manageable for a large, fast-growing economy than for a small, stagnant one, because a larger economy generates more revenue to service and repay it. Nepal's public debt has risen to somewhere on the order of 40 to 45 percent of GDP in recent years — a figure to confirm against the official debt data, and one that has climbed markedly from the much lower levels of a decade or so ago, when it sat closer to a fifth of GDP. The debt is split, very roughly, between domestic and external borrowing.

Is a debt of around 40 to 45 percent of GDP "too much"? There is no single magic threshold, and anyone who quotes one as gospel is oversimplifying. Whether a debt is sustainable depends on several things, and laying them out gives you the real test of when borrowing becomes dangerous. It depends, first, on the *cost* of the debt: debt that is mostly concessional, at very low interest and long maturities, is far less burdensome than debt at high commercial rates, because the annual interest cost is lower and the repayment is spread out — and Nepal's external debt, being largely concessional, is in this respect comparatively benign. It depends, second, on what the borrowed money was *used for*: debt incurred to build productive assets — roads, power, irrigation — that raise the economy's future capacity to produce and therefore to generate revenue can be self-justifying, because the investment helps pay for itself; debt incurred merely to fund consumption — salaries, ordinary running costs — builds nothing to help repay it and is therefore far more worrying. It depends, third, on whether the debt is growing *faster or slower than the economy*: if the economy grows faster than the debt, the debt-to-GDP ratio falls over time and the burden eases; if the debt grows faster than the economy, the ratio climbs and the country is on a path toward trouble. And it depends, fourth, on the country's ability to *earn the foreign currency* needed to service foreign-currency debt, which for Nepal ties back to exports, remittances, and reserves.

Applying this test to Nepal yields a nuanced verdict, which is the honest one. On the reassuring side, Nepal's debt is, by the standards of many countries, still at a moderate level relative to GDP, and its external portion is largely concessional and therefore cheap to service. On the worrying side, the debt has been *rising fast* — roughly doubling as a share of GDP over a decade — and, crucially, a great deal of the borrowing has gone to fund spending that does not build productive assets, given the budget's heavy recurrent tilt and chronic capital under-execution that we examined in Part 6. Borrowing heavily and then failing to spend the capital budget that the borrowing was partly meant to fund is close to the worst of both worlds: the country takes on the debt and the future interest obligation, but does not get the productive assets that would have helped justify and repay it. So the careful assessment is neither panic nor complacency: Nepal's debt level is not yet alarming by international standards and its terms are favourable, but the *trajectory* — rapid growth, much of it not translated into productive investment — is the genuine concern, and it is the trajectory, not the current level alone, that a citizen should watch.

## Who does Nepal owe?

It helps to know the creditors, because to whom you owe money, and on what terms, shapes how dangerous the debt is. Nepal's debt has two halves, and they have different creditors.

The *external* half is owed largely to international financial institutions, on those favourable concessional terms. The two most important are the **World Bank** — specifically its arm that lends to the poorest countries on highly concessional terms — and the **Asian Development Bank**, a regional institution that is one of Nepal's largest development lenders. Alongside these multilateral institutions, Nepal owes money to individual foreign governments through bilateral loans — among them its giant neighbours and other development partners. The dominance of concessional, multilateral lending in Nepal's external debt is, as noted, a genuinely favourable feature: this is patient, cheap money, not the volatile, expensive borrowing from commercial markets that has plunged some other countries into crisis. The *domestic* half is owed within Nepal — chiefly to the country's banks and financial institutions, and to other holders of government bonds and bills, who have lent their money to the government in exchange for interest. This domestic debt is in rupees, free of currency risk, but it is the source of the crowding-out concern, because every rupee the banks lend to the government is a rupee not available to lend to private businesses.

Knowing the creditors sharpens the judgement. Because so much of Nepal's external debt is concessional and multilateral, Nepal is at far lower risk of the kind of sudden debt crisis that afflicts countries dependent on expensive, fickle commercial borrowing. But the domestic borrowing, growing to fill the deficit, presses on the limited pool of domestic credit and risks crowding out the private sector — which matters all the more because a thriving private sector is exactly what Nepal needs to grow its way out of its dependence on remittances and borrowing in the first place. The creditors are, on the whole, the right kind; the worry is less about who is owed and more about how fast the total is climbing and how little of it is building anything.

## The quiet tax of the past: interest

We come now to the most insidious consequence of debt, the one that connects this post directly back to the recurrent-spending problem of Part 6, and that a citizen most needs to understand: **interest**.

Every loan carries interest, and that interest must be paid every single year, for as long as the debt is outstanding, before the government spends a rupee on anything else. Interest on past borrowing is not a choice; it is a contractual obligation that comes due whether or not the economy is doing well, whether or not there is a flood or a crisis, whether or not the government would rather spend the money on schools or roads. And — this is the crucial link — interest payments are classified as *recurrent* spending. They are part of that nearly 60 percent of the budget that runs the state and builds nothing. So every year of borrowing adds not only to the debt stock but to the *permanent recurrent interest bill*, which grows as the debt grows and which crowds out, within the recurrent budget, the resources that might otherwise go to services — and which crowds out, within the budget as a whole, the capital spending that builds the future.

This creates the danger of a slow, self-reinforcing trap, and it is worth seeing clearly. A government runs a deficit and borrows to cover it. The borrowing adds to the debt. The larger debt means a larger annual interest bill. The larger interest bill, being recurrent and non-negotiable, consumes more of the budget, leaving less for everything else — including less for the productive capital spending that might grow the economy. With less productive investment, the economy grows more slowly, generating less revenue, which makes the deficit harder to close, which means more borrowing, which means yet more interest. Round and round it can go: borrowing to cover deficits, the deficits made harder to close by the very interest the borrowing creates. This is the mechanism by which a country can find an ever-larger share of its budget consumed simply by servicing the debts of the past, with ever less available to invest in its future. Nepal is not in an acute version of this trap — its concessional debt keeps the interest bill lower than it would otherwise be — but the rising trajectory of debt, combined with the failure to convert much of the borrowing into productive assets, pushes gently in this direction, and the interest line in the recurrent budget grows quietly each year, a permanent tax levied by the borrowing decisions of the past on the citizens of the present and future.

## A worked example: the interest that ate the schools

Make the interest trap concrete with a composite illustration of how borrowing decisions ripple into the future, because the mechanism is easier to feel than to state abstractly.

Imagine the government, in a given year, borrows a substantial sum to cover its deficit — and imagine, as Nepal's record suggests is all too possible, that much of the spending the borrowing funded was recurrent or that the capital projects it was meant to fund went largely unspent, so that the borrowing did not result in productive assets. The country now carries the new debt, and with it a new annual interest obligation — say, a certain number of billions of rupees that must be paid every year going forward. That interest obligation does not disappear; it joins the recurrent budget permanently. Five years later, a future Finance Minister sits down to write a budget and finds that this interest, accumulated from this and other years of borrowing, now consumes a meaningful slice of the recurrent budget — a slice that must be paid before anything else, and that is therefore unavailable for, say, building and staffing the new schools the country needs. The money that might have built schools is instead paying interest on past borrowing that itself built no schools. The borrowing of the past has, in effect, eaten the schools of the future — not through any dramatic act, but through the quiet, relentless arithmetic of interest on debt that was not invested productively.

Notice the moral of the example, which is the moral of the whole post. Borrowing is not free, and it is not neutral with respect to the future. Borrowing to build something that raises the economy's future capacity can be wise, because the asset helps generate the revenue to service and repay the debt — the borrowing pays for itself, or partly so. But borrowing to fund consumption, or borrowing to fund capital projects that then go unbuilt, is close to the worst thing a government can do with debt: it incurs the permanent interest obligation without acquiring the productive asset that would justify it, levying a quiet tax on every future budget in exchange for nothing durable. This is why, when you assess a deficit, the question is never simply "how big is it?" but "what is the borrowed money buying, and will what it buys help pay for itself?" A large deficit that is building productive infrastructure may be defensible; a smaller deficit that is funding consumption and unbuilt projects may be more worrying. The size of the borrowing matters less than what the borrowing does — and in Nepal's case, the answer to "what does it do" is, too often, "less than it should," which is the real reason the rising debt deserves concern.

## For the technically minded: the financing waterfall

For readers who work with numbers, the cleanest way to see how the deficit is filled is as a *waterfall* — starting from total spending and showing each source of funds stacking up to cover it, until the gap is closed. Here is the budget's financing logic laid out that way, in both currencies at our assumed rate.

```text
TOTAL SPENDING ......................................  2124.34  NPR billion   (USD ~14.16 bn)

  covered by:
  (1) Revenue (taxes + non-tax) .....................  1405.31  NPR billion   (USD ~9.37 bn)
  (2) Foreign grants (gifts, not repaid) ............    61.74  NPR billion   (USD ~0.41 bn)
      ---------------------------------------------------------------------
      Own resources + grants .........................  1467.05  NPR billion   (USD ~9.78 bn)

  DEFICIT (the gap that must be BORROWED) ...........   657.29  NPR billion   (USD ~4.38 bn)

  filled by borrowing:
  (3) Foreign loans (mostly concessional) ...........   247.28  NPR billion   (USD ~1.65 bn)
  (4) Domestic borrowing (gross) ....................   410.00  NPR billion   (USD ~2.73 bn)
      ---------------------------------------------------------------------
      Total new borrowing ............................   657.28  NPR billion   (≈ the deficit)

  note on domestic borrowing:
      of the 410.00 gross, ~245.89 just REPAYS maturing domestic debt (roll-over);
      genuinely NEW domestic debt added this year .....   164.11  NPR billion   (USD ~1.09 bn)
```

The waterfall makes several things visible at once that the speech presents gently. It shows that revenue plus grants cover only about 69 percent of spending, so nearly a third must be borrowed. It shows that grants are a small contributor and that the deficit is filled overwhelmingly by loans that must be repaid with interest. And it shows, in the note at the bottom, the roll-over subtlety — that a large part of the gross domestic borrowing merely repays old debt, so the net addition to the debt pile is smaller than the gross figure suggests. A reader who builds this waterfall, rather than reading the financing line by line, sees the true shape of how the budget is funded: substantially on borrowed money, mostly in loans, adding meaningfully to a debt stock whose trajectory is the real concern. And the same reader can extend the logic forward — every rupee in lines (3) and (4) becomes future principal to repay and future interest to pay, the interest landing permanently in the recurrent budget, year after year, long after this budget is forgotten.

## How budgets deceive: the hidden deficit

The technique of deception for this post concerns the deficit itself, and it is one of the most important to understand because the deficit is precisely the number a government most wishes to make look smaller than it is: **the hidden, or understated, deficit**.

The trick has several forms, all aimed at the same goal — making the gap between spending and revenue appear smaller, and the public finances appear healthier, than they really are. One form, which we met in the previous post, is *revenue optimism*: assume more revenue will be collected than is realistic, and the deficit on paper shrinks accordingly, even though the real gap is larger. A second form is the use of *labels and classifications* that obscure how much is really being borrowed or spent — for instance, the way the "financing" stream and the roll-over of debt can make the borrowing picture harder to read than a simple statement of the net new debt would be. A third form, common in many countries and worth watching for, is *off-budget* spending and borrowing — obligations kept off the main budget books, in public enterprises, in guarantees, in special vehicles, so that the headline deficit excludes them and looks smaller than the government's true financial commitments. A fourth is simply *emphasis*: presenting the deficit as a modest-sounding share of GDP, or burying it beneath the proud announcements of spending, so that the public's attention never settles on the fact that nearly a third of the budget is borrowed money. In each case the aim is the same — to let the government enjoy the political benefit of ambitious spending while minimising the political cost of acknowledging how much of that spending is funded by debt against the future.

The defence is to reconstruct the deficit yourself, from the spending and the revenue, rather than accepting the government's framing of it. Take total spending; subtract revenue and grants; what remains is what must be borrowed, regardless of how it is labelled or presented — and that, as our waterfall showed, is NPR 657.29 billion, nearly a third of the budget. Then ask the deeper questions: Does the revenue assumption look optimistic, which would mean the *real* deficit is even larger than the stated one? Are there obligations kept off the main books — in public enterprises, in guarantees — that the headline deficit excludes? How much is the debt actually growing in net terms this year, and how fast is the total debt rising relative to the economy? And, above all, what is the borrowed money buying — productive assets that will help repay it, or consumption and unbuilt projects that will leave only the interest bill behind? A citizen who reconstructs the deficit from first principles, refuses to be soothed by optimistic revenue or comfortable framing, and keeps asking what the borrowing actually buys, cannot be deceived about the most important fact a budget tries to soften: that this government, like most, plans to spend far more than it earns, and to commit the future to making up the difference.

## Where we go from here

We have followed the gap. You now know what a deficit is — the flow of borrowing in a single year, here about NPR 657 billion, nearly a third of all spending — and how it differs from debt, the accumulated stock of past borrowing. You know the difference between a grant, which is a gift, and a loan, which must be repaid with interest; and between foreign borrowing, which is often cheap and concessional but carries currency risk, and domestic borrowing, which avoids currency risk but crowds out the private sector. You understand what public debt is, that Nepal's stands on the order of 40 to 45 percent of GDP and has been rising fast, and that whether a debt is dangerous depends on its cost, its use, its growth relative to the economy, and the ability to service it. You know who Nepal owes — largely concessional multilateral lenders abroad, banks and bondholders at home — and why the favourable terms are reassuring while the trajectory is not. You understand the quiet tax of interest, classified as recurrent, growing with the debt, devouring the future budget. And you have met the deception of the hidden deficit, and the discipline of reconstructing the gap yourself and asking always what the borrowing buys.

We have now mapped both the spending side and the funding side of the budget — what it spends, how it divides that spending, where the money comes from, and how the gap is filled. In Part 9 we add the dimension we have so far set aside: the fact that Nepal has not one government but three tiers, and that a large share of this federal budget flows down to seven provinces and seven hundred and fifty-three local governments. We will learn what fiscal federalism is, how the transfers work, and why dividing money across three levels of government is at once a way of bringing power closer to the people and a source of great expense and dispute. All rupee figures are nominal unless stated otherwise.

The deficit (about NPR 657.29 billion), grants (NPR 61.74 billion), foreign loans (NPR 247.28 billion), gross domestic borrowing (NPR 410 billion), domestic repayment (about NPR 245.89 billion), and net new domestic borrowing (about NPR 164.11 billion) are from the budget and should be confirmed against the Ministry of Finance's financing tables; the deficit is computed here as total spending minus revenue minus grants. The total public debt figure, given as on the order of 40 to 45 percent of GDP, and the split between domestic and external debt, should be confirmed against the Public Debt Management Office's data; the precise ratio depends on the exact debt stock and GDP. The characterisation of Nepal's external debt as largely concessional is well established but the exact concessional share should be verified.

And the assumption beneath the whole series: a borrowing plan is an announced intention, and the deficit a planned figure that the year's actual revenue and spending will reshape — very possibly widening it if revenue falls short. The most important question about a deficit is not how large it is, but what the borrowed money buys — and whether what it buys will help the country repay it, or leave behind only the bill.
