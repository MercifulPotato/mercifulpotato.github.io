Of all the things Nepal might build its future on, one stands out as the country's single greatest natural economic advantage: water falling from great height. Nepal's vertical geography — the very thing that makes it so hard to build roads and so vulnerable to landslides — also gives it an extraordinary endowment of fast-flowing rivers descending from the Himalayas, and that descending water can be turned into electricity. Hydropower, the generation of electricity from moving water, is the resource that many Nepalis and many economists believe could transform the country: clean, renewable, domestically owned, potentially abundant, and — crucially for a country that earns its foreign currency by exporting its people — potentially *exportable* to its energy-hungry neighbours. If Nepal has a path to prosperity built on its own productive resources rather than on remittances and borrowing, electricity is the most plausible candidate.

This post examines what the budget does with that opportunity, and it contains one of the budget's most revealing internal tensions. We will look at the substantial allocation to energy, at Nepal's ambition to generate power and sell it abroad, and at the obstacles — above all transmission — that stand between the ambition and the reality. Then we will confront a genuine contradiction: a budget that, on one hand, stakes the country's future on *expanding* the use of electricity, while on the other hand *taxing* electricity consumption through the new 5 percent charge above 50 units we met in the tax post. And we will return, with the substance-over-label discipline we built, to the "Green Tax," to ask whether it is climate policy or revenue wearing environmental clothing. Finally we will set all this against Nepal's profound vulnerability to a changing climate it did almost nothing to cause. By the end you will understand both the promise of Nepal's greatest resource and the confused signals this budget sends about it.

## Why electricity is Nepal's great opportunity

To understand the stakes, you have to understand why electricity matters so much more for Nepal than the energy allocation's size alone might suggest. The reasons connect to nearly every economic vulnerability we have traced in this series.

Recall Nepal's predicament: a landlocked, low-income country that imports far more than it exports, depends on remittances and borrowing to pay for those imports, and lacks a strong base of its own foreign-currency-earning industry. Now consider what abundant domestic electricity could do for that predicament, on several fronts at once. First, it could *reduce imports*: a great deal of Nepal's import bill is energy itself — petroleum products, brought overland from India, paid for in scarce foreign currency. Every unit of domestic hydropower that replaces imported fuel — by electrifying cooking (replacing imported gas), transport (electric vehicles replacing petrol), and industry — directly reduces the import bill and the foreign-currency drain. Second, it could *power domestic industry*: reliable, affordable electricity is a precondition for manufacturing and for the productive economy Nepal needs to build, and chronic power shortages have historically held that economy back. Third, and most excitingly, it could become an *export*: Nepal's hydropower potential far exceeds its own domestic needs, which means surplus electricity could be *sold to its neighbours* — to energy-hungry India and, through India, potentially to Bangladesh — earning foreign currency by Nepal's own productive effort, the very thing the country most lacks. Electricity is thus not just another sector; it is potentially the engine that could address Nepal's import dependence, its industrial weakness, and its shortage of genuine exports, all at once. This is why it is fair to call it Nepal's greatest economic bet.

The scale of the underlying resource is genuinely large. Nepal's rivers hold hydropower potential measured in the tens of thousands of megawatts of economically feasible capacity — vastly more than the country currently generates or consumes. (The precise figures for theoretical, technical, and economically feasible potential vary by study and should be taken from authoritative energy-sector sources; the point is that the potential dwarfs current capacity.) Over recent years, Nepal has made real progress: installed generation capacity has grown substantially, the country has moved from chronic shortages and load-shedding (scheduled power cuts) toward periods of surplus, especially in the wet monsoon season when the rivers run high, and it has begun *exporting* surplus power to India, with arrangements being developed for larger volumes and for export onward to Bangladesh. This is real, tangible progress on the country's most promising resource, and it represents one of the genuine economic bright spots of recent Nepali development. The budget's energy allocation builds on this momentum.

## The energy allocation, and the transmission bottleneck

This budget allocates a substantial sum to the Ministry of Energy, Water Resources and Irrigation: about **NPR 114.02 billion (around USD 760 million)** — one of the larger ministry allocations, reflecting the priority placed on the sector. This money funds generation projects (building new hydropower capacity), transmission and distribution (the lines and infrastructure that carry electricity from where it is generated to where it is used and to the border for export), and irrigation (the water-management projects that share the ministry, important for agriculture). The allocation signals genuine commitment to the sector that is the country's best economic hope.

But here we meet the obstacle that, more than any other, stands between Nepal's hydropower ambition and its realisation, and it is essential to understand because it is so often overlooked in the excitement about generation: **transmission**. Generating electricity is only half the problem; the electricity must then be *carried* — from the remote mountain rivers where it is generated, across the country to the cities and industries that use it, and to the border to be exported. This carrying is done by transmission lines — the high-voltage infrastructure of towers and cables — and substations, and building this transmission infrastructure is itself enormously difficult and expensive, contending with the same vertical, landslide-prone terrain, the same land-acquisition disputes, and the same slow procurement that plague all construction in Nepal. The chronic problem is that *generation has often outpaced transmission*: hydropower projects have been built that cannot fully deliver their power because the transmission lines to carry it were not ready, so that electricity which could be used or sold is instead *wasted* — generation capacity sitting idle or "spilled" because there is no line to carry its output. This is a genuine and recurring bottleneck, and it means that the headline measure everyone watches — installed *generation* capacity, the megawatts built — can be misleading, because megawatts that cannot be transmitted are megawatts that deliver nothing. The real constraint on Nepal realising its hydropower potential is increasingly not generation but transmission (and, for exports, the cross-border transmission and the commercial and political arrangements to sell the power). A budget serious about hydropower must therefore be judged not only on the generation it funds but on whether it adequately funds and accelerates the transmission that turns generation into delivered, usable, sellable power — and a citizen should watch the transmission progress as closely as the generation headlines.

## The electricity export ambition

The most economically transformative part of Nepal's energy story is the possibility of *exporting* surplus electricity, and it deserves examination because it is both genuinely promising and genuinely contingent. The logic is compelling: Nepal's hydropower potential exceeds its domestic needs, especially in the wet season; its giant neighbour India has enormous and growing energy demand; and beyond India lies Bangladesh, also energy-hungry, reachable through Indian territory. So Nepal could, in principle, become an electricity exporter — earning foreign currency by selling clean power, exactly the kind of productive export the country most needs. Real progress has been made: Nepal has been exporting surplus power to India during the high-generation season, and arrangements have been developed toward larger and longer-term export volumes and toward export to Bangladesh through India. The vision of Nepal as a regional clean-energy exporter, its rivers powering the wider region and earning the country its keep, is one of the most hopeful in South Asian development.

But the honest analysis must note the contingencies, because export depends on factors beyond Nepal's control. Exporting electricity to and through India depends on *India's willingness* to buy Nepali power and to allow its transmission onward — a commercial and geopolitical relationship in which Nepal is the smaller party, dependent on the policies and goodwill of a much larger neighbour, exactly as it is for its trade routes generally. It depends on the *cross-border transmission infrastructure* being built and adequate. It depends on the *commercial terms* being favourable enough to make export profitable. And it depends on the *seasonality* of hydropower — Nepal generates a surplus in the wet season but may face shortfalls in the dry season, complicating its ability to be a reliable year-round exporter and sometimes requiring it to *import* power in the dry months even as it exports in the wet ones. So the export ambition, while real and promising, is contingent on relationships and infrastructure and terms that are not wholly in Nepal's hands, and a citizen should treat grand export targets with the same care we apply to all targets — as aspirations dependent on many things going right, not as accomplished facts. The promise is genuine; the realisation depends on India, on transmission, on terms, and on the seasons.

## The contradiction: taxing the thing you want to grow

Now we come to the budget's revealing internal tension, the one that connects this post directly to the tax post and that exemplifies the kind of inconsistency a careful reader looks for. On one hand, as we have just established, Nepal's entire energy strategy — its greatest economic hope — rests on *expanding* the use of electricity: electrifying homes, cooking, transport, and industry, substituting domestic clean power for imported fuel, and growing both domestic consumption and exportable surplus. Encouraging people to *use more electricity* (in place of imported fuel) is, broadly, a national goal. On the other hand, this very budget introduces the **5 percent VAT on electricity consumption above 50 units** that we met in the tax post — a tax that, by making electricity more expensive above a threshold, *discourages* electricity consumption. The budget is, in effect, taxing the very thing its energy strategy is trying to promote.

Let us examine this tension fairly, because there is a defence and there is a critique, and a careful citizen should weigh both. The *defence* is twofold. First, the tax spares the smallest users: by applying only above 50 units, it leaves the basic consumption of the poorest, lowest-using households untaxed, so the disincentive falls only on larger users who can better afford it — making the measure progressive in its design and protecting the vulnerable. Second, the tax raises *revenue*, which a deficit budget needs, and one might argue that taxing the consumption of larger electricity users is a reasonable way to raise it, especially as electricity use grows. So the measure is not indefensible: it is designed to spare the poor and to raise needed revenue.

The *critique*, however, is real and goes to coherence. A government that genuinely wished to *maximise* the substitution of domestic electricity for imported fuel — to get households cooking with electricity instead of imported gas, driving electric vehicles instead of burning imported petrol, and powering industry with domestic hydropower — would presumably want electricity to be *as cheap as possible* relative to the imported fuels it is meant to replace, to tip the economic calculation in electricity's favour. *Taxing* electricity consumption pushes in the opposite direction: it makes electricity more expensive, which at the margin discourages exactly the switch from imported fuel to domestic power that the energy strategy is trying to encourage. The contradiction is that one part of the budget (the energy strategy) wants to grow electricity use, while another part (this tax) discourages it — and the two are not obviously reconciled. A defender might respond that the 50-unit threshold means the tax only bites on consumption well above basic needs, where the substitution incentive is less critical, or that the revenue need justifies a modest disincentive. But the tension remains a genuine one, and it is exactly the kind of internal inconsistency — one hand promoting what the other hand taxes — that reveals a budget's priorities to be less perfectly aligned than its rhetoric suggests. Whether the modest revenue and the protection of small users justify taxing a resource the country is otherwise trying to promote is a real question, and the contradiction is worth seeing clearly rather than glossing over.

## The Green Tax, revisited

We met the **Green Tax** in the tax post and flagged it for skeptical examination; here, in the context of energy and climate, we can complete that examination, because this is its proper home. Recall what it is: a new levy that *consolidates* two existing charges collected at customs — an infrastructure development tax (historically on imported petroleum) and a road maintenance fee — under a single, environmentally-branded name. We applied the substance-over-label discipline and asked whether the "green" name reflected green substance. In the energy-and-climate context, we can sharpen the analysis.

There is a *genuine environmental logic* available for a tax on petroleum: burning fossil fuels emits the carbon that drives climate change, so taxing fuel raises the cost of carbon-emitting consumption and, in principle, discourages it — a standard tool of climate policy known as carbon pricing. To the extent the Green Tax falls on petroleum, it has a real environmental dimension, and could legitimately be called "green" in that sense. Moreover, there is a *coherence* with the energy strategy here that the electricity tax lacked: taxing imported fuel while promoting domestic electricity tips the economic balance *toward* electricity and *away* from fuel, which is exactly what the substitution strategy wants — so a fuel tax is, unlike the electricity tax, *aligned* with the goal of electrification. Viewed this way, a tax on petroleum could be both green and strategically coherent.

But the skepticism remains warranted on the same grounds as before, and the energy context does not dispel it. The questions are: Is the Green Tax actually a *new or higher* charge designed to discourage emissions, or merely a *consolidation at a similar level* of levies that already existed and were already being collected for revenue and road maintenance? If it is substantially a rebranding — the same money, collected as before, now under a virtuous name — then its environmental effect is whatever the pre-existing levies already had, and the "green" label adds marketing rather than substance. Where does the revenue actually *go* — to environmental and climate purposes (as a genuine green tax's revenue arguably should), or into general revenue and road maintenance as before? And does consolidating the levies change the *incentive* at all, or merely the name? If the answers reveal a rebranding of existing fuel and road charges, with the revenue flowing where it always did, then the Green Tax is largely the old levies in green clothing — defensible as a fuel tax with some environmental dimension, but not the bold new climate measure its name implies. The honest verdict is nuanced: a tax on fuel does have a real environmental logic and does cohere with the electrification strategy, so the "green" label is not baseless; but if the substance is mostly consolidation and rebranding of existing charges, the label overstates the novelty and the environmental ambition, and a citizen should judge it as what it substantively is — a consolidated fuel and road levy with a green name — rather than as the climate policy the name suggests.

## Climate vulnerability: the cruel irony

No honest discussion of energy and the "green" framing in Nepal can omit the larger climate context, which is one of profound and cruel irony, and which the budget's environmental measures should be set against. Nepal is among the countries *most vulnerable* to climate change in the world, and yet among those that have contributed *least* to causing it. Both halves of that sentence matter.

Nepal contributes almost negligibly to global greenhouse-gas emissions — it is a poor country with little heavy industry and low per-person emissions, a tiny fraction of the global total. The carbon driving climate change has been emitted overwhelmingly by the wealthy, industrialised nations and the large emerging economies, not by Nepal. And yet Nepal suffers the consequences acutely, because of its geography. Its Himalayan glaciers are melting as the planet warms, which threatens the long-term water supply of the rivers on which its agriculture, its drinking water, and its hydropower all depend — a slow-moving catastrophe for a country whose economy rests on those rivers. The melting also creates the acute danger of *glacial lake outburst floods* — when meltwater pools behind unstable natural dams of ice and rock in the high mountains and then bursts catastrophically, sending walls of water down the valleys with devastating force, a hazard that climate change is intensifying. Climate change is making the *monsoon* more erratic — with more intense and unpredictable rainfall, more destructive floods and landslides in a country already prone to them, and disruptions to the agricultural cycle on which most Nepalis depend. So Nepal faces severe, escalating climate harm — to its water, its agriculture, its infrastructure, and the safety of its people — caused overwhelmingly by emissions it did not produce. This is the deep injustice at the heart of the global climate problem, and Nepal is one of its sharpest examples.

This context bears on the budget in two ways. First, it means that genuine climate measures — real adaptation to the coming harms (protecting against floods and glacial-lake bursts, securing water, building resilience) and real mitigation (the clean hydropower that reduces fuel use) — are not optional luxuries for Nepal but matters of survival, which makes the seriousness or cosmetic-ness of the budget's "green" framing genuinely important rather than merely rhetorical. A country this exposed needs real climate action, not green labels on revenue measures. Second, it underlies Nepal's position in international *climate finance* — the principle, established in global climate negotiations, that the wealthy nations who caused the problem should help fund the adaptation and clean development of the poor nations who suffer it. Nepal, as a low-emitting, highly-vulnerable country, has a strong moral claim on such international climate finance, and pursuing it is a legitimate and important part of the country's strategy for funding both adaptation and clean energy. So the climate context both raises the stakes of the budget's environmental seriousness and points to an external source of funding that Nepal can rightly seek. A citizen evaluating the budget's "green" measures should hold this context in mind: for a country this vulnerable, the difference between real climate action and cosmetic green branding is not a matter of marketing but, potentially, of survival.

## A worked example: a household crossing the threshold, and a stranded megawatt

Make the energy tensions concrete with two linked illustrations — one at the household level, one at the system level.

First, the household and the electricity tax. Imagine a family that, encouraged by the national push toward electrification and the availability of grid power, decides to do exactly what the energy strategy wants: to switch from an imported-gas cookstove to electric cooking, and perhaps to charge an electric scooter, reducing their use of imported fuel. Doing so naturally *increases* their electricity consumption — quite possibly pushing it above the 50-unit threshold. And the moment it does, they encounter the new 5 percent VAT on consumption above 50 units, which makes their increased electricity use more expensive than it would otherwise be. So the family that responds to the electrification strategy by using more electricity is met by a tax on that very increase. The 50-unit threshold means the smallest users are spared, but a family making exactly the fuel-to-electricity switch the strategy wants is likely to cross it and be taxed. The contradiction is lived, at the household level: the budget urges electrification with one hand and taxes the resulting electricity use with the other, and the family feels both at once. Whether the modest tax meaningfully discourages the switch, or is small enough to be ignored, is an empirical question — but the signal is mixed, and that mixed signal is the point.

Second, the system and the transmission bottleneck. Imagine a new hydropower project, funded and built, that comes online in a remote valley, capable of generating a substantial number of megawatts of clean electricity — a success for the generation strategy, the kind of project that makes the headlines and the ribbon-cuttings. But imagine that the transmission line needed to carry that power from the remote valley to the grid and the cities was not completed in time — delayed by land disputes, difficult terrain, or slow procurement, the usual obstacles. The result is a *stranded megawatt*: electricity that *could* be generated and used or sold, but that cannot be carried, and so is wasted — the turbines spinning below capacity, the potential power spilled, because there is no line to take it where it is needed. The project counts in the headline generation figures; it adds to the megawatts "built." But it delivers far less than its capacity, because transmission lagged generation. This is the bottleneck made concrete, and it illustrates why a citizen must look past the generation headlines to ask whether the transmission to *deliver* the power was also built. A megawatt that cannot be transmitted is a megawatt that powers nothing, however proudly its generation was announced — and the gap between megawatts built and megawatts delivered is exactly the kind of distinction that separates real energy progress from headline energy progress.

## For the technically minded: energy line items and the electricity-VAT step

For readers who work with numbers, here is the energy allocation in schematic form, followed by a sketch of how the electricity tax actually works at the 50-unit threshold — which reveals its progressive design and its contradiction with electrification.

The energy allocation (the breakdown into generation, transmission, distribution, and irrigation should be taken from the Ministry of Finance and the energy-sector documents; the headline is the ministry total):

| Energy-sector element | Note |
|---|---|
| Ministry of Energy, Water Resources and Irrigation total | NPR 114.02 billion (USD ~0.76B) |
| Generation (new hydropower capacity) | Builds megawatts; the headline measure |
| Transmission and distribution | Carries power; the binding bottleneck — watch this |
| Irrigation | Shares the ministry; vital for agriculture |
| Electricity exports (to India; toward Bangladesh) | Contingent on India, transmission, terms, seasons |

Now the electricity-VAT step, showing how the 50-unit threshold makes the tax progressive while still discouraging the consumption electrification wants to grow:

```text
# 5% VAT applies only to electricity consumption ABOVE 50 units.
def electricity_bill(units, price_per_unit, vat_rate=0.05, free_threshold=50):
    base_cost = units * price_per_unit
    taxable_units = max(0, units - free_threshold)   # only consumption above 50 units is taxed
    vat = taxable_units * price_per_unit * vat_rate
    return base_cost + vat

# A small user (<= 50 units) pays NO new VAT -> protects the poorest. Progressive design.
small_user  = electricity_bill(40, price_per_unit)   # vat = 0
# A family that electrifies cooking/transport uses MORE -> crosses 50 -> gets taxed.
big_user    = electricity_bill(120, price_per_unit)  # vat on 70 units

# The tension: the energy STRATEGY wants families to do exactly what 'big_user' did
# (use more electricity, replacing imported fuel) -- but the TAX penalises that very increase.
# Protecting small users (good) and discouraging the fuel-to-electricity switch (bad) at once.
```

The calculation makes both halves of the tension visible at once. The threshold genuinely protects the smallest, poorest users, who pay no new tax — a real progressive virtue. But the same threshold means that any household responding to the electrification strategy by using more electricity (to replace imported fuel) crosses into the taxed zone and is penalised for the increase — the contradiction made arithmetic. A developer or analyst modelling the policy would see immediately that it pulls in two directions: progressive protection of small users, and a disincentive to exactly the consumption growth the energy strategy wants. That is the kind of internal tension that the budget's confident rhetoric about both electrification and revenue obscures, and that totalling and modelling the actual policy reveals.

## How budgets deceive: green-washing and the megawatt mirage

This post offers two related techniques of deception: **green-washing** — attaching environmental virtue to measures that are substantially about revenue — and the **megawatt mirage** — celebrating generation capacity built while ignoring whether it can actually be delivered.

The first, *green-washing*, is the substance-versus-label deception applied to environmental branding, and the Green Tax is its case study. As we have discussed, attaching the word "green" to a tax invokes the moral authority of climate responsibility and discourages scrutiny — who would oppose a green tax? But if the substance is largely a consolidation and rebranding of existing fuel and road levies, collected and spent much as before, the "green" label does marketing rather than environmental work. The deception is especially potent for a country like Nepal, so genuinely vulnerable to climate change, because the public rightly *wants* climate action, which makes them receptive to anything labelled green — and that receptivity can be exploited by branding ordinary revenue measures as climate policy. The defence, as always, is to judge by substance: ask of any "green" measure whether it actually changes environmental incentives, whether it is new or merely renamed, where its revenue actually goes, and whether it would deserve its green name if the name were stripped away. Real climate measures — genuine carbon pricing, genuine adaptation spending, genuine clean-energy investment — earn the label; rebranded revenue measures do not, however virtuous their titles. For a country facing the climate harms Nepal faces, telling real green action from green-washing is not a matter of marketing but of whether the country is actually preparing for the catastrophe bearing down on it.

The second, the *megawatt mirage*, is specific to energy but illustrates a general deception: celebrating an *input or capacity* while ignoring the *output or delivery*. Generation capacity — megawatts built — is a vivid, announceable, ribbon-cuttable number, and governments love to celebrate the hydropower they have "added." But, as we have seen, megawatts that cannot be *transmitted* deliver nothing — they are stranded, spilled, wasted, because the lines to carry them were not built. So a government can celebrate impressive generation figures while the actual *delivered* power — electricity that genuinely reaches homes, industries, and export markets — lags far behind, held back by the unglamorous transmission that no one cuts a ribbon for. The deception is in pointing to the headline generation capacity as though it were delivered power, when the binding constraint and the real measure of progress is delivery, not generation. The defence is to ask, of any energy achievement, not "how many megawatts were generated or built?" but "how much power was actually *delivered* — used, or sold — and was the transmission to carry it also built?" A citizen who watches delivered power rather than installed capacity, and who asks about transmission as insistently as about generation, cannot be fooled by the megawatt mirage — and will judge Nepal's hydropower progress by what its rivers actually power, not by what its turbines could theoretically produce if only the lines existed to carry it away.
