## Part 1: Making the Abstract Concrete

Throughout this series, we have referred to Nepal's "infrastructure gap" in somewhat abstract terms. This article makes it concrete. We will examine the actual numbers — where Nepal is today, where it needs to be, what it would cost to get there, and where the money can come from. This foundation is essential for evaluating whether the Alternative Development Finance Fund can make a meaningful difference.

Before we proceed, a note on data quality. Infrastructure investment data in Nepal is imperfect. Numbers from different sources (government, multilateral banks, academic studies) sometimes differ significantly. Exchange rates fluctuate. Some estimates are optimistic; others are conservative. Where possible, we use ranges rather than single-point estimates, and we note the source and limitations of specific numbers. The goal is not false precision but honest orientation.

## Part 2: The Electricity Sector

### Where Nepal Is

Nepal's installed electricity generation capacity as of recent years is approximately 2,500 to 3,000 megawatts (MW). This includes:

- Hydropower: the dominant source, accounting for over 95% of installed capacity
- Solar: a rapidly growing but still small contribution
- Thermal: minimal

The 2,500 to 3,000 MW figure represents current installed capacity — the maximum the system can produce under ideal conditions. Actual generation varies significantly with the season. In the wet season (June through November, the monsoon), river flows are high and generation is at or near capacity. In the dry season (December through May), river flows drop substantially and many run-of-river hydropower plants operate at 20% to 40% of their rated capacity. This seasonal variation is one of Nepal's central electricity planning challenges.

Net electricity exports to India during recent years have been significant and growing — Nepal now earns foreign exchange from electricity exports, a transformation from the load-shedding era of just a decade ago. But domestic demand is also growing rapidly, and the long-term goal is to serve much larger domestic industrial and residential demand while also exporting.

### Where Nepal Needs to Be

The Sixteenth Five-Year Plan sets a target of 40,000 MW of electricity generation capacity. The government has also articulated an electricity export target of 10,000 MW to India in the medium term.

The gap between current capacity (~2,750 MW) and the target (40,000 MW) is approximately 37,250 MW — roughly 13 times current capacity. This is not a linear expansion; it is a transformation.

### What It Would Cost

Nepal's hydropower construction costs vary enormously by project type, location, and size. Small hydropower projects in accessible locations might cost $1,000 to $1,500 per kilowatt (kW) of installed capacity. Large storage projects in remote Himalayan valleys might cost $3,000 to $5,000 per kW or more. Run-of-river projects of medium size in accessible locations typically fall in the $1,500 to $2,500 per kW range.

Using a blended average of approximately $1,500 to $2,000 per kW for a realistic mix of project types:

- 37,250 MW additional capacity × $1,500/kW = approximately $55.9 billion
- 37,250 MW additional capacity × $2,000/kW = approximately $74.5 billion

At current exchange rates, these figures represent approximately Rs. 7.5 trillion to Rs. 10 trillion for generation capacity alone.

Add transmission infrastructure to move power from generation sites to load centers: approximately $500 to $800 per kW of transmission capacity, suggesting another Rs. 2 trillion to Rs. 3 trillion.

Add distribution infrastructure to bring power to end users: another Rs. 1 trillion to Rs. 2 trillion.

Total electricity sector investment requirement to reach the 40,000 MW target: approximately Rs. 10 trillion to Rs. 15 trillion over the next 10 to 20 years. This is Rs. 500 billion to Rs. 1.5 trillion per year.

Current annual investment in Nepal's electricity sector (from all sources — government budget, multilateral loans, private investment, Chinese and Indian investment) is approximately Rs. 100 billion to Rs. 200 billion per year. The gap between current investment and the investment needed to reach the target is enormous — even if electricity investment doubles, it would take decades longer than the plan envisions.

### What Role the ADFF Can Play

If the Alternative Development Finance Fund achieves its initial paid-up capital of Rs. 25 billion and operates with a leverage ratio of 4:1 (total portfolio four times equity), it can support approximately Rs. 100 billion in electricity sector investment — perhaps two to three major hydropower projects. This is meaningful but modest relative to the total need. The fund's real value is not its absolute scale but its role as a catalytic instrument: demonstrating financing structures that others can replicate, de-risking early investments to attract subsequent private capital, and building the institutional knowledge base for Nepal's infrastructure finance.

## Part 3: The Road Network

### Where Nepal Is

Nepal's total road network as of recent data is approximately 30,000 to 35,000 kilometers. Of this:

- Strategic roads (national and provincial highways): approximately 12,000 to 14,000 km
- District roads: approximately 10,000 to 12,000 km
- Urban roads: approximately 3,000 to 5,000 km
- Local roads (mostly rural, often unpaved): approximately 5,000 to 10,000 km

A critical distinction: "road" in Nepal includes many unpaved tracks, seasonal roads, and dirt paths that are not usable year-round or cannot handle significant freight. The all-weather paved road network is significantly smaller than the total figure.

Nepal has no operational rapid transit system. Kathmandu's only metro or BRT (Bus Rapid Transit) project has been studied for years but not built. The valley's transportation relies on private vehicles and informal bus/minibus services.

### Where Nepal Needs to Be

The government's transport development objectives include:
- Completing the East-West Highway (Mahendra Highway) upgrades and missing links
- Completing the Mid-Hill Highway connecting east and west through the hills
- Building north-south corridors connecting the Terai to hill districts
- Connecting all district headquarters by all-weather road
- Building ring roads around major cities
- Building expressways and tunnels to improve Kathmandu Valley connectivity

### What It Would Cost

Road construction costs in Nepal vary from approximately Rs. 15 million to Rs. 20 million per kilometer in flat Terai areas to Rs. 80 million to Rs. 200 million per kilometer in hill areas, and even higher for major tunnel projects (which can cost Rs. 500 million to Rs. 2 billion per kilometer for complex tunnels).

The Department of Roads estimates that completing priority road projects over the next decade would require approximately Rs. 1.5 trillion to Rs. 2 trillion. This does not include urban roads, local roads, or maintenance of the existing network.

Current annual road investment from government sources is approximately Rs. 80 billion to Rs. 120 billion. The gap between investment needed and investment available is substantial.

## Part 4: Aviation Infrastructure

### The Kathmandu Airport Problem

Tribhuvan International Airport (TIA) in Kathmandu is one of the most constrained major airports in the world. It has a single runway (10/28), surrounded by hills, with significant approach restrictions. Aircraft must navigate through the Kathmandu Valley bowl, with high terrain on all sides limiting approach angles and creating operational constraints in low visibility. The airport handles approximately 4 to 5 million passengers per year but was designed for a fraction of that traffic. Runway capacity is approximately 200 aircraft movements per day; actual movements approach or exceed this on busy days.

Extending TIA is not practical given its geographic constraints. The solution — a new large airport — has been planned for decades but not built.

### Nijgadh International Airport

The proposed Nijgadh International Airport in Bara district in the Terai has been in planning since the early 2000s. It would have two runways, could handle large aircraft, and would be located in flat Terai terrain with no approach restrictions. It would provide a second gateway to Nepal and potentially serve as a hub for regional aviation.

The project has been controversial due to:
- Environmental impact: the original site would require clearing approximately 2.4 million trees, raising serious environmental concerns and opposition from environmental groups
- Cost: estimates have ranged from Rs. 200 billion to Rs. 700 billion, depending on the design and phasing
- Financing: no credible financing plan has been finalized despite years of planning

### Regional Airports

Nepal has approximately 45 regional airports and airstrips, serving communities throughout the hills and mountains. Many are basic — short unpaved runways, minimal facilities, dependent on small propeller aircraft. They are lifelines for communities where roads do not exist but are operationally challenging and commercially marginal.

The investment needed to upgrade Nepal's aviation infrastructure — completing Nijgadh, upgrading regional airports, building airside and landside infrastructure — is estimated at Rs. 300 billion to Rs. 500 billion over ten years.

## Part 5: Water Supply and Sanitation

Nepal faces significant water supply and sanitation challenges, particularly in urban areas.

The Kathmandu Valley receives approximately 450 to 470 million liters of water per day from all sources (Melamchi Water Supply Project, groundwater, surface water). The valley's population requires perhaps 600 to 700 million liters per day for adequate supply. The gap creates rationing, dependence on expensive tanker water for poor households, and significant over-extraction of groundwater that is causing ground subsidence in parts of the valley.

The recently completed Melamchi Water Supply Project (a major water diversion tunnel bringing water from the Sindhupalchowk district) was Nepal's largest infrastructure project for decades — it cost approximately Rs. 60 billion and took over 15 years from inception to completion, repeatedly delayed by technical challenges and financing issues. It now provides a significant additional supply, but the distribution network in Kathmandu needs substantial upgrade to deliver the water effectively.

Outside the Kathmandu Valley, water supply and sanitation infrastructure varies greatly. Open defecation has been significantly reduced (Nepal was declared "open defecation free" in 2019), but basic sanitation infrastructure, piped water supply, and wastewater treatment remain inadequate in many areas.

Total investment needed in water and sanitation across Nepal over the next decade: approximately Rs. 500 billion to Rs. 700 billion.

## Part 6: Urban Infrastructure

Nepal is urbanizing rapidly. The proportion of the population living in urban areas has grown from approximately 15% in 2001 to over 20% in recent surveys, and the urban population is projected to continue growing.

Urban infrastructure needs include:
- Urban roads and traffic management
- Public transportation (the Kathmandu Valley needs a light rail or BRT system urgently)
- Solid waste management (Kathmandu's waste management is among the most inadequate in South Asia)
- Drainage and flood management (the valley and many Terai cities suffer from severe flooding during monsoon)
- Urban parks and public spaces
- Digital infrastructure (fiber optic networks for government services and businesses)

The investment needed in urban infrastructure across Nepal over the next ten years is estimated at Rs. 1 trillion to Rs. 2 trillion.

## Part 7: The Total Picture — The Investment Gap

Summing up the rough estimates across sectors:

| Sector | Estimated 10-Year Investment Need (Rs. billion) | Current Annual Investment Rate (Rs. billion) | Annual Gap (Rs. billion) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electricity | 1,000 – 1,500 | 100 – 200 | 700 – 1,200 |
| Roads | 1,500 – 2,000 | 80 – 120 | 600 – 1,100 |
| Aviation | 300 – 500 | 10 – 20 | 20 – 30 |
| Water and Sanitation | 500 – 700 | 30 – 50 | 20 – 40 |
| Urban Infrastructure | 1,000 – 2,000 | 50 – 100 | 50 – 100 |
| **Total** | **4,300 – 6,700** | **270 – 490** | **1,390 – 2,470** |

The annual investment gap — the difference between what Nepal needs and what it currently invests — is roughly Rs. 1.5 trillion to Rs. 2.5 trillion per year. This is consistent with the Act's own recitation that Nepal needs "more than Rs. 100 kharba" (Rs. 10 trillion) over the coming decade.

## Part 8: Where Can the Money Come From?

Given this enormous gap, where can the financing come from? The realistic sources are:

**Government budget:** Nepal's total government budget is approximately Rs. 1.5 trillion to Rs. 2 trillion per year. Capital expenditure (investment spending) is typically around Rs. 300 billion to Rs. 400 billion, of which infrastructure gets a portion. The government can increase capital expenditure, but it is constrained by revenue (which is growing but slowly), borrowing limits, and debt sustainability concerns.

**Multilateral development banks:** The World Bank, ADB, AIIB, and other multilateral banks collectively commit approximately Rs. 100 billion to Rs. 200 billion per year to Nepal in loans and grants. This is significant but finite.

**Bilateral creditors:** India, China, Japan, and other bilateral creditors provide infrastructure loans. China has been particularly active (primarily in road and energy), as has India. These commitments are variable and subject to geopolitical considerations.

**Private investment (domestic):** Nepal's private sector — domestic companies, private banks, capital market investors — has historically invested relatively little in large infrastructure beyond distribution companies and small hydropower. Building the frameworks to attract more domestic private capital is one of the key objectives of the Act.

**Private investment (foreign):** Foreign private investment in Nepal's infrastructure — primarily in hydropower by Indian and Chinese companies — has grown but remains limited compared to the investment needed.

**Diaspora investment:** As discussed, remittances are large, but channeling a portion into productive investment through the remittance fund mechanism is a theoretical opportunity that has not yet been practically realized.

**The Alternative Development Finance Fund:** The fund, with its initial capacity of approximately Rs. 100 billion in total portfolio (based on Rs. 25 billion equity and 4:1 leverage), is one important additional source. Over time, if it demonstrates success, raises additional equity, and develops its capacity to attract co-investment, it could grow into a significantly larger source. But even a well-functioning fund will cover only a fraction of the total gap.

The honest conclusion is this: Nepal's infrastructure gap cannot be closed by any single institution or mechanism. It requires a comprehensive, coordinated strategy across all financing sources simultaneously, with the Alternative Development Finance Fund playing a meaningful catalytic role rather than a dominant one. The fund's most important contribution may not be its own direct investment but its demonstration effect — showing that Nepal's infrastructure can be financed through sophisticated instruments, attracting more private capital to replicate and scale successful models.
