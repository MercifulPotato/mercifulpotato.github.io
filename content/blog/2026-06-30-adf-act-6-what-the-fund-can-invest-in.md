## Part 1: Why Investment Rules Matter

In any investment institution, the rules that define what can and cannot be invested in are among the most consequential provisions. Too narrow, and the institution cannot deploy its capital effectively because too few eligible projects exist. Too broad, and the institution can invest in almost anything, creating opportunities for politically motivated decisions, misallocation of resources, and the kind of "everything to everyone" mandate that historically produces poor results.

The Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 sets out its investment rules in Sections 4 and 5. Section 4 defines the sectors and projects eligible for investment. Section 5 defines categories of projects that are explicitly excluded. Together, these two sections constitute the investment mandate of the fund.

This article examines both sets of rules in detail — not just describing what they say, but analyzing why each rule exists, what it is trying to achieve, and where the tensions and risks lie.

## Part 2: The Eligible Sectors — What Can Be Funded

Section 4, Subsection 1 lists the sectors in which the fund may invest. The list is comprehensive and covers the major physical infrastructure categories that constrain Nepal's development. Let us go through each one.

### Energy Development and Electricity Generation

The Act specifically lists "urja bikas ra bidyut utpadan" — energy development and electricity generation. This is the most important category in the Nepali context.

Nepal's hydropower potential is, as noted in earlier articles, enormous — estimated at over 80,000 megawatts of theoretical potential, with perhaps 40,000 to 50,000 megawatts practically exploitable. The Sixteenth Plan targets 40,000 megawatts of generation capacity. The fund is being created, in part, specifically to help finance this target.

Hydropower projects are capital-intensive, long-lived, and geographically challenging. A typical medium-sized hydropower project (100 to 300 megawatts) requires hundreds of millions of dollars in investment, has a construction period of three to seven years, and then operates for 30 to 50 years generating steady revenues. The revenue comes from electricity sales — to the Nepal Electricity Authority (the state utility), to industrial consumers, or through electricity export to India or Bangladesh.

The financing challenge for hydropower is the mismatch between when money is spent (all during construction, before a single watt is generated) and when revenue arrives (steadily over decades of operation). This makes pure equity investment difficult — the wait for returns is too long. It also makes pure commercial bank debt difficult — banks want to lend for five to seven years, but hydropower projects cannot service large debt from the limited early revenues of a new project. Development finance — whether from multilateral banks, government programs, or a fund like the one this Act creates — bridges this mismatch by providing patient capital at terms compatible with the project's financial structure.

### Electricity Transmission and Distribution

Generating electricity is only half the challenge. The electricity must be transmitted from the generation site (often in remote mountain valleys) to where it will be consumed (cities and the Terai) through transmission lines, and then distributed to end users through the distribution network.

Nepal's transmission infrastructure is critically underdeveloped relative to its generation ambitions. Even today, Nepal exports surplus electricity to India during the wet season (when rivers are full and generation is high) but does not have adequate transmission capacity to distribute that electricity within the country at all times. Building the transmission backbone — the high-voltage lines that connect generation sites to major load centers — requires substantial investment in challenging terrain.

Distribution infrastructure — the medium and low voltage lines, transformers, and connections that bring electricity to homes and businesses — also requires investment, particularly in rural areas.

### Road Construction and Expansion

Roads are, quite simply, the most transformative infrastructure for rural economic development. When a community gets connected to the road network, transportation costs for agricultural products fall, markets become accessible, healthcare becomes reachable, children can attend school without hours of walking, and economic activity diversifies. Decades of development research consistently shows that road connectivity is one of the highest-return infrastructure investments in rural developing countries.

Nepal's road network, while growing, remains inadequate. The terrain makes road construction expensive — a kilometer of road in the hills can cost many times more than a kilometer on flat ground. Many areas still lack all-weather roads, meaning they are effectively cut off during monsoon season when dirt tracks become impassable. The ambitious "mid-hill highway" (the Prithvi Rajmarg expansion project connecting east and west Nepal through the middle hills) has been under construction for decades.

The fund's ability to invest in road construction is important because it allows private capital to be channeled into road projects — either as toll roads (where user fees provide the revenue stream to service investment) or as public-private partnerships where the government provides a revenue guarantee.

### Railway Construction and Expansion

Nepal's railway network is minimal — essentially a small narrow-gauge line in the Terai linking a few towns near the Indian border. By South Asian standards, where India has an extensive rail network and Bangladesh has been expanding its own, Nepal is dramatically under-railed.

The government has ambitious plans for railway development: a cross-country east-west railway in the Terai, and potentially a high-altitude north-south railway connecting the Terai to Kathmandu (and eventually, in the most ambitious versions, to the Chinese railway network that has reached Lhasa and is planned for expansion toward the Nepal border).

Railway investment is even more capital-intensive than road investment, and the revenue model is complex. The fund's inclusion of railway projects signals ambition, but railway investments at this scale would likely require international financing partnerships well beyond what the fund can provide alone.

### Airport Construction and Improvement

Nepal's aviation infrastructure is constrained by geography and historical underinvestment. Tribhuvan International Airport in Kathmandu handles several million passengers per year but is at capacity and faces significant operational constraints. Regional airports serve smaller communities but many are basic or seasonal.

The proposed Nijgadh International Airport in the Terai has been in planning and controversy for years — planned as a large international hub that would reduce Kathmandu's dominance and open up the Terai to direct international connectivity, but questioned due to environmental concerns (large-scale deforestation) and financing uncertainty.

### Tunnel Road Construction and Expansion

Nepal is building tunnels. Several major tunnel road projects are underway or planned — connecting the Kathmandu Valley to outlying areas, reducing travel times from hours to minutes, and enabling year-round connectivity where seasonal landslides previously blocked roads for months. Tunnels are expensive to build but transformative in impact. The fund is authorized to invest in tunnel road construction and operation.

### Special Economic Zones, Industrial Parks, and Dry Ports

"Vishesh arthik kshetra, audyogik park, suvidha bandargarh" — special economic zones, industrial parks, and convenience ports (dry ports) — are infrastructure for industrial and commercial development rather than pure physical connectivity.

A special economic zone (SEZ) is a designated area with special regulations — often including tax incentives, simplified customs procedures, and infrastructure investments — designed to attract foreign and domestic manufacturing investment. Nepal has been developing SEZs (the Special Economic Zone Act was passed earlier), but the infrastructure within and serving the zones needs investment.

Industrial parks aggregate manufacturing and processing industries, providing shared infrastructure (power, water, waste treatment, roads, telecommunications) that individual small or medium enterprises could not afford on their own.

Dry ports are inland facilities that provide customs clearance and freight logistics services, reducing the cost and time of trade for landlocked countries like Nepal that cannot use seaports directly.

### Information Technology Parks and Special Tourism and Sports Infrastructure

"Suchana pradwigi park" (IT parks), "vishesh paryatan purwadhar" (special tourism infrastructure), and "vishesh khelkud purwadhar" (special sports infrastructure) represent the more service-sector-oriented investments the fund can make.

IT parks provide subsidized, high-quality physical and digital infrastructure for technology companies. Nepal has a growing technology sector, and IT parks in Kathmandu and other cities could help it expand. Tourism infrastructure — cable cars, visitor centers, trekking route infrastructure, mountain rescue facilities — could expand Nepal's capacity to attract and serve the high-value tourists that generate the most economic benefit. Sports infrastructure is a newer addition to development finance mandates globally, recognizing the economic and social benefits of major sporting facilities and events.

### Urban Infrastructure

"Shahri purwadhar nirmana" — urban infrastructure construction — is a broad category covering the physical systems that make cities work: water supply, sewage and waste treatment, urban roads, public transit, parks and public spaces, and digital infrastructure.

Nepal is urbanizing rapidly. The Kathmandu Valley's population has grown from perhaps 1 million in the 1980s to over 3 million today, and several provincial capitals have also grown significantly. Urban infrastructure investment has lagged behind urbanization dramatically, contributing to congestion, pollution, inadequate water supply, and poor waste management.

### Public Digital Infrastructure

"Sarwajnik digital purwadhar" — public digital infrastructure — covers broadband networks, digital government systems, data centers, and the connectivity infrastructure that underpins the digital economy. Nepal's internet connectivity has improved significantly in recent years (mobile internet is widely available) but high-speed fixed broadband remains concentrated in urban areas.

### Cable Cars, Ropeway Systems, and Podway Systems

"Kebal kar, rajjumarg, podway" — cable cars, ropeways, and podways — are particularly relevant for Nepal given its terrain. In mountainous areas where roads are expensive to build and maintain, aerial ropeways and cable cars can provide connectivity at lower cost. They are used for both passenger transport and freight (particularly for moving agricultural products from remote farming communities to market access points). Several ropeway projects exist or are planned in Nepal.

### Other High-Return Projects

Finally, the Act includes a catch-all provision: "uchcha arthik pratifal dine bhanee samitibaat nirnaaya bhaeka anya sambhawya aayojana" — other viable projects that the Board has determined will provide high economic returns. This provision gives the Board flexibility to approve investment in projects that fall outside the specific categories listed but are nonetheless viable, high-return investments. This flexibility is important — the list of categories should not be so rigid that obviously good investments are excluded on technicalities. But the catch-all also creates risk: it gives the Board discretion that could be used to justify politically motivated investments. The quality of the Board's judgment and independence is therefore critical.

## Part 3: The Priority System — Not All Eligible Projects Are Equal

Eligibility alone is not enough. Section 4, Subsection 2 adds a priority layer: even among eligible projects, the fund will prefer to invest in:

**Projects listed in the National Project Bank:** Nepal Rastra Bank (here referring to the national infrastructure project bank, not the central bank) maintains a database of projects that have been identified, studied, and prepared as investment-ready. Inclusion in this bank signals that the project has undergone basic feasibility analysis and has been formally recognized by the government as a priority.

**Projects in the Government's Annual Programme:** Projects that are included in the government's annual development budget and programme have been approved through the planning and budgeting process.

**Projects recommended by the Investment Board of Nepal (IBN) under PPP arrangements:** The Investment Board of Nepal is an institution established to promote, facilitate, and approve large investment projects in Nepal, particularly public-private partnership projects. Projects that have gone through IBN's rigorous approval process have been subject to significant scrutiny.

**Projects that the Fund itself has studied and found worthy of investment:** The fund can conduct its own project identification and feasibility analysis, not waiting for projects to be presented from outside.

This priority system is sensible. It tries to direct capital toward projects that have already been analyzed and approved through credible processes, rather than toward new, ad-hoc proposals that might not have been properly evaluated. It also creates a pipeline discipline — the fund is not expected to create projects from scratch but to deploy capital into a stream of pre-approved projects.

## Part 4: What the Fund Cannot Invest In — The Exclusion List

Section 5 is as important as Section 4. It specifies what the fund cannot invest in, regardless of any other consideration. The exclusion list is a critical governance safeguard. Let us examine each exclusion and why it exists.

### Projects Outside the Section 4 Categories

This is the baseline exclusion: anything not in the eligible categories listed in Section 4 is automatically ineligible. This prevents scope creep — the fund cannot drift into consumer lending, agricultural loans, or any other activity outside its infrastructure development mandate.

### Projects with Estimated Cost Below Rs. 1 Billion

The fund cannot invest in projects with an estimated cost below Rs. 1 billion (approximately $7.5 million). This minimum threshold excludes very small projects from the fund's mandate.

The rationale is straightforward: the fund is a sophisticated financial institution that will incur significant costs in evaluating, structuring, and monitoring each investment. For very small projects, these transaction costs would represent an unreasonably high proportion of the total investment. Small infrastructure projects should be financed through commercial banks, microfinance institutions, local government budgets, or other mechanisms better suited to small-scale lending.

The Rs. 1 billion threshold is a reasonable starting point, though it should be revisited periodically — if Nepal's capital markets develop and transaction costs fall, the threshold might appropriately be lowered to capture more mid-sized projects.

### Projects with Low Expected Financial Returns

Projects with "nyun" (low) expected financial returns are excluded. This is a critical provision. The fund is not a charity or a grant-giving institution; it is a financial institution that must generate returns sufficient to cover its operating costs, service its debt, and maintain its capital base. Investing in projects that cannot generate adequate financial returns would deplete the fund's capital and compromise its long-term viability.

This exclusion has an important corollary: purely social infrastructure — schools, rural health posts, social housing — is generally not financeable on commercial terms and therefore falls outside the fund's mandate. These investments are important for development, but they must be financed through grants and the government budget, not through a development finance institution that must maintain financial sustainability.

### Projects Unable to Provide Adequate Collateral

Loans from the fund require collateral — assets pledged as security for the loan. If a borrower defaults, the collateral can be seized and sold to recover the debt. The Act excludes projects that cannot provide adequate collateral for the loans or guarantees they seek.

This is a standard lending discipline. But it creates a tension in the Nepali context: many of the most impactful infrastructure projects — particularly in remote areas — involve assets (a remote road, a community water system) that have limited market value as collateral and would therefore be excluded under this rule. The question of how to finance high-social-return but low-collateral infrastructure remains an important policy gap that the Act does not fully resolve.

### Projects That Want Bonds Backed by the Fund's Own Bonds as Collateral

This is an unusual but important exclusion. The Act says the fund will not invest in projects that want to use the fund's own bonds or debentures as collateral for loans they are seeking. This prevents a circular arrangement where the fund's own instruments are used as collateral for the same fund's loans — a structure that would be opaque, confusing, and potentially fraudulent.

### Projects Implemented by Natural Persons (Individuals)

The fund cannot invest in projects implemented by "prakratik byakti" — natural persons (individual human beings as opposed to legal entities like companies or institutions). Infrastructure projects of the scale the fund targets require corporate or institutional implementation structures — companies, joint ventures, special purpose vehicles — with appropriate corporate governance, legal accountability, and financial management capacity. Lending to individuals for infrastructure projects would be inappropriate given the scale, complexity, and governance requirements involved.

### Projects Involving Current Board Members or Senior Officers, or Their Connected Persons

This is the most important anti-corruption exclusion. The Act says the fund cannot invest in any project implemented by:
- A company, firm, or institution where the current chairman, directors, or CEO has held a fundamental shareholding, partnership, directorship, or membership for at least three years before their appointment
- A partnership between the fund and such a company or institution

The proviso clarifies that investments that began before the relevant person was appointed are not affected.

This exclusion directly addresses the conflict of interest problem. If a member of the fund's board owns a company that then applies for investment from the fund, the obvious potential for a corrupt transaction — the board member using their position to direct fund money to their own company — must be prevented. This exclusion creates a "cooling period" (three years) that prevents people from recently divesting their interests in companies and then immediately joining the board to steer investments toward their former companies.

The three-year cooling period is a policy choice that can be debated. Some jurisdictions use longer periods (five years is common for senior positions in financial regulatory bodies). The key question is whether three years is long enough to prevent gaming — someone selling a stake in a company three years before seeking a board appointment specifically to become eligible, with the intent of later steering investments toward that company through informal channels even if no longer an owner.

## Part 5: Critical Analysis of the Investment Rules

Having described the investment rules, let us assess their design critically.

### Strengths

The sectoral focus is appropriate. The eligible sectors — energy, roads, airports, railways, urban infrastructure — are exactly the areas of highest infrastructure deficit in Nepal and highest potential development impact. The fund is not being asked to do everything; it has a clear lane.

The exclusion of projects below Rs. 1 billion is sensible fiscal management — it ensures the fund's capacity is concentrated where it has the most impact.

The conflict-of-interest exclusions are thoughtful. The three-year rule for board members and executives is a meaningful safeguard.

The priority system (project bank, annual programme, IBN recommendations) creates a discipline that should — in principle — steer capital toward pre-analyzed, government-priority projects rather than ad-hoc political favors.

### Weaknesses and Risks

The catch-all provision ("other viable projects determined by the Board to provide high economic return") creates significant discretionary authority for the Board. Without robust criteria and processes for exercising this discretion, it could become a vector for politically motivated investments.

The "low financial return" exclusion creates a gap for high-social-return, low-financial-return infrastructure. Nepal has enormous need for rural connectivity projects that may never generate adequate financial returns to be commercially viable. These will not be financed by the fund — they need separate mechanisms, which the Act does not create.

The conflict-of-interest rules focus on the Board and CEO but say less about other senior staff, advisors, and external parties with influence over investment decisions. A comprehensive conflict-of-interest regime should cover everyone involved in investment decisions.

The exclusion list does not address a subtler problem: politically motivated investments that technically meet all the criteria. A politically connected company can structure a compliant project proposal, tick all the boxes on the eligibility criteria, and still receive investment primarily because of political connections. The investment rules are necessary but not sufficient guardrails against capture. The institutional culture, independence of management, and quality of oversight are equally important.
