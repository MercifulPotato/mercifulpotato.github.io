A budget does not write itself. It is written by people — particular people, with particular beliefs, particular debts to particular supporters, and particular fears about the next election. To understand why this budget promises what it promises, you have to understand who held the pen. And the people who held the pen for Nepal's 2083/84 budget are, by any measure, the most surprising group to hold it in the country's modern history.

This is the post where we meet them. But because this series assumes you know nothing, we cannot simply name names and move on. First we have to understand the machinery these people occupy: what a parliament is, how a government forms inside it, what a Finance Minister actually does, and who has to agree before a budget can become real. Then, with that machinery in mind, we will tell the extraordinary story of how a brand-new political party, led by a former mayor who first became famous as a musician, came to write the national plan — and we will do it with the same skepticism we apply to everyone, because a dramatic origin story is not the same as competent government, and we owe you honesty rather than excitement.

## What is a parliament?

Strip the word down. A parliament is an assembly of people, chosen by the citizens, whose job is to make the laws and to hold the government to account on the people's behalf. That is the core idea behind every legislature in every democracy, however they differ in detail. The citizens cannot all gather in one place to argue out every law and scrutinise every rupee, so they elect representatives to do it for them. Those representatives, gathered together, are the parliament.

Why does a parliament matter for a budget specifically? Because of a principle so old and so important that whole revolutions have been fought over it: *the executive may not tax or spend without the consent of the people's representatives.* The government of the day — the ministers who actually run things — cannot simply reach into the public's pockets or open the public treasury on its own authority. It must ask the elected assembly for permission, every year, in the form of the budget laws. The parliament's power over money is the deepest check the people have on those who govern them. A government that could fund itself without ever facing the legislature would be a government with no master but itself. So when we say the budget is "presented to Parliament," we are describing the moment a government goes, hat in hand, to ask the people's representatives for the legal authority to carry out its plan. Everything else in this post is about who, exactly, is being asked, and who is doing the asking.

## How Nepal is governed: the shape of the state

Nepal is a *federal democratic republic*. Each of those three words is doing real work, and we should unpack them, because the budget is shaped by all three.

*Republic* means Nepal has no king. This is recent and hard-won. For most of its history Nepal was a monarchy; the institution of the king was abolished only in 2008, after a long and painful political struggle, and the country became a republic with an elected, largely ceremonial head of state in place of a hereditary one. *Democratic* means the people choose their representatives through elections, and those representatives, not a monarch or a general, hold legitimate authority. *Federal* means power is divided among more than one level of government — and this is so important to how the budget works that we will give it an entire post of its own later in the series. For now, hold only the headline: since the 2015 constitution, Nepal is no longer governed from Kathmandu alone. There are three tiers of government — one federal government for the whole country, seven provincial governments, and seven hundred and fifty-three local governments — and the budget we are studying is the *federal* budget, the plan for the topmost tier, which among other things sends large sums of money down to the other two. We flag that now and set it aside.

At the federal level, the system separates, at least on paper, into the familiar branches: a *legislature* that makes laws (the Parliament), an *executive* that carries them out (the government — the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers), and a *judiciary* that interprets them (the courts). The budget is born in the executive, must be approved by the legislature, and can be challenged in the judiciary. Our concern in this post is the first two: who sits in the legislature, and who forms the executive.

## Nepal's Parliament: two houses

Nepal's federal Parliament has two chambers, which is what "bicameral" means — *bi* for two, *cameral* from the Latin for chamber. Many countries use a two-house design, and the reasoning is usually some mix of providing a check on hasty lawmaking and giving different kinds of representation a voice. Nepal's two houses are the House of Representatives and the National Assembly.

The **House of Representatives** is the lower house and the more powerful of the two, especially where money is concerned. It has **275 members**, and the way those members are chosen is itself worth understanding, because it shapes how power is distributed. Of the 275, **165 are elected directly** from individual geographic constituencies — one member per constituency, the candidate with the most votes winning the seat, a system often called "first-past-the-post." The remaining **110 are elected by proportional representation**, a system in which citizens vote for a *party* rather than a local candidate, and each party is awarded a share of these 110 seats roughly in proportion to its share of the national vote. This mixed design is deliberate: the directly elected seats tie members to particular places and communities, while the proportional seats ensure that a party's overall national support, and the representation of groups that might otherwise be shut out, is reflected in the chamber. When we look at the election results in a moment, you will see both numbers — a party's total seats and its share of the proportional vote — because each tells a different part of the story.

The **National Assembly** is the upper house. It has **59 members**, chosen not by direct national election but through a more indirect process involving provincial-level electors and a small number of presidential appointments, and its members serve staggered terms so that the whole house is never replaced at once. Its role is to deliberate, to revise, and to provide a more reflective second look at legislation. But on the specific matter of *money* — the budget laws that authorise taxing and spending — the House of Representatives has the decisive say. The budget's core financial legislation is treated as a "money bill," and money bills are the province of the lower house; the National Assembly may discuss and suggest, but it cannot block the people's directly elected chamber on the public finances. This matters because it means the question of who controls the budget reduces, in the end, to the question of who controls the House of Representatives. Hold that thought, because the election we are about to describe answered that question with a force Nepal had not seen in a generation.

## How a government forms

Parliament makes the laws, but Parliament does not, day to day, *run the country*. That is the job of the government — the executive — and in Nepal's system the government is drawn from the Parliament itself rather than elected separately. This is the essence of a *parliamentary* system, as opposed to a *presidential* one like that of the United States, where the head of the executive is elected directly and independently.

Here is how it works. After an election to the House of Representatives, the person who can command the support of a majority of its members — typically the leader of the largest party, if that party has enough seats, or the leader of a coalition of parties that together add up to a majority — becomes the **Prime Minister**. The Prime Minister is the head of government, the person who actually leads the country's administration. The Prime Minister then appoints a team of senior colleagues to run the various departments of state — finance, home affairs, health, education, foreign affairs, and the rest. Each of these is a **Minister**, each heads a **Ministry**, and together with the Prime Minister they form the **Council of Ministers**, sometimes called the Cabinet. This Council is the engine room of government: it is where the major decisions are taken, where the budget is shaped before it ever reaches Parliament, and where the real power of the executive lives.

Above this elected machinery sits the **President**, the ceremonial head of state and the symbol of the nation. In Nepal's republican system the President is not the head of government and does not run the country day to day; the President's role is largely formal and constitutional — performing official functions, and, crucially for our purposes, formally *authenticating* the laws that Parliament passes, including the budget legislation, before they take full effect. We will return to that authenticating role in the next post, because it is the final step that turns a passed bill into a binding law. For now, simply keep the President distinct in your mind from the Prime Minister: the President is the dignified, mostly symbolic head of state; the Prime Minister is the powerful, elected head of government who actually governs.

## What the Finance Minister and the Ministry of Finance do

Of all the ministers, one matters most for our subject: the **Finance Minister**, who heads the **Ministry of Finance**. This is the minister who stands up in Parliament on Jestha 15 and reads the budget aloud, and the ministry that, more than any other, actually builds it.

The Ministry of Finance is the financial brain and nervous system of the entire government. It is responsible for forecasting how much revenue the state will collect, for negotiating and managing the loans and grants that fill the gap, for setting tax policy, for allocating money among all the other ministries, and for keeping the books. When the various ministries submit their wish lists — and every ministry always wants more money than there is — it is the Ministry of Finance that weighs them against the available resources and against the government's priorities, and decides who gets what. The budget is, in a sense, the Ministry of Finance's annual verdict on those competing demands, ratified by the Council of Ministers and then sent to Parliament for approval. The Finance Minister is the public face of that verdict and the person who must defend it. So when we ask "who wrote this budget," the honest answer is: a whole apparatus of officials in the Ministry of Finance, working under the direction of the Finance Minister, within the priorities set by the Prime Minister and the Cabinet. The names at the top matter enormously, because they set the direction. But behind them stands a permanent machinery of civil servants whose forecasts and habits shape what is possible.

That is the machinery. Now, at last, the people.

## The people in the room: an extraordinary arrival

To understand who wrote this budget, you have to understand what happened to Nepal in the year before it was written, because this is not a budget produced by the familiar faces of Nepali politics. It is the first budget of a government that did not exist, in its current form, eighteen months earlier.

For most of the past several decades, Nepali politics was dominated by a small set of long-established parties and a recurring cast of veteran leaders who cycled in and out of the Prime Minister's office in shifting coalitions. The two largest forces were the Nepali Congress, the country's grand old centrist party, and the Communist Party of Nepal (Unified Marxist–Leninist), usually called the UML, led for years by the veteran politician K. P. Sharma Oli; a third major force grew from the Maoist movement that had fought a long insurgency before entering mainstream politics. Governments were typically coalitions stitched together among these parties, and they had a reputation — fair or not, and the citizens increasingly judged it fair — for instability, for prioritising the sharing of office over the delivery of results, and for a persistent stain of corruption. K. P. Sharma Oli was the Prime Minister heading the government as this story opens.

Then, in **September 2025**, that order broke. A wave of protests — driven heavily by young people, and remembered as the "Gen Z" protests — erupted out of accumulated frustration: anger at corruption, at a lack of jobs and opportunity that had pushed so many of the young abroad, and at a political class widely seen as serving itself. The pressure was sufficient that **Prime Minister K. P. Sharma Oli resigned**, and the country was set on the path to an early general election. We should be careful and fair here, in keeping with this series' refusal to flatter anyone: street movements, however genuine their grievances, are not automatically wise, and the collapse of an old order does not guarantee that what replaces it will be better. A protest expresses what people are against far more clearly than what they are for. What the protests did establish beyond doubt was a hunger for something different. What they could not establish was whether the something different would govern well.

The early general election was held on **5 March 2026**, to fill all 275 seats of the House of Representatives. The result was a political earthquake. The **Rastriya Swatantra Party** — a young, self-consciously anti-establishment party, only founded in 2022, whose name translates roughly to "National Independent Party," led by **Rabi Lamichhane** — won an outright landslide. It took **182 of the 275 seats**, and won the highest share of the proportional vote, around **47.8 percent**, of any party since the current electoral system began. This was something Nepal had not seen in the modern federal era: a *single party with a clear majority of its own*, rather than yet another fragile coalition. The contrast with the established parties was brutal. The Nepali Congress suffered its worst-ever result, reduced to around **38 seats**. Oli's UML fell to roughly **25 seats**, and Oli himself lost his own constituency. The Maoist-rooted force won around **17 seats**. The old guard was not merely defeated; it was, for the moment, swept aside.

The figure who became the public face of the new government was **Balen Shah** — formally Balendra Shah — who became **Prime Minister**. His path to that office is itself part of why this budget feels different. Balen Shah first became a public figure not as a politician but as a rapper and a structural engineer, and he rose to political prominence by winning the mayoralty of Kathmandu, the capital, as an independent, building a reputation as an energetic outsider impatient with the old ways. He joined the Rastriya Swatantra Party as a senior leader in late December 2025, became the party's candidate to lead the country, and in the election defeated none other than K. P. Sharma Oli in their shared constituency. To hold the public purse, the new government appointed as **Finance Minister** the economist **Dr. Swarnim Wagle** — a figure with serious technical credentials in economics and development policy, whose presence in the role signalled, at least, an intention to bring professional economic thinking to the writing of the budget.

So this is the room. A brand-new party with a commanding majority of its own. A charismatic outsider Prime Minister who came to politics through music and municipal government. A credentialed economist holding the finance portfolio. And, across the aisle, a battered and shrunken opposition. This is who wrote Nepal's 2083/84 budget, and it is unlike any group that had written one before.

## Reading the room with skepticism: what this configuration means for the budget

It would be easy, and lazy, to tell this story as a fairy tale: the corrupt old order swept away, the fresh and honest newcomers given a mandate to fix everything, a technocrat economist in charge of the money. This series does not deal in fairy tales, so let us think about what this configuration actually means for the budget, including the parts that should worry a careful citizen.

Consider first the *mandate and the expectations*. A party that wins a landslide on a wave of anti-establishment hope arrives in office carrying enormous expectations and very little patience — its own supporters', and its own. The temptation for any such government is to *over-promise*: to fill its first budget with bold, eye-catching commitments that match the scale of the hope that elected it, whether or not the money and the machinery exist to deliver them. As we move through this series and examine the budget's many ambitious promises — sweeping health coverage, a sovereign artificial-intelligence facility, dramatic tax cuts, large social allowances — you should keep this incentive firmly in mind. A first budget written under the pressure of a landslide mandate is exactly the kind of document in which announcements can outrun capacity. The fresh political will is real and valuable. The risk that the promises exceed what can be done is equally real.

Consider next the *lack of a track record*, which cuts in two directions and we should be honest about both. On one hand, a new party unburdened by the failures and compromises of the past has a genuine chance to do things differently, and the involvement of a serious economist in the finance role is a real reason for cautious optimism about the technical quality of the budget. On the other hand, governing a country is not the same as running a campaign or even a city, and this government has never done it. The established parties, for all their faults, knew how the machinery worked; a new government must learn on the job, and the gap between intending to reform and actually executing reform through a slow, entrenched bureaucracy is precisely where so many good intentions die. A clean slate is an opportunity and a risk at once.

Consider, finally, the *weakness of the opposition*, which is a subtler danger than it first appears. We are taught to cheer when a reforming government wins a strong majority, and there is something to that: a government with a secure majority can actually pass its programme rather than being held hostage by coalition partners. But a budget, remember, is the people's chief instrument for holding the government to account on money, and that accountability works best when there is a strong, capable opposition in Parliament to scrutinise every figure, expose every evasion, and force the government to justify itself. When the opposition has been reduced to a fraction of the chamber, that scrutiny weakens. A government facing only a small and demoralised opposition can pass a budget with little real challenge to its assumptions and its omissions. So the very landslide that gives this government the power to act also removes much of the parliamentary check on how it acts. That is not a reason to wish the election had gone differently; it is a reason for citizens, journalists, and analysts outside Parliament — including readers of a series like this one — to supply the scrutiny that a thinned-out opposition cannot. In a sense, when the opposition is weak, the duty of holding the budget to account falls more heavily on you.

## A worked example: two governments, two sets of incentives

To make the role of incentives concrete, imagine the same identical economic situation handled by two different governments, and watch how the politics, not the economics, shapes the budget.

Picture first a tired, late-term coalition government, several years into power, its popularity worn down, distracted by the constant work of holding its quarrelling partners together, and aware that an election is looming. Such a government, writing a budget, faces a powerful temptation toward caution and crowd-pleasing in equal measure: it may avoid hard but necessary choices that would anger any of its partners or any voting bloc, it may pad the budget with small giveaways aimed at the next election, and it may set comfortable, unambitious targets it is confident of meeting so as to avoid the embarrassment of a public miss. Its budget will tend to be a document of survival.

Now picture a brand-new government, fresh from a landslide, in the first flush of a powerful mandate, its supporters expecting transformation, with the next election years away. Such a government faces the opposite temptation: toward boldness, scale, and statement-making. It wants its first budget to *announce a new era*. It may set dramatic targets — a growth rate far above recent experience, sweeping new programmes, headline reforms — partly because it genuinely intends them and partly because anything modest would disappoint the hope that elected it. Its budget will tend to be a document of ambition, and ambition, untested by the experience of actually running the machinery, can shade into the unrealistic.

Neither of these caricatures is the whole truth of any real government, and the actual budget will contain elements of both caution and ambition. But the exercise reveals the essential point: the *same economy* will produce a *different budget* depending on who is writing it and what they fear and hope. When you read this budget's confident growth target of 7 percent against an economy that just grew 3.85 percent, or its long list of bold new commitments, you are not only reading economic forecasts. You are reading the incentives of a particular kind of government at a particular moment — a new government, with a huge mandate, a thin opposition, and everything to prove. Knowing that does not tell you the budget is wrong. It tells you exactly where to point your skepticism.

## How budgets deceive: the mandate halo

The technique of deception for this post is political rather than arithmetic, and it is especially potent for a government like this one. Call it **the mandate halo**.

Here is the move. A government that has won a large and genuine electoral mandate enjoys a kind of glow of legitimacy, and that glow tends to spread, in the public mind, from the things the election actually settled to things it did not settle at all. The election settled that this party should govern; the people chose it, decisively, and that choice deserves respect. But the election did *not* settle whether any particular budget number is realistic, whether the growth target is achievable, whether the revenue forecast is sound, or whether a promised programme can actually be delivered. Those are technical and empirical questions, and a landslide at the ballot box answers none of them. The mandate halo is the tendency to treat the political legitimacy of *who* is governing as if it were proof of the soundness of *what* they are proposing — to assume that because the people chose this government, this government's plan must therefore add up. Defenders of a popular government deploy this constantly, often without even meaning to: criticism of a budget figure is met not with a defence of the figure but with a reminder of the election result, as though winning power were the same as being right.

The defence is to hold the two questions firmly apart. The legitimacy of a government's *authority* and the soundness of its *arithmetic* are completely different matters, established by completely different means — one by an election, the other by evidence and analysis. You can fully accept that a government won fairly and deserves to govern, and still insist that its growth target is implausible, its revenue forecast optimistic, or its flagship promise undeliverable, because those are not insults to the voters' choice; they are questions the voters' choice did not address. When you find an argument that defends a budget number by pointing to the size of the election win, you have found the mandate halo, and you should set the election result gently aside and ask, again, the only questions that actually test a figure: measured how, compared to what, and on the basis of what evidence? A mandate is permission to govern. It is not a forecast, a guarantee, or a proof. Keep it in its place, and you will be one of the few people scrutinising this budget on its merits rather than its glamour.

## Where we go from here

We have met the people in the room and the machinery they occupy. You now understand what a parliament is and why its control of money is the people's deepest check on government; how Nepal's bicameral Parliament is built, with a 275-member House of Representatives that holds the decisive power over the budget and a 59-member National Assembly that does not; how a government forms from the largest force in that House, with a Prime Minister leading a Council of Ministers and a largely ceremonial President above; and what the Finance Minister and the Ministry of Finance actually do. And you now know the remarkable story behind this particular budget — the Gen Z protests of 2025, the fall of K. P. Sharma Oli, the March 2026 landslide that handed the Rastriya Swatantra Party 182 seats and made Balen Shah Prime Minister with the economist Dr. Swarnim Wagle as Finance Minister — together with the reasons a careful citizen should watch a government like this with hope and with caution in equal measure. You have met the mandate halo, and the discipline of separating the right to govern from the soundness of the plan.

In Part 4 we follow the budget out of the room and into the law. Presenting a budget is not the same as enacting it, and enacting it is not the same as carrying it out. We will trace the journey from the Finance Minister's speech through the formal legislation — the Appropriation Bill and the Finance Bill that becomes the Finance Act — through the parliamentary stages of debate, amendment, and authentication, and into the circulars and machinery that turn words into action or let them quietly lapse. By the end of Part 4 you will understand exactly why "announced" is a world away from "law," and even further from "done" — the single most important distinction in this entire series.

## A Note on Numbers and Assumptions

This post is mostly about people and institutions rather than money, but the same standards apply. Where monetary figures recur — the total budget of NPR 2,124.34 billion (about USD 14.16 billion), the 7 percent growth target against 3.85 percent actual growth — the US dollar equivalents use our single assumed rate of **1 US dollar = 150 Nepalese rupees**, an approximation close to the Nepal Rastra Bank reference rate in mid-2026; the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee at 1.60 and drifts daily, so treat dollar figures as intuition, not accounting.

The political figures — 182 seats of 275 for the Rastriya Swatantra Party, around 47.8 percent of the proportional vote, roughly 38, 25, and 17 seats for the principal opposition parties — are the reported results of the 5 March 2026 election and should be confirmed against the final record of the Election Commission of Nepal; secondary sources occasionally differ on exact tallies and on the precise allocation of smaller parties' seats. The sequence of events around the September 2025 protests and the change of government is reconstructed from contemporary reporting.

And the assumption beneath the whole series, which this post should make vivid: a budget is written by particular people with particular incentives, and the fact that those people won an election — however decisively and however legitimately — does not make their numbers true. Authority and accuracy are different things, established in different ways. We will keep testing the accuracy, election or no election.
