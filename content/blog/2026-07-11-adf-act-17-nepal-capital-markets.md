On most trading days, the Nepal Stock Exchange — called NEPSE, pronounced in Nepali financial circles more or less as it is spelled — transacts a volume of business that would be unremarkable even for a small regional exchange in a country of Nepal's size. On the busiest days, trading volume has occasionally touched tens of billions of rupees. On ordinary days, it is far less. Compared to a major market — the Bombay Stock Exchange, for instance, where daily turnover routinely runs into hundreds of billions of rupees — NEPSE is a small institution. But it is not irrelevant. For the Alternative Development Finance Fund to succeed in its ambitions, NEPSE and the broader Nepali capital market ecosystem need to develop in particular ways, and understanding what currently exists and what is missing is essential for anyone who wants to think clearly about the Fund's realistic prospects.

**What Capital Markets Are For**

The phrase "capital markets" refers, broadly, to the collection of markets and institutions through which savers make their savings available to borrowers for purposes of investment. The category includes stock markets, where companies sell ownership stakes to investors; bond markets, where governments and companies borrow money from investors against a promise to pay interest and repay principal; and a variety of derivative and structured instruments built on top of these basic categories. Capital markets are distinguished from bank lending by their structure: in bank lending, a single institution (the bank) takes deposits from many savers and makes a decision to lend to a borrower, concentrating the credit analysis and risk-taking in one place. In capital markets, the loan or equity investment is divided into many small units — bonds or shares — that are sold to many different investors, distributing the risk broadly and allowing the instrument to be traded in a secondary market.

For infrastructure financing, the bond market is more important than the stock market. Infrastructure projects are capital-intensive and generate long-term cash flows — a hydropower plant, once built, generates revenue for fifty years or more. The appropriate financing for such a project is long-term debt — bonds or bank loans — whose maturity is matched to the project's cash flow profile. An infrastructure bond might have a twenty- or thirty-year maturity, paying semi-annual coupon payments from the project's operating revenues and repaying principal at the end of the period. This structure works well for investors with long-term liabilities — pension funds and insurance companies that need to match long-duration assets to long-duration obligations — and it works well for infrastructure developers who need patient capital that will not need to be refinanced at an uncertain future cost.

The equity market is also important, but in a different way. For an infrastructure project, equity typically represents 20 to 30 percent of the total capital needed. Infrastructure equity investment requires tolerance for long construction periods during which no revenue is generated, and confidence in the regulatory and contractual environment that will govern the project's revenues over its operational life. Stock market listing of infrastructure assets — through initial public offerings or secondary listings of project companies — can be a mechanism for mobilizing domestic equity capital and for eventually allowing the development finance fund to exit investments by selling shares to the market, recovering capital for redeployment.

**NEPSE: History and Current State**

Nepal established its securities market in 1976 with the formation of the Securities Exchange Centre, which became the Nepal Stock Exchange Limited in 1993 when the government converted it to a company form. NEPSE is the sole stock exchange in Nepal, operating as a regulated monopoly — unlike larger markets that have multiple competing exchanges, Nepal has a single market for listed securities.

The exchange listed predominantly financial sector companies in its early decades — commercial banks, insurance companies, development banks, and finance companies. This reflects the trajectory of Nepal's private sector more broadly: financial services were among the first sectors to attract private investment and to benefit from liberalization, and the most straightforward companies to list because they have standardized financial statements and regulated capital structures. As of the mid-2020s, the financial sector still dominates NEPSE's listed companies and its market capitalization, though hydropower companies have become increasingly prominent as private-sector power development accelerated.

The NEPSE All Shares Price Index — NEPSE's benchmark index — has been one of the more volatile market indices in South Asia over the past two decades. It experienced a dramatic rally in 2020 and 2021, when the combination of pandemic-related liquidity, low interest rates, and the closure of alternative investment opportunities drove retail investors into the market in large numbers and pushed the index to historical highs. It subsequently corrected sharply as interest rates rose and the macroeconomic environment tightened. This boom-bust pattern is characteristic of retail-dominated markets with limited institutional investor participation, and NEPSE is emphatically a retail-dominated market. Surveys of NEPSE trading consistently find that individual retail investors account for the large majority of trading volume, with institutional investors — mutual funds, insurance companies, employee provident funds — playing a much smaller role than they do in comparable markets at similar development stages.

The dominance of retail investors creates several characteristics in the NEPSE market that are significant for infrastructure financing. Retail investors tend to have short time horizons — they are trading for capital gains rather than holding for long-term income. They tend to be sensitive to sentiment and narrative rather than fundamentals, creating momentum-driven price movements. They tend to lack the analytical resources to conduct sophisticated company analysis. And they tend to have limited capital individually, meaning that very large share issuances — like those that infrastructure companies require — can be difficult to absorb without the participation of institutional investors.

**The Bond Market: Almost Nonexistent**

If Nepal's equity market is undersized and retail-dominated, Nepal's domestic bond market is, at least through the first half of the 2020s, essentially nascent. The government issues Treasury bills and some longer-term securities for purposes of domestic borrowing. Nepal Rastra Bank, the central bank, places these instruments primarily with commercial banks, which hold them as liquid assets on their balance sheets. A secondary market for government securities exists but is thin — trading is sporadic and prices are not as informationally efficient as in more developed markets. The market for corporate bonds — debt instruments issued by companies and infrastructure developers rather than the government — has been developing but remains extremely limited.

The absence of a functioning corporate bond market is one of the most significant structural constraints on infrastructure financing in Nepal. The fundamental problem is a set of interconnected gaps. There is limited supply of corporate bonds because companies and infrastructure developers have been able to access bank loans and do not yet have the experience, legal framework, or investor relationships needed to issue publicly traded debt. There is limited demand for corporate bonds because the institutional investors who would normally be the primary buyers — pension funds, insurance companies, mutual funds — are themselves limited in scale and, in some cases, in regulatory authority to invest in fixed-income instruments. There is limited market infrastructure — credit rating agencies capable of rating corporate issuers with credibility, bond trustees with the expertise to protect investors' interests, a Securities Board of Nepal regulatory framework fully adapted to corporate bond issuance — for bond markets to function efficiently.

The Citizens Investment Trust and the Employees' Provident Fund are the two largest institutional investors in Nepal. The Citizens Investment Trust manages retirement savings products sold to working Nepalis outside the formal employment sector, while the Employees' Provident Fund manages the mandatory retirement contributions of formal sector employees. Together, these two institutions manage hundreds of billions of rupees and are the largest domestic pools of investable capital outside the commercial banking system. Their investment behavior matters enormously for the development of Nepal's capital market: if they invest primarily in government securities and bank deposits — which has historically been the pattern — the corporate bond market cannot develop because its natural anchor investors are absent. If regulatory frameworks were updated to allow and encourage these institutions to invest in infrastructure bonds issued by creditworthy entities, they would represent a large and patient source of demand for exactly the kind of long-duration instruments that infrastructure projects need to issue.

**The Chicken-and-Egg Problem**

Nepal's capital market development suffers from a classic chicken-and-egg problem. Corporate bonds need institutional investors to buy them, but institutional investors need a track record of corporate bonds in which to invest. Infrastructure companies need a liquid market to list and eventually monetize their investments, but market liquidity requires institutional participation that does not yet exist at scale. Rating agencies need companies to rate to develop their expertise and revenue, but companies need rated bonds to access the bond market.

The way out of this problem typically requires a combination of regulatory reform to enable institutional investor participation, government or development finance institution intervention to create the first transactions that establish market norms and precedents, and time as the market learns how to function and market participants develop the skills needed to operate in it.

The Alternative Development Finance Fund has a role to play in each dimension. By issuing bonds itself — the legislation explicitly authorizes the Fund to issue bonds as a mechanism for raising capital — the Fund becomes an issuer of investment-grade debt securities that can help develop market infrastructure. A Fund bond, backed by government capital and invested in infrastructure assets, would be a well-understood instrument with clear credit characteristics and would give rating agencies a prominent issuer to rate, give institutional investors a credible fixed-income option, and give the Securities Board a real-world transaction on which to develop its regulatory practice. The Fund as an active bond issuer is thus not just a financing mechanism for the Fund itself but a market-development intervention that creates the precedents other infrastructure issuers can follow.

The Fund's equity investments in hydropower and other infrastructure companies will, over time, create listed infrastructure companies of sufficient scale and quality that NEPSE can develop a meaningful infrastructure sector. Currently, while private sector hydropower companies exist and several are listed on NEPSE, the sector is fragmented — many small and medium companies rather than a few large ones with the market capitalization and investor relations capacity to attract institutional interest. As the Fund co-invests in larger projects and those projects eventually list on NEPSE, the market gains the large, liquid infrastructure stocks that can attract institutional investors and help transition NEPSE from a retail-dominated market toward a more balanced one.

**What Specifically the Fund Needs**

For the Alternative Development Finance Fund to operate at the scale envisioned in the legislation, it needs several things from Nepal's capital market ecosystem that do not yet fully exist.

It needs a functioning secondary market for long-term bonds, so that when it issues bonds to raise capital, those bonds can be traded by investors who want to adjust their holdings. An investor who buys a twenty-year bond from the Fund is willing to do so only if they believe they can sell it before maturity if their circumstances change. Without secondary market liquidity, the Fund will have to offer higher yields to compensate investors for the illiquidity, increasing the Fund's cost of capital.

It needs institutional investors — particularly the Citizens Investment Trust and the Employees' Provident Fund — to be willing and able to invest in the Fund's bonds and in the infrastructure companies in which the Fund is a co-investor. This requires regulatory changes at both the investment mandate level for these institutions and potentially at the Securities Board level.

It needs credit rating infrastructure — a credible domestic credit rating agency, or the acceptance of ratings from international agencies — so that the Fund's bonds can be rated and investors can make informed assessments of their credit quality. Nepal Rastra Bank and the Securities Board have been developing this area, but it remains incomplete.

It needs legal infrastructure for bond issuance and enforcement — bond trustee regulations, standardized trust deed documentation, clear insolvency rules that protect bondholders' claims — to give investors confidence that their rights under a bond instrument will be respected in the event of problems. Investors will not commit long-term capital to a market where the legal enforcement of their rights is uncertain.

None of these things will be in place fully and reliably on the day the Fund begins operations. The Fund's early years will therefore require a combination of pragmatism — working with what exists, accepting higher costs and lower scale than the theory suggests — and active advocacy for the market development reforms that will make its long-term model work. The Fund's management will need to engage with Nepal Rastra Bank on monetary policy and financial stability issues, with the Securities Board on capital market regulatory reform, with the Citizens Investment Trust and the Employees' Provident Fund on investment mandate questions, and with NEPSE on listing and trading infrastructure. This broader capital market development role is not written into the Fund's legislation — it is focused on investment — but it will in practice be inseparable from the Fund's ability to operate at scale.
