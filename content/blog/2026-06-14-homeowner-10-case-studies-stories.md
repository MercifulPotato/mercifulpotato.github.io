## Part 1: Why Stories Matter

Nine posts into this series, you have covered a lot of ground. You know how your HVAC system works. You know what the building envelope is and why water is its enemy. You know how to evaluate a contractor, what a mechanic's lien is, and how to calculate whether your mortgage paydown rate makes sense. You have a maintenance calendar and a list of tools.

What you cannot get from explanations alone is the felt experience of actually being a homeowner — the 11 PM text from a neighbor saying your basement is flooding, the quiet discovery of a perfectly normal-looking wall that turns out to be entirely rotted behind the drywall, the satisfaction of fixing the running toilet yourself in fifteen minutes with a $7 part from the hardware store. The fear when the inspector says the words "active termite activity." The relief when the roof contractor says "this is repairable, not a full replacement."

This final post is different from the others. It is ten case studies — stories drawn from the kinds of experiences first-time homeowners actually have in the first five years. They are composite and representative, not specific individuals, but every situation described is grounded in real patterns. Read them as preparation, as anticipation management, and as reassurance that whatever goes wrong in your home, it has gone wrong for someone else first, and they got through it.

## Case Study 1: The Deferred Furnace

**The situation:** Marcus and Elena bought a 1987 ranch-style home in suburban Ohio for $285,000 in October. They stretched their budget to get into the neighborhood they wanted. The home inspection noted "HVAC system is 17 years old; recommend budgeting for replacement within 2–3 years." They read it, noted it, and thought "2 to 3 years is 2 to 3 years." They did not budget specifically for HVAC replacement.

The following January — three months after they moved in — temperatures dropped to 5°F for a week. On the third night of the cold snap, the furnace stopped working. The house temperature dropped to 48°F by morning.

Marcus called an HVAC company. The service call fee during an emergency was $175. The technician arrived, diagnosed a failed inducer motor, and quoted $650 for the part and labor. Good news, right? Except the technician also looked at the heat exchanger and found a crack — a CO safety hazard that rendered the furnace condemned regardless of whether the inducer motor was fixed.

The furnace needed to be replaced. During the cold snap, every HVAC company in the region was slammed. The only equipment immediately available was a basic efficiency model. The installed cost was $5,400. They put it on a credit card at 22% APR because their emergency fund — also depleted by the down payment and moving expenses — did not fully cover it. It took them 14 months to pay off the card, during which time they paid approximately $760 in interest.

**The lessons:**
- The home inspector told them. The inspection report is a maintenance and budget planning document, not just a negotiation tool. When an inspector says "budget for replacement," believe them.
- A home emergency fund — separate from the general emergency fund — should have been their first financial priority after closing. $5,000 to $7,000 set aside in the first 6 months would have covered this without interest charges.
- HVAC replacement in a non-emergency situation, planned in spring or fall, is both cheaper (competitive bids, not emergency rates) and gives time to evaluate options (including heat pumps, as we covered in Post 3).
- Emergency service markups are real. After-hours HVAC in January costs more than the same work in May because it must.

**What they did next:** They built a $6,000 home emergency fund over the next year before spending money on anything cosmetic. They also signed up for an HVAC maintenance contract that year.

## Case Study 2: The Gutter That Forgot to Drain

**The situation:** Sarah bought a cape cod in suburban Massachusetts. The previous owner had professionally landscaped the property and the curb appeal was part of what sold her. She loved the mature plantings, the window boxes, the carefully trimmed foundation shrubs.

She moved in in August. In November, she noticed that the basement smelled slightly musty. In December, she noticed a small water stain on the lower portion of the interior of the front basement wall. In January, after a prolonged warm spell that melted a substantial snowpack, she found three inches of water on the basement floor.

The diagnosis: the gutters on the front of the house were completely clogged — packed with the leaves from three nearby large maples, which had dropped after the previous owner's last cleaning. The clogged gutters had overflowed all fall, directing water against the foundation. The mature plantings immediately against the foundation had graded the soil toward the house over decades of mulch accumulation. The foundation drain was intact but overwhelmed by the volume of water.

The remediation: professional water extraction ($850), basement drying ($1,200 with industrial fans and dehumidifiers over two weeks), mold remediation of the drywall at the base of the wall ($2,400). Total: $4,450. Plus the cost of gutter cleaning twice a year and the cost of regrading the soil away from the foundation and cutting back the foundation plantings.

**The lessons:**
- Inspect the gutters before and immediately after buying a home. They may have been neglected in the transition period.
- A basement that has never flooded before will flood if the water management system fails. The system worked for years; when one component failed, everything downstream was affected.
- The signs were there: the musty smell in November, the water stain in December. Each of these was a warning that should have triggered investigation. Early investigation would have found a wet wall, not a flooded basement.
- A $0 gutter cleaning in October would have prevented a $4,450 remediation in January.

**What she did next:** Bi-annual gutter cleaning goes on the calendar the day after closing, for every home she ever owns. She also installed a water alarm ($25, battery-powered, sits on the basement floor) that would alert her by beeping and text message if water reached the sensor.

## Case Study 3: The Inspector That Missed It

**The situation:** David and Christine bought a 1995 colonial in suburban Georgia. The home inspection was thorough — 47 pages, detailed, with good photos. The inspector did note that access to the crawl space was limited due to low clearance and debris near the access hatch, and that a "full crawl space inspection was not possible."

Eight months after moving in, Christine noticed a soft spot in the kitchen floor near the dishwasher. A few weeks later, the soft spot had grown. They lifted the vinyl flooring and found the subfloor soft and discolored beneath. A contractor opened the floor from above and a plumber inspected from the crawl space.

The dishwasher supply line had a slow leak — had been leaking for what appeared to be years. The water had saturated the subfloor, traveled into the crawl space, and provided ideal conditions for termites. When the pest control company came out, they found an active termite infestation that had been feeding on the wet subfloor framing. The crawl space had not been inspected adequately because it was not accessible.

Repairs: subfloor replacement ($3,400), structural framing in the crawl space ($4,200), termite treatment ($1,800), reinstall vinyl flooring ($1,100). Total: $10,500.

**The lessons:**
- "Not accessible" in an inspection report is a flag, not a clearance. When an inspector cannot fully access a space, consider hiring a specialist for that space — a crawl space inspector, or a pest control company doing a WDO (Wood Destroying Organism) inspection (required in many Southern states for any real estate transaction).
- A WDO inspection is separate from a home inspection. In termite-active regions (the Southeast, the Gulf Coast, much of California), it should be standard practice.
- Slow leaks under appliances are invisible — until they are not. The monthly under-sink visual check described in Post 4 should be extended to the dishwasher area, where the supply line and drain are also at risk.
- The supply line to the dishwasher should be a braided stainless steel line. If it is still the original plastic line from 1995, replace it proactively.

**What they did next:** WDO inspection is now a first-week item for any home they evaluate. Quarterly crawl space inspection with a headlamp and photos. All appliance supply lines replaced with braided stainless steel.

## Case Study 4: The Contractor Who Disappeared

**The situation:** James hired a local contractor to remodel his master bathroom — new tile shower, new vanity, new toilet, new flooring. The quote was $18,500. He had found the contractor through a yard sign at a neighbor's house (who had used him for a much smaller job — replacing a toilet). He did not check the contractor's license, did not ask for a certificate of insurance, and did not get any other quotes. The price seemed fair and the guy seemed nice.

The payment schedule the contractor proposed: 50% up front ($9,250) to order materials, 40% at rough-in ($7,400), 10% at completion ($1,850). James agreed and paid the first payment.

Work began. The contractor and one helper demoed the existing bathroom — removed the old tile, toilet, and vanity, and left a gutted bathroom shell with plastic sheeting over the doorway. Then the contractor became difficult to reach. A day turned into a week. James's texts were answered vaguely ("waiting on materials"). After three weeks of the gutted bathroom, the contractor stopped responding entirely.

James had paid $9,250 and had a destroyed bathroom and no contractor. The contractor was not licensed (James never verified). He had no workers' compensation or general liability insurance. He had vanished.

Resolution: James hired a new contractor to complete the bathroom, starting essentially from scratch. Total cost for the completed bathroom: $11,800 for the completion (materials had not been ordered by the first contractor despite the "materials deposit"). Total out-of-pocket: $21,050, compared to the original quote of $18,500 and the actual work value of approximately $18,000 to $20,000. Loss attributable to the contractor fraud: approximately $9,250.

He filed a police report (contractor fraud can be a criminal matter). He filed a complaint with the state Attorney General's consumer protection office. He recovered $0. The contractor was found to have done the same thing to at least four other homeowners in the region.

**The lessons:**
- Verify licensing before paying a dollar. A 2-minute search on the state contractor board website costs nothing and would have revealed this contractor was unlicensed.
- Never pay 50% upfront for a large project. 10% to 20% maximum.
- A referral for a small job is not a referral for a large job. The neighbor's experience replacing a toilet tells you nothing about how this contractor handles a $18,500 renovation.
- Get three quotes. A second and third quote would have provided comparative context and additional contractors to check against.

**What he did next:** Three quotes, verified license and insurance, 10% deposit, and references actually called for every contractor he has hired since.

## Case Study 5: The Toilet That Was Trying to Tell Them Something

**The situation:** Priya and Anil moved into a 1978 split-level. Within the first month, they noticed the toilet in the main bathroom occasionally ran for a few seconds after flushing, then stopped. They called it "the toilet doing its thing" and ignored it.

Over two years, the toilet's behavior gradually changed — running for longer periods, running more frequently, eventually running almost constantly. They had adapted to the sound over time and truly no longer noticed it.

At a dinner party, a guest who happened to be a plumber asked what was wrong with the toilet. That conversation prompted them to look at their water bill. It had increased by $45 to $60 per month over the previous 18 months compared to the months after they moved in. A running toilet wastes approximately 200 gallons per day in the worst cases.

The repair: a $7 flapper and 15 minutes. The cost of not doing the repair for two years: approximately $900 to $1,200 in excess water charges.

**The lessons:**
- Small symptoms that you adapt to are still symptoms. The toilet was communicating a problem in the most obvious way it could. They chose not to hear it.
- Comparing utility bills year over year is a useful diagnostic. A creeping increase in water, gas, or electric bills often indicates a developing problem — a running toilet, a water heater losing efficiency, an HVAC system working harder than it should.
- The repair was so trivial — $7 and 15 minutes — that the entire $900+ loss is attributable purely to not acting on a known problem.

## Case Study 6: The Unpermitted Addition

**The situation:** Rachel and Tom bought a 1965 ranch home that had been expanded. The listing said 1,850 square feet; the county tax records said 1,250. The seller explained that the previous owner had added a room addition in the back and a finished basement, "all done properly." They loved the extra space and bought the house.

Four years later, they decided to sell. The listing agent reviewed the county records and confirmed what was visible: approximately 600 square feet of the home were not in the official records. The real estate attorney they consulted confirmed that the addition and basement had no permits on record. The county had never inspected the work.

Their buyer's lender required the work to be permitted retroactively as a condition of the mortgage. The retroactive permitting process required an engineer to inspect the structural work (some of which needed remediation — the addition's roof connection to the original structure was non-compliant) and the electrical panel, which had been improperly expanded with mismatched breakers. The permit process took 11 weeks. The remediation work cost $8,400. The sale was delayed by nearly three months.

**The lessons:**
- Tax records and official square footage should be verified against what is actually present before purchase. A discrepancy is a due diligence flag.
- "Done properly" from a seller means nothing in the absence of permits and inspection records. Permits are the documentation that work was done properly.
- Unpermitted work does not disappear — it becomes a problem at the next sale, or when the home is damaged and insurance is involved, or when a municipal inspection occurs.
- A retroactive permit process is costly, slow, and disruptive. The original permit for a room addition would have cost a few hundred dollars and a few weeks.

## Case Study 7: The Tree That Didn't Look Dead

**The situation:** Kevin and Mira's 1990 colonial in suburban Maryland had a large silver maple at the back of the property. The tree was beautiful in summer — full canopy, good shade. In July of their third year, during a moderate windstorm (sustained winds of 30 MPH, gusts to 45 MPH), a major limb — approximately 15 inches in diameter and 30 feet long — dropped from 40 feet up and landed on the corner of the rear of the house. It went through the roof, through the ceiling of the second floor, and partially through a bedroom floor. Mercifully, the bedroom was unoccupied.

The insurance claim: $47,000 for the roof section, structural repairs to the second floor framing, ceiling repair, and contents replacement.

Post-event, an arborist inspected the fallen limb. It had been dead for at least two years — the wood was desiccated, the cambium was long dead, and there was internal decay at the branch union. The health of the rest of the tree was marginal — the tree was near the end of its natural lifespan and had multiple other compromised limbs.

The tree removal: $3,800 (covered by homeowner's insurance as part of the debris removal). The three other large limbs over the house that also needed to be removed: an additional $2,200 not covered because they were identified by an arborist but removed preventively rather than after a loss event.

**The lessons:**
- Silver maples are beautiful and fast-growing and notoriously prone to branch failure. Knowing your tree species and their failure characteristics matters.
- An arborist inspection every 3 to 5 years for large trees near structures is inexpensive insurance against exactly this scenario. The inspection cost is $200 to $500. The dead limb removal would have cost $800 to $1,500. The storm damage cost $47,000.
- Dead limbs in a full-canopied deciduous tree can be very difficult to identify from the ground in summer. An arborist's trained eye sees what a homeowner cannot.
- The insurance covered the damage, but no insurance covers the stress of a tree coming through the ceiling of your child's bedroom.

## Case Study 8: The Solar Calculation That Worked

**The situation:** Diane and Robert bought a 2005 brick colonial in central Virginia. They had a south-facing roof in excellent condition, modest energy consumption (1,100 kWh per month average), and an electricity rate of $0.14 per kWh. They were genuinely interested in rooftop solar but cautious — they had heard mixed things.

They followed the process described in Post 3: reviewed 12 months of utility bills, assessed their roof (south-facing, no significant shading, good condition, 18 years old — they had it independently inspected and it had at least 7 to 10 years of life remaining), and got quotes from three installers.

Quote 1: 9.6 kWp system, 26 panels, SunPower 370W panels with integrated microinverters, estimated annual production 12,800 kWh, total cost $31,200, net after 30% federal ITC: $21,840.

Quote 2: 9.4 kWp system, 28 panels, Canadian Solar panels with Enphase microinverters, estimated annual production 12,500 kWh, total cost $26,600, net after federal ITC: $18,620.

Quote 3: 8.8 kWp system, 22 panels, REC Group panels with SolarEdge optimizers, estimated annual production 11,900 kWh, total cost $29,800, net after federal ITC: $20,860.

They chose Quote 2 — mid-range cost, reputable components, the installer had 14 years of local history and dozens of verifiable local references.

Outcome: The system went live in March. By the end of the first full year, it had produced 12,340 kWh — within 1.3% of the estimate. Their annual electricity bill dropped from approximately $1,848 to approximately $186 (they still drew power at night and on overcast days, but their net metering credits covered most of it). Annual savings: approximately $1,660. At that savings rate, the payback period on their net investment of $18,620 was 11.2 years. The system has a 25-year performance warranty from the panel manufacturer.

**The lessons:**
- The process matters. Getting three quotes, evaluating on equivalent terms, checking references, and choosing a contractor with local longevity produced a good outcome.
- The federal tax credit is real and material — it reduced the cost by nearly one-third.
- The estimates from reputable installers using quality equipment tend to be accurate. The system produced within 1.3% of the estimate in year one.
- In a state with good solar resources and net metering, the economics are straightforward and the payback is genuine. Virginia's electricity rates and solar resource are not exceptional — this is not Hawaii or California. Solar worked here because the analysis was done correctly.

## Case Study 9: The Water Heater They Replaced at the Right Time

**The situation:** When Layla and Marcus bought their 2001 colonial, the home inspection noted a 19-year-old water heater in fair condition — sediment buildup audible during operation, slight corrosion at the base, T&P valve had never been tested per seller disclosure. The inspector recommended "budget for replacement soon."

Unlike Case Study 1 where the HVAC advice was ignored, Layla and Marcus took this one seriously. They budgeted $2,000 for water heater replacement in their first year.

At month eight, the water heater began producing inconsistent hot water — not quite hot enough, then scalding, then inconsistent. Marcus called a plumber who confirmed the thermostat had failed but also noted that the tank had significant corrosion inside (visible through the drain valve) and that replacement was the right call. Attempting to repair would only delay a failure by months at most.

They replaced it with a heat pump water heater — the Rheem ProTerra 50-gallon, which qualified for the federal 25C tax credit (30% of cost, up to $600 specifically for the water heater) plus a $300 utility rebate from their electric utility.

Installed cost: $1,600 (after a sale on the unit) plus $450 installation = $2,050 total. Federal tax credit: $600. Utility rebate: $300. Net cost: $1,150.

Their electric hot water costs dropped by approximately $480 per year compared to what the old electric resistance water heater would have cost. The payback on the net $1,150 investment was 2.4 years.

**The lessons:**
- Acting on the inspector's recommendation at month eight meant acting before a crisis. They had budgeted for it, they had time to research options, they had time to get a plumber who was not charging emergency rates, and they had time to choose the most efficient technology.
- The combination of a federal tax credit and a utility rebate made the heat pump water heater cost less than a standard electric tank would have cost — and its operating costs are dramatically lower.
- Doing the research on available incentives before making the purchase — not after — is the key. The 25C credit requires the purchase be of a qualifying product. The utility rebate required an application before installation.

## Case Study 10: The House That Taught Them Everything

**The situation:** This final case study is not about a single incident. It is about Jennifer, who bought a 1952 brick cape cod in a well-established neighborhood for $340,000. She was single, 31 years old, a first-time buyer with a 10% down payment, an emergency fund of $5,000, and absolutely no experience with home maintenance beyond changing light bulbs.

In five years, here is what happened:

**Year 1:** The main water shutoff valve — an old gate valve that the home inspector noted was "hard to operate" — seized when she tried to exercise it after reading Post 1 (or its real-world equivalent). A plumber replaced it and the immediately adjacent section of galvanized pipe with a ball valve and copper. Cost: $340. She had the plumber point out everything he saw while he was there — a reasonable investment in education.

The HVAC was 14 years old. She signed up for an HVAC maintenance contract ($189/year, two visits included) and had both a fall and spring service done. In the spring, the technician found a capacitor failing and replaced it proactively for $85 parts and labor. She avoided a summer AC failure.

She cleaned gutters in April and November, on schedule. She replaced every smoke and CO detector in the house — they were all more than 10 years old. She tested all GFCI outlets. Two of them were non-functional; she had an electrician replace them.

**Year 2:** The kitchen sink faucet began dripping. She watched one YouTube tutorial, went to the hardware store, bought the correct cartridge, replaced it herself in 45 minutes. Cost: $22. First successful plumbing repair.

In August, the upstairs toilet started running intermittently. She recognized it immediately (see Case Study 5), bought a flapper that evening, replaced it in 10 minutes. Cost: $6.

She got her soil tested, adjusted the pH with limestone, and overseeded the thin back lawn in September. By the following spring, the back lawn was substantially better. Cost: $80.

She pulled the refrigerator out, cleaned the coils. Disgusting amount of dog hair (her rescue greyhound). Done. Cost: $0.

**Year 3:** The furnace — now 17 years old — had a rough heating season. Two service calls. The second technician recommended replacement. She did not panic. She called two additional HVAC companies for their opinions and quotes. All three agreed: replace. She chose a cold-climate heat pump over a new gas furnace — the federal ITC covered 30% of the cost and the utility rebate covered another $1,000. She paid $6,200 net (after incentives) for a system that heats and cools and costs dramatically less per month to operate. Heating and cooling bills dropped by approximately $75 per month.

She also had the ductwork inspected during this project and had two disconnected sections repaired and the accessible ducts sealed with mastic. The contractor found that 22% of conditioned air had been leaking into the attic. After sealing, the house felt more comfortable and the HVAC ran fewer cycles.

**Year 4:** The roof — a 22-year-old asphalt shingle roof — was inspected by a roofer she had on her contractor list. The verdict: 2 to 4 years of life remaining, condition fair. She used this as a planning horizon. She started setting aside $250 per month into a dedicated roof replacement fund.

She discovered a slow drip under the kitchen sink — the supply line to the hot water faucet had a pinhole leak at the braided section. She shut off the supply valve under the sink, caught the drip in a bowl, and called a plumber the next morning (not emergency rates). The supply line was replaced for $85 labor. No damage. The floor of the cabinet was dry. She had caught it in the monthly check.

She had the chimney inspected and swept — she used the fireplace a few times a year and had never had it done. The sweep found moderate creosote accumulation and cleared it. He also found that the damper was stuck in a partially open position, wasting significant heat. Repair: $150. Well worth it.

**Year 5:** She sold the house. The inspector her buyer hired found essentially nothing beyond normal maintenance items for a 57-year-old house — the systems were all in good working order, recently serviced, well-documented. There were no negotiation items. Her home sold in two weeks at asking price. The buyers' agent told her it was the best-documented home they had seen in years of transactions: a binder with service records, appliance manuals, warranty documents, paint colors, contractor contacts, permits — everything.

Her profit on the sale, net of her transaction costs: approximately $68,000. In five years. On a house she loved living in, that taught her more than any guide could have.

**The lessons from five years:**
- Maintenance is cumulative. Every task done correctly adds up to a house that costs less and sells better.
- The knowledge accumulates too. By year three, Jennifer was a competent homeowner who understood her systems, knew her contractors, and could recognize a developing problem before it became a crisis.
- The emotional arc of home ownership for most first-time buyers goes: excitement → anxiety → competence → confidence. The anxiety phase is real but temporary. It is primarily the anxiety of not knowing. Every system you understand, every repair you navigate, every contractor relationship you build reduces it.
- The financial arc is similar: stress → discipline → stability → wealth. The early years are financially tight. The maintenance fund needs to be built. The emergency fund needs to be rebuilt after use. But after a few years, the equity is real, the maintenance is routine, and the house is working for you as much as you are working for it.

## Part 2: The Common Threads

Looking across these ten cases, several themes emerge consistently.

### Water Wins When You Are Not Paying Attention

Cases 2, 3, and 7 are all fundamentally about water — water that was directed somewhere it should not go, water that leaked slowly and invisibly, water falling as rain from a height. In every case, the water won because the homeowner was not paying attention. In every case, attentive maintenance would have caught the problem before the expensive phase.

Water is patient. It finds every gap, every failed seal, every moment of inattention. The homeowner's job is to be more patient.

### The Cost of Ignoring Something You Know

Cases 1 and 5 are about situations where the homeowner knew something was wrong — the inspection said the furnace was old, the toilet was audibly running — and chose not to act. In both cases, the inaction was significantly more expensive than the action would have been.

The psychology is understandable: after closing, money is tight, and every item on the list feels like one more expense you cannot afford. But the way to think about it is opposite: addressing the known-but-manageable problem while it is still manageable is how you avoid the expensive emergency version of the same problem.

### Verification Is Not Paranoia

Cases 4 and 6 are both about trusting instead of verifying. Trusting that the contractor was licensed. Trusting that the addition was permitted. Verification — license lookup, permit search, reference calls — takes minutes and costs nothing. The failure to verify cost these homeowners thousands.

Trust is a reasonable starting point in human relationships. In contractor relationships and real estate transactions, it is the starting point for conversations, not for contracts and payments.

### The Inspector Is Not Infallible

Case 3 illustrates that home inspectors — despite their skills and experience — work under constraints. They cannot inspect what they cannot access. They cannot see inside walls. They spend 3 to 4 hours on a house that took years to develop its problems. The inspection report is the beginning of due diligence, not its end. In high-risk situations (limited access areas, termite-prone climates, old plumbing or electrical), specialist inspections are worth every dollar.

### Knowledge Is the Best Tool

Jennifer's story in Case 10 is not a story about having money, or having a spouse to share the load, or having any particular technical skill. It is a story about learning, consistently, and applying what she learned. Every repair she navigated — the faucet cartridge, the toilet flapper, the supply line she shut off before calling the plumber — added to her knowledge and her confidence. Every service she had done educationally — being present when the HVAC tech serviced the system, asking the plumber to explain what he was seeing — added to her understanding.

Home ownership is learnable. The learning is not optional — it costs you either in time or in money, and the time version is cheaper.

- [ ] Winterize irrigation system

## Part 5: Final Thoughts

You bought a house. That was brave. It is a bigger commitment than it looked from the outside, and it requires more from you — financially, physically, intellectually, emotionally — than anyone fully warns you about.

But it is also genuinely worth it, for most people, most of the time. The stability of a home that is yours. The freedom to paint walls and plant gardens and hang things without asking permission. The pride of a space that reflects what you care about. The wealth that builds slowly but reliably in equity. The knowledge that accumulates — about your specific home, about the systems that keep you comfortable, about your neighborhood and neighbors and community.

Home ownership is a practice, not a state. You do not arrive at being a good homeowner. You practice it, every month, every season, every year. The practice gets easier. The crises get smaller relative to your capacity to handle them. The costs get more predictable as the surprises diminish.

You will get there. Everyone who has ever owned a home started exactly where you are right now: standing in an empty space that was now somehow, improbably, theirs.

Take care of it. It will take care of you.
