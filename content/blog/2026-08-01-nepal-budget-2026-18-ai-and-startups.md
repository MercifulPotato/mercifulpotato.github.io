Every budget contains a pitch about the future — the eye-catching, forward-looking commitments meant to signal vision and ambition, to capture headlines, and to convey that the government is modern and looking ahead. For this new government, swept to power by a young, frustrated electorate hungry for a different and more modern Nepal, the future pitch is especially prominent, and it centres on technology: a sovereign artificial-intelligence facility, support for startups and innovation, incentives for the information-technology sector, and the modernisation of the state's assets through privatisation. This is the budget's "Digital Nepal" story — its vision of a country leaping into the technological future.

This post examines that pitch with the particular skepticism it deserves — not because technology and innovation are unworthy goals (they are genuinely worthy, and some of these measures are sound), but because the future pitch is, of all parts of a budget, the one most prone to a specific deception: the *buzzword* — the shiny, headline-grabbing announcement that signals vision while being too small, too vague, or too constrained to amount to a serious, funded commitment. We will examine the most striking announcement — a sovereign AI compute centre — and ask, by looking at what such a thing actually requires and what the budget actually provides, whether it is a serious commitment or political signalling. We will look at the startup and IT measures, which are more substantial. And we will examine the privatisation moves, including the sale of state assets and a new vehicle for managing bad debts. By the end you will be able to read a budget's future pitch and distinguish the serious, funded commitments from the buzzwords sized for headlines rather than impact.

## The headline: a sovereign AI compute centre

The most attention-grabbing technology announcement in the budget is the proposal for Nepal's first *sovereign artificial-intelligence compute centre* — a national facility for the computing power that artificial intelligence requires. This is exactly the kind of announcement designed to capture headlines and signal that Nepal is at the technological frontier, joining the global rush toward artificial intelligence. It sounds visionary, modern, ambitious. And it is precisely the kind of announcement that this post's skepticism is built to examine, because the gap between such a vision and what it would actually require — and what the budget actually provides — is the heart of the matter.

Let us understand what an "AI compute centre" actually is and what it would genuinely require, because only then can we judge whether the announcement is serious. *Artificial intelligence*, in its current form, depends on enormous amounts of *computing power* — specialised, expensive computer hardware (particularly specialised chips, often called GPUs, originally designed for graphics but now the workhorses of AI) running in large data centres, consuming vast amounts of electricity, and requiring sophisticated infrastructure to house, power, cool, and operate them. A "compute centre" for AI is, essentially, a large, specialised data centre packed with this expensive hardware. Building and running one requires, at minimum, four things, and each is a serious challenge for Nepal: first, *enormous capital* — the specialised AI chips alone are extraordinarily expensive, and a serious AI compute facility can cost tens or hundreds of millions of dollars, or far more for a large one; second, *abundant, reliable electricity* — AI data centres are voracious consumers of power, requiring large amounts of reliable electricity (and recall, with some irony, that this is the country whose budget *taxes* electricity consumption and whose power supply, while improving, is seasonal and constrained); third, *the specialised chips themselves* — which Nepal does not manufacture and must import, with scarce foreign currency, and which are, moreover, subject to international export controls that restrict their sale, so that even buying them may be difficult; and fourth, *specialised talent* — the engineers and scientists with the expertise to build and run such a facility and to do anything useful with it, a scarce resource in a country that loses much of its technical talent to emigration.

Now apply the test: what does the budget actually provide for this and for technology generally? The allocation to the Ministry of Science, Technology and Innovation is about **NPR 4 billion** — around **USD 27 million**. That figure is the reality check, and it is sobering. NPR 4 billion (USD 27 million) is the *entire* allocation for the science, technology and innovation ministry — covering all its activities, of which a sovereign AI compute centre would be only one. And USD 27 million is, against the actual cost of a serious AI compute facility, a *tiny* sum — far less than what a single serious AI data centre would cost, let alone a sovereign national capability of the kind the announcement implies. The specialised chips alone for a serious facility could cost many times the entire STI budget. So the arithmetic reveals a stark gap: the announcement promises a sovereign AI compute centre — a thing that would require enormous capital, abundant power, scarce chips, and specialised talent — while the budget provides, for all of science and technology, a sum that is a small fraction of what such a facility would actually cost. The announcement and the funding do not match.

What should a citizen conclude from this gap? The honest conclusion, applying the buzzword skepticism, is that the "sovereign AI compute centre" is, on the evidence of the funding, far more likely to be *political signalling* — a buzzword designed to capture headlines and signal that Nepal is modern and at the frontier — than a serious, funded commitment to actually build such a facility. The vision is announced; the funding to realise it is absent; and the constraints (power, chips, talent, capital) are formidable and unaddressed. This does not mean nothing will happen — perhaps some modest computing facility, a fraction of the grand vision, might be established, or perhaps the announcement is intended to attract foreign investment or partnership to fund the real thing — but it means the grand announcement of a "sovereign AI compute centre" should be read with heavy skepticism, as a statement of aspiration and a piece of modern-sounding signalling rather than a funded plan to actually build a serious AI capability. The gap between the visionary announcement and the tiny funding is exactly the signature of buzzword budgeting, and a citizen should recognise it as such — welcoming the aspiration toward technology while not mistaking a headline-grabbing announcement, unmatched by funding and unaddressed by the constraints, for a real commitment.

## The startup and IT measures: more substantial

Not all the technology pitch is buzzword, and fairness requires recognising the parts that are more substantial and better-grounded. The budget's measures for *startups* and the *information-technology sector* are, on the whole, more serious and better-suited to Nepal's actual situation than the AI compute centre, and they deserve more credit.

The budget provides for a **Nepal Enterprise Facility** with an allocation on the order of NPR 500 million (around USD 3.3 million) — a fund to support enterprises, including startups. And it connects to the broader push, which we met in the business post, to build a startup ecosystem: the limited-liability-partnership law to enable venture-capital and private-equity funding, and the various supports for young companies. A startup is a young, innovative, high-growth-potential company, often technology-based, and a thriving startup ecosystem can be a powerful engine of innovation, job creation, and economic dynamism — exactly what a country seeking to modernise its economy and create opportunity for its young people might want. So support for startups is a reasonable and well-motivated goal. The cautions are familiar: the Nepal Enterprise Facility, at NPR 500 million, is modest; building a startup ecosystem requires far more than a fund — it requires the funding ecosystem (the venture capital the LLP law aims to enable), the talent, the supportive regulation, the market, and the broader business environment we discussed, much of which faces the binding constraints (power, connectivity, policy stability) that no budget measure quickly fixes. So the startup support is a reasonable start, but modest, and its effect depends on the broader ecosystem developing. Still, it is more grounded than the AI compute centre — a genuine if modest support for a genuine opportunity.

More substantial still are the *information-technology export incentives* we previewed in the business post: a **50 percent income-tax exemption on IT-export income**, and an exemption related to *sweat equity* in the IT sector. Let us understand why these are among the more sensible technology measures. We have established throughout this series that Nepal desperately needs *exports* — productive activities that earn foreign currency — and that its geography (landlocked, mountainous) blocks many traditional export paths. *Information-technology services* — software development, IT outsourcing, digital services sold to clients abroad — are a striking exception: they require *talent and connectivity* rather than ports and heavy infrastructure, so they are one of the few export paths *not* blocked by Nepal's geography. A young person with skills and an internet connection can, in principle, export IT services to clients anywhere in the world, earning foreign currency without any goods crossing a border. So IT exports are perhaps the single most promising export opportunity suited to Nepal's actual constraints, and incentivising them — through a 50 percent tax exemption on IT-export income — is a genuinely sound and well-targeted measure. The *sweat-equity* exemption is also thoughtful: "sweat equity" refers to ownership stakes that founders and employees earn through their *work* (their "sweat") rather than by investing money — a crucial mechanism in startups, where founders and early employees are often paid partly in ownership rather than cash; an exemption that eases the tax treatment of such sweat equity supports the startup model of compensating talent with ownership, which is exactly how technology startups attract and retain people when they cannot pay large salaries. So the IT-export and sweat-equity measures are among the more sophisticated and better-targeted elements of the budget's technology pitch — aimed at a genuine, geography-suited opportunity (IT exports) and at the actual mechanics of how startups work (sweat equity). They deserve real credit, and they show that the budget's technology pitch is not all buzzword — it contains genuinely sensible, well-grounded measures alongside the headline-grabbing AI announcement.

So the technology pitch is mixed, and a fair assessment holds both parts. The *sovereign AI compute centre* is, on the evidence of the funding and the constraints, largely buzzword — a visionary announcement unmatched by funding and unaddressed by the formidable requirements, to be read as signalling rather than a serious plan. But the *IT-export incentives and sweat-equity exemption*, and to a lesser extent the *startup support*, are genuinely sensible, well-targeted measures aimed at real, geography-suited opportunities and at the actual workings of startups. A citizen should distinguish these — skepticism toward the headline AI buzzword, credit toward the grounded IT and startup measures — rather than dismissing or celebrating the whole technology pitch indiscriminately. The future pitch contains both the shiny announcement sized for headlines and the modest, sensible measures sized for actual effect, and telling them apart is exactly the skill this post aims to build.

## The privatisation and restructuring moves

The budget also addresses the *state's assets and enterprises* through measures of privatisation and restructuring, which deserve examination because they involve the government's ownership of, and exit from, parts of the economy. Let us understand the main moves and the issues they raise.

The budget mentions the *sale of shares* in Nepal Telecom — the state-owned telecommunications company — which is a form of *privatisation*: the government selling part of its ownership in a state enterprise to private investors. Why might a government do this? *Privatisation* — transferring state-owned enterprises or stakes in them to private ownership — is pursued for several reasons: to raise revenue (the sale brings money to the government, helping with the deficit), to improve the efficiency of the enterprise (on the theory that private ownership and market discipline run businesses better than the state does), and to reduce the state's role in commercial activities better left to the private sector. Selling a stake in Nepal Telecom could raise revenue and, in theory, improve the company's efficiency. The issues, as always, are about execution and terms: privatisation can be done well (a fair sale at a good price that genuinely improves the enterprise) or badly (assets sold too cheaply, to the well-connected, in ways that enrich a few rather than benefiting the public), and it can be controversial (selling a strategic state asset raises questions about national control, about whether the public is getting fair value, and about who benefits). So the sale of Nepal Telecom shares is a real privatisation move whose merits depend entirely on the terms, the price, the transparency, and the buyers — a citizen should watch *how* it is done, not just that it is announced, because privatisations are notoriously vulnerable to the assets being sold cheaply to the well-connected.

The budget also mentions restructuring in *civil aviation* — historically, proposals to split the Civil Aviation Authority of Nepal, which has combined the roles of *regulator* (overseeing aviation safety) and *operator* (running the airports and air-navigation services), into separate bodies. Combining these roles is widely seen as a problem (a body that both runs the airports and regulates their safety has a conflict of interest — it is regulating itself), and separating them into a distinct regulator and a distinct operator is a standard and sensible reform that international aviation bodies have long urged for Nepal. So the civil-aviation restructuring, if actually carried out, addresses a genuine institutional problem and is a reasonable reform — though, like all such reforms, its value depends on actual implementation, and such restructurings have been proposed before without being completed.

Most interesting is the proposal for a **National Asset Management Company** — and this deserves explanation because it is a sophisticated financial instrument whose name is opaque. A "national asset management company," in this context, is what is often called a *bad bank* or *asset-management company* for distressed debt: a vehicle that takes over the *bad loans* (loans that are not being repaid, "non-performing assets") from banks and financial institutions, removing these bad debts from the banks' books so that the banks are cleaned up and can lend again, while the asset-management company works to recover what it can from the bad loans. Why might a government create such a thing? When banks accumulate too many bad loans, they become weak and reluctant to lend, which can choke the flow of credit to the economy; moving the bad loans to a dedicated vehicle can clean up the banks and restore lending. So a national asset-management company can be a useful tool for dealing with bad debt in the banking system. But it raises real issues that a citizen should understand: it can become a way of *bailing out* banks and their borrowers at public expense (if the bad loans are taken over at inflated prices, the public absorbs the loss while the banks and defaulting borrowers are relieved), it can create *moral hazard* (if banks and borrowers expect their bad loans to be taken over, they may lend and borrow more recklessly), and its operation requires great care and transparency to avoid becoming a vehicle for absorbing the consequences of bad banking at public expense. So the National Asset Management Company is a sophisticated and potentially useful tool, but one whose merits depend entirely on how it is structured and operated — and whose opaque name conceals what is essentially a mechanism for dealing with bad debt that could, if mishandled, transfer the costs of bad lending to the public. A citizen should look past the bland name to understand what it actually is and to ask how it will be structured to avoid becoming a public bailout of private banking failures.

## Buzzword budgeting: the general pattern

Let us draw out the general pattern that the technology pitch illustrates, because it applies beyond technology to any part of a budget's future pitch. *Buzzword budgeting* is the practice of including in a budget the shiny, fashionable, headline-grabbing announcements — the things that sound modern and visionary and capture attention — sized and designed for their *signalling* and *publicity* value rather than for actual *impact*, often unmatched by serious funding and unaddressed by the real constraints. The "sovereign AI compute centre" is the archetype: it invokes the most fashionable technology of the moment (artificial intelligence), sounds visionary and modern, captures headlines, and signals that the government is at the frontier — while being unmatched by the funding it would actually require and unaddressed by the formidable constraints (power, chips, talent, capital) that would actually determine whether it could be built. The buzzword's purpose is achieved the moment it is announced and reported — the signal is sent, the headline captured, the impression of vision created — regardless of whether anything is ever actually built, and indeed the announcement may never have been intended to result in an actual facility at all.

Why is buzzword budgeting so tempting, especially for this government? Because the *announcement* of a fashionable, visionary commitment delivers its political value immediately and cheaply — it signals modernity and ambition, it captures headlines, it pleases an audience (here, the young, modernity-hungry electorate that elected this government) — while the *non-delivery* is diffuse, slow, and largely invisible, and the buzzword can be quietly forgotten or replaced with next year's fashionable announcement. A government can build a reputation for vision and modernity by accumulating buzzwords, none of which need ever become real, because the value is in the announcing, not the building. And technology buzzwords are especially potent for a government wanting to seem forward-looking and to appeal to the young and modern, because nothing signals "future" like the latest technological fashion. So a budget's future pitch is the part most prone to buzzwords — to announcements valued for their signalling rather than their substance.

The defence is to *test every future-pitch announcement against its funding and its constraints*. When a budget makes a visionary, fashionable, headline-grabbing announcement — an AI centre, a digital transformation, a technological leap — ask: Is it *funded* at a level remotely commensurate with what it would actually require, or is the funding a tiny fraction of the real cost (the signature of a buzzword)? Are the *real constraints* — for an AI centre, the power, the chips, the talent, the capital — acknowledged and addressed, or ignored? Is there a *credible plan* to actually deliver it, or only an announcement? And, the sharpest question: does this announcement seem sized for *impact* (seriously funded, constraints addressed, plan credible) or for *headlines* (fashionable, vague, underfunded, constraints ignored)? A citizen who tests every future-pitch announcement against its funding and constraints, who refuses to be impressed by a fashionable buzzword unmatched by the resources to realise it, and who distinguishes the seriously-funded commitments from the headline-grabbing signals, cannot be deceived by buzzword budgeting. And such a citizen reads this budget's technology pitch correctly: heavy skepticism toward the underfunded, constraint-defying "sovereign AI compute centre" (a buzzword sized for headlines), genuine credit toward the well-targeted IT-export and startup measures (sensible commitments sized for actual effect). The deception thrives on the public being impressed by fashionable announcements; the defence is to test them against funding and constraints, and to credit only the commitments that are actually serious.

## A worked example: what USD 27 million can and cannot buy

Make the AI-compute-centre gap concrete by examining what the technology allocation could actually buy against what the announcement promises, because the arithmetic is the most powerful skepticism.

The announcement promises a *sovereign AI compute centre* — a national facility for serious AI computing. The entire science, technology and innovation allocation is about NPR 4 billion, around USD 27 million, and a sovereign AI compute centre would be only one of many things that ministry must fund. Now consider what a serious AI compute facility actually costs. The specialised chips at the heart of such a facility — high-end AI accelerators — cost, each, a substantial fraction of, or more than, the price of a car, and a serious facility requires many of them, often hundreds or thousands; the chips alone for a serious cluster can cost tens or hundreds of millions of dollars. Add the data-centre infrastructure to house, power, and cool them, the reliable power supply (large and constant), the networking, and the specialised staff to build and run it, and the cost of a genuinely serious sovereign AI capability runs to many tens of millions of dollars at the very least, and far more for anything substantial — a multiple, often a large multiple, of the *entire* USD 27 million STI budget. So the arithmetic is stark: USD 27 million, even if the *entire* science-and-technology budget were devoted to it (which it cannot be, since the ministry has many other responsibilities), would not buy a serious sovereign AI compute centre — it might buy a modest cluster of chips, a small facility, a fraction of the grand vision, but not the sovereign national AI capability the announcement implies. And this is before confronting the non-financial constraints: the chips face export controls that may prevent their purchase at any price; the power supply is seasonal and constrained (and taxed); the specialised talent is scarce. So what USD 27 million can actually buy — a modest facility at most, if the constraints can even be navigated — falls far short of what the announcement promises — a sovereign AI compute centre.

The example reveals, through simple arithmetic, why the AI compute centre is a buzzword rather than a serious commitment: the funding is a small fraction of the cost, and the constraints are formidable and unaddressed. And it illustrates the general defence — testing an announcement against what its funding could actually buy. A citizen who asks "what could the allocated money actually buy, against what the announcement promises?" immediately sees the gap between the visionary announcement and the modest reality the funding allows, and recognises the announcement as signalling rather than a funded plan. The arithmetic is the most powerful tool against buzzwords: a serious commitment is funded at a level commensurate with its cost; a buzzword is funded at a fraction of its cost, the gap revealing that the announcement was never a serious plan to actually build the thing. By contrast, applying the same arithmetic to the IT-export incentive reveals it as serious: a tax exemption costs the government forgone revenue but requires no enormous capital outlay, and it is well-matched to its goal of encouraging IT exports — so the arithmetic confirms the IT measure as a genuine commitment and the AI centre as a buzzword. The same test — funding against cost and purpose — distinguishes the two, and a citizen who applies it reads the technology pitch correctly.

## For the technically minded: the tech measures and a bad-loan sketch

For readers who work with numbers, here are the technology and privatisation measures in schematic form, with an assessment of which are serious and which are buzzword:

| Measure | Amount / form | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Sovereign AI compute centre | (within STI's ~NPR 4B) | Buzzword — funding a fraction of real cost; constraints unaddressed |
| Science, Technology & Innovation ministry | NPR 4 billion (USD ~27M) | Small for the ambitions attached to it |
| Nepal Enterprise Facility | NPR 500 million (USD ~3.3M) | Modest but real startup support |
| 50% IT-export income exemption | Tax exemption | Serious — well-targeted to a geography-suited opportunity |
| IT sweat-equity exemption | Tax treatment | Serious — fits how startups actually work |
| Nepal Telecom share sale | Privatisation | Real — merits depend on terms and transparency |
| Civil Aviation Authority split | Restructuring | Sensible reform — depends on implementation |
| National Asset Management Company | Bad-debt vehicle | Useful but risky — watch for public bailout of bad banking |

Now a sketch of what a National Asset Management Company actually does, which its opaque name conceals:

```text
# A National Asset Management Company ("bad bank") takes bad loans off banks' books.
def asset_management_company(banks_bad_loans, purchase_price):
    # The AMC BUYS the non-performing loans from the banks:
    for loan in banks_bad_loans:
        amc.acquire(loan, price=purchase_price(loan))   # bank's book is cleaned up
    # Then the AMC tries to RECOVER what it can from the bad loans over time:
    recovered = sum(amc.attempt_recovery(loan) for loan in amc.holdings)
    public_cost = total_paid_to_banks - recovered       # what the PUBLIC ultimately absorbs
    return public_cost

# THE KEY QUESTION -- buried in 'purchase_price':
#   If the AMC buys bad loans at a FAIR (low) price reflecting their true value,
#     the banks bear their losses and the public cost is limited. GOOD.
#   If the AMC OVERPAYS for bad loans (bails out the banks),
#     the public absorbs the banks' losses -- a hidden bailout. BAD.
# The opaque name "National Asset Management Company" conceals that this is a mechanism
# whose fairness depends entirely on the purchase price and the transparency of operation.
# Watch the price; watch who benefits.
```

The code reveals what the bland name hides: a National Asset Management Company is a mechanism for taking bad loans off banks' books, and its fairness to the public depends entirely on the price at which it buys those loans — a fair price limits the public cost, while overpaying becomes a hidden bailout of the banks at public expense. A developer or analyst evaluating it would focus on the purchase price and the transparency, because those determine whether it is a sensible cleanup or a public bailout. And a citizen, equipped with the same understanding, knows to look past the opaque name to ask how the vehicle is structured and who bears the cost — exactly the skepticism that the bland, technical name is designed to deflect. The general lesson, applying across the technology and privatisation pitch: test each measure against its funding, its constraints, and its actual mechanics, distinguishing the serious commitments (the IT incentives) from the buzzwords (the AI centre) and understanding what the opaque names (the asset-management company) actually conceal.

## How budgets deceive: the shiny object

The technique of deception for this post is the one the future pitch most embodies, and it is among the most effective because it exploits our attraction to novelty and vision: **the shiny object** — the fashionable, visionary, headline-grabbing announcement, sized and designed for its signalling and publicity value rather than for actual impact, which captures attention and creates an impression of vision while amounting, in substance, to little.

Here is the move, of which the AI compute centre is the archetype. A budget includes a *shiny object* — an announcement invoking the most fashionable idea of the moment (artificial intelligence, digital transformation, a technological leap), which sounds visionary and modern, captures headlines, and signals that the government is forward-looking and at the frontier. The shiny object's value is in its *attention and signalling* — it impresses, it pleases the audience hungry for modernity, it dominates the coverage — and this value is delivered the moment it is announced, regardless of whether it is ever realised. Meanwhile the shiny object is typically *unmatched by serious funding* (a fraction of what it would actually cost), *unaddressed by the real constraints* (which are ignored in the announcement), and often *vague about actual delivery* — because its purpose was never really to be built, but to signal and to capture attention. The deception is in the gap between the *impression* the shiny object creates (vision, modernity, a leap into the future) and its *substance* (an underfunded, constraint-defying, possibly-never-intended announcement). The public, dazzled by the fashionable vision, credits the government with vision and modernity, while the shiny object quietly fails to materialise, to be replaced next year by a new shiny object. And the shiny object also serves to *distract* — its glamour draws attention away from the more mundane but more consequential parts of the budget (the recurrent spending, the deficit, the execution problems), focusing the conversation on the exciting future rather than the difficult present.

The defence is to *test every shiny object against funding, constraints, and substance, and to keep your attention on the consequential rather than the glamorous*. When a budget announces a fashionable, visionary, attention-grabbing commitment, ask: Is it actually *funded* commensurately with its cost, or is the funding a fraction of what it would require (revealing a shiny object, not a serious plan)? Are the real *constraints* addressed, or ignored? Is there a credible plan to *deliver* it, or only an announcement designed to impress? And — resisting the distraction — am I letting the glamour of the shiny object draw my attention away from the more consequential, less glamorous parts of the budget that actually determine the country's finances and the people's lives? A citizen who tests shiny objects against their funding and constraints, who refuses to credit a fashionable announcement as vision merely because it sounds modern, and who keeps attention on the consequential rather than being dazzled by the glamorous, cannot be deceived by the shiny object. And such a citizen reads this budget's future pitch correctly — recognising the "sovereign AI compute centre" as a shiny object (fashionable, underfunded, constraint-defying, designed for headlines) while crediting the genuinely sensible measures (the IT incentives) and, crucially, not letting the glamour of the AI announcement distract from the consequential realities — the recurrent burden, the deficit, the execution problems — that the earlier posts examined and that matter far more to Nepal's actual future than any buzzword. The deception thrives on dazzling the public with fashionable vision; the defence is to test the vision against the resources to realise it, and to keep one's eyes on what actually matters.
