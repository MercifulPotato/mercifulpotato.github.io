## Part 1: The Outdoor Half of the Job

When you bought your house, you bought everything from the property line to the property line — the grass, the trees, the shrubs, the driveway, the fence, the garden beds, all of it. Depending on your background, this may be an exciting opportunity or a source of genuine anxiety. Many first-time homeowners have never maintained a lawn, never used a lawn mower, never pruned a shrub, and have very little idea how to keep living things alive outdoors at scale.

This post is a comprehensive introduction to the outdoor systems of your home: the lawn and its specific needs, the equipment you need to maintain it, the water required to keep it healthy, the trees on your property and the risks they carry, and the broader landscape — beds, shrubs, hardscaping, and soil.

The goal is not to make you a professional landscaper. It is to give you the knowledge to maintain what you have, make informed decisions about improvements, and know when to hire help versus when to do it yourself.

## Part 2: Know Your Grass

This is the foundation of everything. There are two broad categories of lawn grass in North America: **cool-season grasses** and **warm-season grasses**. Understanding which type you have dictates when you fertilize, when you seed, when you water, how short you mow, and what problems to expect.

### Cool-Season Grasses

Cool-season grasses thrive in temperatures between 60°F and 75°F. They grow actively in spring and fall, go semi-dormant or slow significantly in summer heat, and can remain green through mild winters.

**Kentucky Bluegrass:** The "classic" American lawn grass in northern states. Dense, dark green, spreads by rhizomes (underground stems), and produces a very attractive lawn. Requires more water and more fertilizer than other cool-season grasses. Less drought-tolerant. Slow to establish from seed (4 to 6 weeks to germinate). Most productive in USDA zones 3 to 7.

**Tall Fescue:** More drought-tolerant than Kentucky bluegrass, with a coarser texture. Excellent disease and wear resistance. Does not spread — it is a bunch-type grass that must be seeded rather than spreading on its own, which means bare spots persist unless overseeded. More adaptable to partial shade. Very popular in the transition zone (zones 5 to 7, the area where neither cool- nor warm-season grasses are perfect).

**Fine Fescues (Creeping Red, Chewings, Hard, Sheep Fescue):** Very shade-tolerant, require less water and less fertilizer than other cool-season grasses, and are the best option for low-maintenance lawns or difficult sites. Not as traffic-tolerant as bluegrass or tall fescue.

**Perennial Ryegrass:** Fast to germinate (5 to 7 days), bright green color, good wear tolerance, often used in seed blends. Tends to be less cold-hardy than bluegrass or fescue and may thin or die out in harsh winters. Also used widely as a nurse grass (quick temporary cover while slower grasses establish).

**Seeding time for cool-season grasses:** Late summer to early fall (late August through October in most of zones 3 to 7) is the ideal seeding window. Soil temperatures are warm enough for germination (above 50°F), air temperatures are cooling and favorable for young grass, and there is typically adequate rainfall. This timing also avoids the competition from summer annual weeds that would outcompete new seedlings.

Spring seeding is the secondary option — possible, but the new grass has less time to establish before summer heat stress arrives, and it must compete with spring weed germination.

### Warm-Season Grasses

Warm-season grasses thrive in temperatures between 80°F and 95°F. They go dormant (turn brown) in fall when temperatures drop below roughly 50°F and green up again in late spring when soil temperatures rise above 60°F. They are grown primarily in the Southeast, Gulf Coast, Southwest, and mild coastal regions.

**Bermuda Grass:** The dominant warm-season grass across the South. Extremely heat and drought tolerant, spreads aggressively by stolons and rhizomes (great for filling in, challenging to contain), very wear-tolerant, and requires significant mowing frequency during the growing season. Not shade tolerant.

**Zoysia:** Dense, carpet-like, moderate drought tolerance, reasonable shade tolerance. Slower to establish than Bermuda but once established it is very competitive against weeds. Goes dormant in winter. Available in several varieties with different textures.

**St. Augustine:** Popular in the Gulf Coast and Florida. Broad-blade, shade-tolerant (the most shade-tolerant warm-season grass), fast-spreading, but not cold-hardy and not tolerant of heavy foot traffic. Susceptible to chinch bugs.

**Centipede:** Low-maintenance, low-fertility-requirement, slow-growing. Often called the "lazy man's grass." Very popular in the Southeast but limited to zones 7 to 9. Sensitive to high pH soils and traffic.

**Buffalo Grass:** Native prairie grass, extremely drought tolerant, very low water requirement, slow growing. Best in zones 5 to 8 in low-rainfall areas. Good ecological choice where water conservation is a priority.

**Seeding / Plugging / Sodding time for warm-season grasses:** Late spring to early summer, when soil temperatures are reliably above 65°F. Bermuda and centipede can be seeded; zoysia and St. Augustine are almost always established via sod or plugs.

### The Transition Zone

A broad band of the United States — roughly from Virginia to Kansas, including the Mid-Atlantic, the Carolinas, Tennessee, Arkansas, Oklahoma, and the southern Plains — falls in the "transition zone" where neither cool- nor warm-season grasses are fully comfortable. This is the hardest zone for lawns, because neither grass type is perfect.

Common approaches:
- Tall Fescue (the most commonly successful option in the transition zone — tolerates both heat and cold reasonably well)
- Cool-season / warm-season mixture with seasonal dominance shifting between them
- Acceptance of less-than-perfect turf during the stress seasons

## Part 3: The Lawn Mower — Choosing, Using, and Maintaining

### Types of Lawn Mowers

**Walk-behind gas mower (self-propelled):** The workhorse of American lawn care. A 21-inch to 22-inch cutting width is standard for most residential lawns. Self-propelled models (the drive system propels the mower rather than requiring you to push it) are worth the extra cost for sloped terrain or large areas. Features to look for: reliable engine (Briggs & Stratton and Honda are well-regarded), rear-wheel drive or all-wheel drive for hills, variable speed drive, bag/mulch/side-discharge capability, easy height adjustment.

**Battery-electric walk-behind mower:** The fastest-growing category in residential mowing. Modern 40V to 80V lithium-ion battery mowers have substantially closed the gap with gas performance for most residential lawns under 1/4 acre. No gas, no oil, no carburetor problems, less maintenance, quieter, no emissions. Battery life on a single charge is typically 30 to 60 minutes depending on the model and conditions. For larger lawns, you need a larger battery or a spare.

**Robotic mower:** Autonomous mowers that run on a schedule, cutting small amounts of grass continuously. They keep the lawn at a consistent height with minimal clippings (the tiny clippings fall into the lawn and decompose). They require installation of a boundary wire to define the cutting area. Most effective on relatively flat, simple-shaped lawns. A good robotic mower costs $800 to $3,000+. Worth considering if your schedule does not accommodate regular mowing — they run without any labor once set up.

**Riding mower / lawn tractor:** For lawns larger than 1/2 acre, a riding mower saves significant time. Entry-level riding mowers (Husqvarna, Cub Cadet, John Deere) start around $1,500 to $2,500 for modest deck sizes (42 to 46 inches). Larger properties benefit from a zero-turn mower, which can mow around obstacles much more efficiently than a traditional riding mower.

**Reel mower (push, non-motorized):** For small, flat lawns and the environmentally conscious. No engine, very quiet, excellent cut quality on short grass. Not appropriate for long grass or rough terrain.

### Mowing Height — The Most Important Mowing Setting

The biggest mistake most homeowners make with their lawn mower is cutting the grass too short. Cutting too short — "scalping" — is one of the most common sources of lawn stress, weed invasion, and turf failure.

The general rule: **never remove more than one-third of the grass blade height in a single mowing.** If your target height is 3 inches, mow when the grass reaches 4 to 4.5 inches.

**Recommended mowing heights by grass type:**

| Grass Type | Recommended Height | Low End | High End |
|------------|-------------------|---------|----------|
| Kentucky Bluegrass | 2.5–3.5 in | 2 in | 4 in |
| Tall Fescue | 3–4 in | 2.5 in | 4.5 in |
| Fine Fescue | 2.5–3.5 in | 2 in | 4 in |
| Perennial Ryegrass | 2–3 in | 1.5 in | 3.5 in |
| Bermuda | 0.5–1.5 in | 0.5 in | 2 in |
| Zoysia | 1–2 in | 0.5 in | 2.5 in |
| St. Augustine | 3.5–4 in | 2.5 in | 4 in |
| Centipede | 1.5–2.5 in | 1 in | 3 in |

In summer heat, raise the cutting height to the upper end of the range — taller grass is more heat and drought tolerant, shades the soil to retain moisture, and has deeper roots. In fall, you can lower slightly as temperatures cool.

### Mowing Frequency

Mow often enough that you never remove more than one-third of the blade. During peak growing season, this may mean mowing every 5 to 7 days for cool-season grasses in spring and fall, every 7 to 10 days in summer. Warm-season grasses during their growing season (June through August) may need mowing every 5 to 7 days.

Do not let the lawn get long and then scalp it — this stresses the grass severely and creates the ideal conditions for weeds.

### Lawn Mower Maintenance

**At the start of each season:**
- Change the engine oil (gas mowers): use the weight specified in the manual, typically SAE 30 or 10W-30
- Replace the air filter
- Replace the spark plug
- Sharpen or replace the blade: a sharp blade cuts cleanly; a dull blade tears the grass, leaving ragged brown tips that are susceptible to disease
- Clean the underside of the deck (remove accumulated grass clippings — they trap moisture and contribute to corrosion)
- Check the tire pressure (self-propelled and riding mowers)
- For riding mowers: also check belts, grease fittings, and battery

**Blade sharpening:** A lawn mower blade should be sharpened at least once per season, more frequently if you mow large areas. You can take the blade to any hardware store or small engine shop for sharpening ($10 to $20), or sharpen it yourself with an angle grinder or a file. A properly sharpened blade has a clean, slightly angled edge, not a razor edge (which would be too brittle).

**At the end of the season (gas mowers):**
- Run the mower dry (run until it runs out of gas) or add fuel stabilizer to the gas tank
- Change the oil
- Clean the deck thoroughly
- Store in a dry location (not outside uncovered)

## Part 4: Fertilizing — What Your Lawn Actually Needs

Fertilizer provides the nutrients that grass needs to grow: primarily nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K) — expressed on every fertilizer bag as the N-P-K ratio.

**Nitrogen (N)** drives leafy green growth and is the primary nutrient most lawns need.
**Phosphorus (P)** supports root development. Established lawns often need little or no additional phosphorus; in fact, excess phosphorus can cause problems in waterways (phosphorus runoff is a major contributor to algae blooms in rivers and lakes). Many municipalities now restrict or prohibit phosphorus in lawn fertilizers except when establishing new turf.
**Potassium (K)** supports overall plant health, disease resistance, and stress tolerance.

### Soil Testing — Do This Before Fertilizing

Before buying any fertilizer, test your soil. A soil test reveals your soil's pH and existing nutrient levels, allowing you to buy and apply only what your lawn actually needs rather than guessing. Over-applying fertilizer is wasteful, expensive, and environmentally harmful.

Soil test kits are available at hardware stores and garden centers for $15 to $25, or you can send a sample to your local university cooperative extension office for a more detailed analysis (usually $10 to $25). The extension office result will include specific recommendations for your lawn's needs.

**Soil pH** is the most important result. Grass grows best in a pH of 6.0 to 7.0. Below 6.0 (acidic), nutrients become less available and moss and weeds thrive. Above 7.5 (alkaline), nutrient availability also decreases. To raise soil pH (more alkaline), apply ground limestone. To lower pH (more acidic), apply elemental sulfur.

### Fertilization Schedule by Grass Type

**Cool-season grasses:**
- **Primary feeding:** Late summer to early fall (Labor Day through October) — this is the most important feeding of the year, supporting root development and storage of nutrients for winter and spring growth
- **Secondary feeding:** Late spring (May to June) — lighter application to support spring growth without forcing excessive top growth in approaching summer heat
- **Skip** summer fertilization of cool-season grasses — summer-fed cool-season grass grows rapidly in the wrong direction, becomes stressed, and disease-prone

**Warm-season grasses:**
- **Primary feeding:** Late spring through summer, during active growth
- **Stop fertilizing** 6 to 8 weeks before the first expected frost — late-season nitrogen fertilization stimulates growth that is vulnerable to frost damage

### Understanding Slow-Release vs. Fast-Release Fertilizers

**Fast-release (water-soluble) nitrogen** produces a rapid flush of green growth but lasts only 3 to 4 weeks. Risk of burning the lawn if over-applied. Less expensive per unit of nitrogen.

**Slow-release nitrogen** (sulfur-coated urea, polymer-coated urea, or organic sources like feather meal or blood meal) releases nitrogen gradually over 8 to 16 weeks, producing steady, even growth without burn risk. More expensive per unit of nitrogen but more effective per application.

Most quality lawn fertilizers use a blend of fast-release and slow-release nitrogen for an initial green-up followed by sustained feeding.

### Application Rates and Methods

Apply fertilizer with a broadcast spreader (rotary) or drop spreader. Calibrate the spreader to apply at the rate specified on the fertilizer bag — over-application causes fertilizer burn (brown streaks where the fertilizer was concentrated) and is wasteful.

Walk at a steady pace in straight overlapping passes. For a broadcast spreader, apply half at the full rate in one direction and the other half in the perpendicular direction — this ensures even coverage.

Water after applying granular fertilizer to move it off the blades and into the soil, unless rain is forecast within 24 hours.

## Part 5: Lawn Watering — Efficiency, Frequency, and Timing

**The target:** Most established lawns need approximately 1 to 1.5 inches of water per week from combined rainfall and irrigation during the growing season. This is not a precise law — it varies with temperature, humidity, grass type, and soil type. Sandy soils need more frequent watering; clay soils hold water longer.

**Deep and infrequent beats shallow and frequent.** Watering deeply (to a depth of 6 to 8 inches) but less frequently trains grass roots to grow deep, making the lawn more drought tolerant. Watering lightly and frequently keeps roots near the surface, where the soil dries out rapidly — creating a lawn that wilts quickly when irrigation is interrupted.

**How to know when to water:** Step on the grass. If it springs back, it does not need water yet. If it stays flat (wilts), it is time to water. Bluish-gray tinges to the grass color and visible footprints that do not spring back also indicate the need for water.

**When to water:** Water early in the morning, when wind is typically low and temperatures are cool. Early morning watering reduces evaporation compared to midday watering, and dries the blades before evening, which reduces disease pressure (fungal diseases thrive on grass that stays wet overnight). Avoid watering in the evening if possible.

**How much is an inch of water?** Place several empty tuna cans or rain gauges in your lawn within the sprinkler pattern and run the system for 30 minutes. Measure the water collected — this tells you how long you need to run your system to apply 1 inch. For most rotary sprinkler heads, 1 inch of water requires 20 to 30 minutes of runtime per zone.

### Irrigation System Basics

**In-ground irrigation systems** consist of underground pipes connecting to sprinkler heads in each zone. A controller (timer) opens electric valves (solenoids) to each zone on a schedule. Properly designed, an in-ground system applies water uniformly and efficiently.

The most important thing to know about your in-ground system: **it is not a "set and forget" system.** Run each zone individually every few weeks and walk through to verify every head is operating correctly. A broken head left unaddressed waters a concentrated small area while leaving adjacent areas dry.

**Smart irrigation controllers** use local weather data (temperature, humidity, evapotranspiration rates, and rainfall) to automatically adjust watering schedules. They avoid watering after rain and increase watering during heat waves. ENERGY STAR and EPA WaterSense certified smart controllers can reduce outdoor water use by 15% to 50% compared to time-clock controllers. The water savings typically pay back the cost of the controller ($80 to $250) within 1 to 2 seasons.

**Rain sensors:** If you do not have a smart controller, at minimum install a rain sensor — an inexpensive ($15 to $30) device that automatically shuts off the irrigation system after rainfall and resumes it when the sensor dries out.

## Part 6: Weed Management

Weeds are the natural opportunists of the plant world — they move into any available niche in the lawn. Understanding what creates those niches is the foundation of weed management.

**The best weed prevention is a dense, healthy lawn.** A thick turf with no bare spots gives weeds nowhere to germinate. Every cultural practice that promotes a dense lawn — proper mowing height, appropriate fertilization, adequate watering, overseeding thin areas — is simultaneously weed prevention.

### Pre-Emergent Herbicides

Pre-emergents work by preventing weed seeds from germinating. They must be applied before weed seeds sprout — applying them to established weeds accomplishes nothing.

**Crabgrass pre-emergent:** Apply in early spring before soil temperatures reach 55°F at a 4-inch depth. This is the most commonly used pre-emergent. Timing is critical — too early and it breaks down before the crabgrass germinates; too late and the crabgrass is already established and unaffected.

Do NOT apply a pre-emergent if you are planning to overseed — it will prevent your grass seed from germinating as well as the weeds.

### Post-Emergent Herbicides

Post-emergents kill weeds that are already growing. They are classified as:
- **Selective:** Kills specific plant types while leaving others unharmed. Broadleaf herbicides (2,4-D, MCPP, dicamba) kill dandelions, clover, and other broadleaf weeds without harming grass. Grass-specific herbicides target certain grass species.
- **Non-selective:** Kills all plants it contacts (glyphosate — Roundup — is the most common). Use very carefully near desired plants. Useful for spot treatment of weeds in beds, cracks in pavement, or complete lawn renovation.

### Natural Weed Control

For homeowners who prefer not to use synthetic herbicides:
- Corn gluten meal is a natural pre-emergent that inhibits root formation in germinating seeds. Less effective than synthetic pre-emergents but has a zero residue concern.
- Hand pulling is effective for low weed populations, especially for taprooted weeds like dandelions — pull when soil is moist to remove the whole root.
- Vinegar (acetic acid) at high concentrations (10% to 20%, not household 5%) is an effective post-emergent for non-selective contact killing. It burns the above-ground portion but does not kill roots of established perennial weeds.

## Part 7: Trees — Your Home's Greatest Asset and Greatest Liability

Trees are perhaps the most valuable thing on a residential property. A mature tree adds thousands of dollars to property value, provides shade that can reduce air conditioning costs by 20% to 30% (for a well-placed large deciduous tree on the south or west side of the house), sequesters carbon, supports wildlife, and transforms the character of a neighborhood.

Trees are also the most dangerous thing on a residential property. A dead tree, a dying tree, or a tree with structural problems can drop branches or fall entirely, injuring people, damaging your house, damaging neighboring property, or killing someone.

Understanding your trees is one of the most important things you can do as a homeowner.

### Tree Species and What They Mean for Your Property

Different tree species have very different maintenance requirements, growth habits, lifespan expectations, and failure modes. Some species are well-suited to residential properties; others are problematic.

**Fast-growing species with structural weaknesses:** Bradford/Callery pear (widely planted but structurally weak and prone to splitting), silver maple (very common but has weak wood and large surface roots that damage driveways and foundations), weeping willow (beautiful but shallow-rooted, messy, and drawn to water lines and sewer pipes), Leyland cypress (fast-growing but prone to disease and failure in wind).

**Long-lived, structurally strong species:** White oak, live oak, bur oak (the oaks are the gold standard of residential trees — long-lived, structurally sound, wildlife-valuable), American beech, tulip poplar (fast-growing but structurally sound), American elm (if resistant variety), sugar maple, northern red maple.

When a tree on your property has a species you do not recognize, have a certified arborist identify it and assess its condition. Knowing what you have is the first step in managing it well.

### Assessing Your Trees for Risk

You do not need to be an arborist to do an initial risk assessment of your trees. Look for these warning signs and bring in an arborist when you see them:

**Dead or dying branches.** Dead branches are brittle, lack leaves (during the growing season), and have gray, cracked, or peeling bark. They can fall without warning. Any dead branch larger than a few inches in diameter that is over a structure, a path, or a vehicle is a liability. It should be removed.

**Co-dominant stems and included bark.** A co-dominant stem is when a tree has two or more main trunks of similar size growing from a single point. These junctions can fail catastrophically under wind or ice load. "Included bark" occurs when bark grows inward between the two stems rather than outward — it is a sign that the junction is weak and the stems are growing apart rather than together. An arborist can assess whether cabling or bracing is appropriate or whether removal is the safer option.

**Root zone problems.** Large surface roots that have been cut (during driveway work, utility installation, landscaping), soil that has been compacted over the root zone (construction equipment, paving), or soil that has been raised or lowered around the trunk — all of these compromise the tree's structural stability and health. A tree that has lost a significant portion of its roots can fail years later, well after the damage event.

**Leaning trees.** A tree that was always straight and has recently started leaning is a serious warning sign — it may have lost root structure on one side and could fall. An arborist should evaluate it immediately.

**Mushrooms, conks, or fungal growth on the trunk or roots.** These are the fruiting bodies of wood-decay fungi that live in the heartwood of the tree. Their presence indicates internal decay — the tree may appear healthy from the outside while being hollow inside. This is a serious structural risk.

**Cracks in the trunk.** Vertical cracks are less concerning than horizontal cracks. A horizontal crack on a large tree trunk is a sign of significant structural failure risk.

**Trees in contact with or growing into the house.** Branches that touch the roof allow squirrels, raccoons, and other pests to access the roof and attic. Limbs in contact with the roof abrade the shingles. Roots that grow toward or under the foundation can displace it over time (though this is more of a risk with certain species — willows, silver maples — and less of a risk with oaks and most other species).

### Working with Certified Arborists

For any significant tree work — removal of trees near structures, pruning of large limbs, hazard assessment — hire a certified arborist. The International Society of Arboriculture (ISA) certifies arborists who have demonstrated knowledge of tree care; you can verify credentials at the ISA website.

Be wary of door-to-door tree services, especially after storms. Storm-chasing tree "services" are a well-documented scam — they often offer low prices, do poor work or unnecessary work, and disappear before problems become apparent.

Tree removal near a structure, near utility lines, or in a tight space is genuinely dangerous and specialized work. It is not a DIY project for homeowners.

**Tree trimming costs:** Routine pruning of a medium-sized tree by a certified arborist typically costs $300 to $800. Large tree removal can cost $1,500 to $5,000 or more in populated areas where the removal must be carefully managed to avoid damage to structures.

### Tree Root Issues

The root systems of trees extend far beyond the visible crown — typically 2 to 3 times the crown diameter, radiating outward from the trunk in the top 18 inches of soil. The roots compete with lawn grass for water and nutrients, which is why the area under a dense tree canopy is often difficult to grow grass.

**Roots and foundations:** Most tree species are not a significant threat to poured concrete or block foundations unless the foundation is already compromised. Tree roots follow moisture; they may find and exploit cracks in a failing foundation or drainage pipe, but they are not generally strong enough to create new cracks in sound concrete. The main risk is from water-seeking species (willow, cottonwood, silver maple) near aging clay or cast iron sewer and water lines, which they can infiltrate through joints and cracks.

**Roots and driveways/sidewalks:** Large surface roots can lift and crack concrete and asphalt pavement. Solutions include cutting the root (carefully, with awareness that large-root cutting can destabilize the tree or cause it to decline), grinding the root (same caveats), installing root barriers when paving or patching, or accepting the root and maintaining a gravel or mulch area instead of pavement near the tree.

### Mulch — The Best Thing You Can Do for Trees

Apply a 2- to 4-inch layer of wood chip mulch in a circle around each tree, extending at least to the drip line (the outer edge of the canopy) if possible, and keeping the mulch 3 to 4 inches away from the trunk itself (mulch directly against the trunk creates moisture and disease problems).

Mulch:
- Retains soil moisture (reduces watering needs by 25%)
- Moderates soil temperature extremes
- Suppresses grass and weed competition in the root zone
- Gradually improves soil structure and fertility as it breaks down
- Prevents lawn mower and string trimmer damage to the trunk (one of the most common ways trees are gradually killed — repeated mechanical damage to the root flare and trunk base)

"Volcano mulching" — piling mulch in a cone against the trunk — is an extremely common mistake that causes trunk decay, disease, and eventual death of the tree. Mulch stays flat and away from the trunk.

## Part 8: The Landscape — Beds, Shrubs, and Soil

### Foundation Plantings and Beds

The shrubs and plantings around the foundation of your house are called foundation plantings. They provide visual appeal and can also serve practical functions — they can help direct water away from the foundation if properly graded, and they provide some insulation to the foundation walls.

Common problems:
- **Overgrown shrubs covering windows or touching the siding:** This is very common in older homes where the original plantings have grown far beyond their intended size. Shrubs that touch the house provide a pathway for insects and moisture against the siding. Cut them back to at least 18 inches of clearance from the house, or remove and replace with species that will not overgrow.
- **Beds pitched toward the house:** The soil in planting beds should slope away from the foundation at a minimum of 1 inch per foot for the first 6 feet. Beds that slope toward the house direct water toward the foundation wall.
- **Invasive species:** Many landscape plants have become invasive in various parts of the country — English ivy, Japanese barberry, burning bush, Bradford pear, Japanese honeysuckle, and others. Check what you have against your state's invasive species list and consider replacement with native alternatives.

### Soil Amendment

Healthy soil is the foundation of a healthy landscape. Most landscape soils — particularly in new developments where the topsoil was stripped during construction — are compacted subsoil with poor structure and low organic matter.

Improving soil health is a long-term process accomplished primarily by:
- Adding compost (finished compost, not raw manure) worked into the soil of beds and topdressed onto the lawn
- Reducing compaction (topdressing with compost, core aeration for the lawn — discussed below)
- Mulching (as discussed for trees — applicable to all beds)
- Minimizing chemical inputs that harm soil microbiome

**Core aeration** for the lawn: A core aerator removes small plugs of soil from the turf, reducing compaction and allowing air, water, and fertilizer to penetrate the root zone more effectively. This should be done annually for high-traffic lawns, every 2 to 3 years for lower-traffic lawns. Do it in early fall for cool-season grasses, late spring for warm-season grasses. Aerators can be rented from equipment rental companies for $80 to $150 per day. Many lawn care companies offer aeration as a service.

### Hardscaping — Driveways, Walkways, and Patios

**Asphalt driveways** should be seal-coated every 3 to 5 years to prevent water infiltration and oxidation. New asphalt should cure for at least 6 months before the first seal coating. Fill cracks (larger than hairline cracks) with rubberized asphalt crack filler before seal coating.

**Concrete driveways and walkways** should be sealed every 3 to 5 years with a concrete sealer appropriate for your climate (silane-siloxane penetrating sealers for maximum protection with minimal change in appearance). Fill cracks with a flexible polyurethane or epoxy crack filler before sealing.

**Concrete pavers and brick:** Polymeric sand in the joints between pavers prevents weed growth and stabilizes the joints. Re-sand joints as needed (every few years, or after heavy rain erosion). Re-level any settled areas.

**Patios and outdoor living spaces:** Wood decks — covered in Post 7 (envelope). Concrete patios: seal as described above. Stamped concrete is prone to cracking and may need professional repair if cracks develop. Stone patios require periodic re-leveling of individual stones and repointing of mortar joints.

## Part 9: The Year in the Lawn — A Seasonal Playbook

### Spring (Cool-Season Grass)
- Remove any winter debris and thatch
- Apply pre-emergent crabgrass control (before soil temperatures reach 55°F)
- Service the lawn mower
- Begin mowing when the grass reaches the one-third rule threshold
- Apply light fertilizer application if needed (not on the fall schedule)
- Overseed bare spots

### Spring (Warm-Season Grass)
- Apply pre-emergent before the lawn greens up fully
- Begin mowing when the lawn is actively growing
- Start fertilization as soon as the lawn is fully green

### Summer (Cool-Season Grass)
- Raise mowing height to the top of the recommended range
- Water deeply and infrequently
- No fertilization (or very light application only)
- Accept some summer dormancy in drought conditions — the grass is not dead, just conserving water

### Summer (Warm-Season Grass)
- Peak growing season — mow frequently
- Apply fertilizer per schedule
- Water adequately for the species

### Fall (Cool-Season Grass)
- This is the most important season for cool-season lawns
- Core aerate if on schedule
- Overseed bare or thin areas (the single most important lawn improvement activity)
- Apply primary fall fertilizer
- Continue mowing until the grass stops growing (often into November in many climates)
- Final mowing at the end of the season slightly lower than normal (reduces matting under snow)

### Fall (Warm-Season Grass)
- Stop fertilizing 6 to 8 weeks before first frost
- Overseed with perennial ryegrass if you want a green winter lawn (overseeding warm-season grass with ryegrass for winter color is common in the South)
- Expect dormancy as temperatures drop

### Winter (All Grass Types)
- Clean and store equipment
- Plan next year's improvements
- Avoid heavy foot traffic on dormant or frozen turf (dormant warm-season grass and frozen cool-season turf are both susceptible to damage from traffic)

## Resources

- **University Cooperative Extension Services:** Your state's land-grant university operates a cooperative extension service with free, regionally specific lawn and garden recommendations. Search "your state + cooperative extension + lawn care" for publications tailored to your climate and grass types.
- **National Gardening Association**: https://garden.org/
- **International Society of Arboriculture — Find a Certified Arborist**: https://www.treesaregood.org/findanarborist
- **EPA WaterSense — Outdoor Water Use**: https://www.epa.gov/watersense/outdoor-water-use
- **USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map**: https://planthardiness.ars.usda.gov/
