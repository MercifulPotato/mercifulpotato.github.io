# Part 10 of 10 — When Foundations Fail: Repair, Renovation, and Restoration of Distressed Foundation Systems

We began this series with the basic physics of how foundations work — load paths, soil mechanics, the vocabulary that engineers use to describe the relationship between a building and the ground beneath it. We traveled from single-family slabs in coastal Virginia to post-tensioned plates on Texas expansive clay, from helical piers in crawl spaces to drilled shafts under Kathmandu apartments. We covered every major foundation type, every major failure mode, and the environmental systems — frost protection, drainage, waterproofing, vapor control — that determine whether a foundation performs well or slowly fails.

We end where most homeowners actually begin: with a problem. A crack. A door that sticks. A basement that leaks. A floor that slopes in a direction nobody intended. The foundation is already there, already installed, already showing signs of distress. What do you do?

This final article is a complete reference for foundation repair — diagnosis, method selection, execution, quality control, and the critically important question that does not get asked often enough: should this foundation be repaired at all, or does the building need a different future?

## Part 1: Diagnosing Foundation Problems — Finding the Actual Cause

The single most important and most frequently skipped step in foundation repair is identifying the actual cause of the distress. Without understanding why the foundation has moved, cracked, or failed, repair is guesswork. And guesswork-based repair often fails within a few years, leaving the homeowner to repeat the expense.

### Categories of Foundation Distress

**1. Differential Settlement**

The foundation has moved downward — not uniformly, but in some areas more than others. This differential movement distorts the structure above: doors rack, windows crack, floors slope, brick veneer develops stair-step cracks.

Causes: Poorly compacted fill under the foundation; erosion of bearing soil from plumbing leaks or subsurface drainage; consolidation of compressible organic or clay layers that were not adequately characterized in the original design; loosening of soil from large tree root extraction; sinkhole formation in karst (limestone) terrain; changes in groundwater table that cause consolidation of normally consolidated clay.

Diagnostic indicators: Cracks that are wider at the top than the bottom (in settling areas) or wider at the bottom (in heaving areas); floor slope toward the settled area; differential elevation survey showing depression in the settled zone.

**2. Expansive Soil Movement**

In expansive clay environments, the foundation moves both up and down with seasonal moisture changes — this is not permanent settlement but cyclical movement. The distress pattern is different: cracks that open and partially close seasonally, floors that vary in levelness throughout the year, doors that stick in spring and swing freely in fall.

We covered this extensively in Articles 3 and 9. Repair for expansive soil movement must address both the structural consequence (the current elevation) and the ongoing cause (moisture variation) or the repair will be undone within a year or two.

**3. Foundation Wall Failure (Basements and Crawl Spaces)**

Foundation walls (basement walls, crawl space walls) can fail in several ways:
- **Horizontal cracking or bowing**: The wall is deflecting inward under lateral soil pressure, particularly when the backfill was placed too quickly, the soil became saturated, or the wall lacks adequate horizontal reinforcement. This is the most serious and most common structural failure mode in basement walls.
- **Stair-step cracking in CMU**: Indicates differential settlement or lateral soil movement; mortar joint failures in the weakest pattern through the block coursing.
- **Vertical cracking in poured concrete**: Typically from shrinkage during curing (especially if the wall was poured in stages); usually not structurally serious unless the crack is wide, actively leaking, or showing differential displacement.
- **Settlement of the footing**: The wall is dropping, usually at one end, typically from bearing failure under the footing.

**4. Slab Cracking**

Slabs crack for many reasons, some serious and some cosmetic:
- **Shrinkage cracking**: Fine, random, surface cracks from concrete drying; cosmetic only
- **Control joint cracking**: Cracks at intentional weakening planes; expected and acceptable
- **Settlement cracking**: Cracks over areas of void formation or differential settlement; potentially serious
- **Heave cracking**: Diagonal cracks from corners indicating differential uplift; potentially serious
- **Post-tensioned tendon failure**: If a tendon breaks (from corrosion, damage, or stressing error), the slab loses its prestress in that zone; cracks may develop; this requires specific investigation

**5. Moisture Intrusion**

Not a structural failure but a serious performance failure:
- **Hydrostatic seepage**: Water entering through cracks or joints under pressure; requires waterproofing repair
- **Condensation**: Interior moisture condensing on cool basement walls; requires dehumidification, not waterproofing
- **Surface drainage infiltration**: Water pooling near the foundation and entering through inadequate sealing; requires drainage correction

### Diagnostic Methods

**Differential elevation survey**: The essential measurement for any foundation distress evaluation. A Zip-Level, precision level, or laser level measures the elevation of all floor and slab surfaces relative to a benchmark. The resulting topographic map of the floor plane reveals where movement has occurred, how much, and in what pattern.

**Crack mapping**: Systematically photograph, measure, and describe every visible crack — its location, width, length, orientation, and whether it shows differential displacement (one side higher than the other). Plot cracks on a floor plan sketch. Patterns in the crack distribution often reveal the failure mechanism.

**Soil borings and laboratory testing**: For any serious repair where the cause is uncertain, a geotechnical investigation is warranted. A few test pits or borings to 10 to 15 feet, with laboratory tests on soil samples, can answer critical questions: Was there organic material under the foundation that has decomposed? Is the bearing stratum adequate? Is there groundwater that was not anticipated? Is the soil expansive?

**Plumbing leak detection**: Electronic or acoustic leak detection equipment can locate active plumbing leaks under slabs. Any foundation repair program should include leak detection as a first step if unexplained center heave or localized settlement is present.

**Crack gauges and benchmarks**: For active cracks (currently moving), installing crack gauges (telltale strips across cracks) and benchmarks (permanent measurement points) allows monitoring over time. Is the crack stable or growing? Is the settlement ongoing or has it stopped? This information is critical for deciding whether to repair now or wait.

**Structural engineer's evaluation**: For any distress that is ambiguous, serious, or involves a below-grade wall, a structural engineer's evaluation is the appropriate next step. The engineer can perform all of the above, interpret the results, and provide a written opinion of the cause, the current structural adequacy, and recommended remediation. Cost: $500 to $3,000 for a residential evaluation.

## Part 2: Underpinning — Stabilizing and Lifting Settled Foundations

Underpinning is the generic term for the process of extending the foundation to greater depth — either to reach more competent bearing or to anchor the foundation against movement. It is the primary method for addressing differential settlement.

### Method 1: Helical Pier Underpinning

Described fully in Article 4 and Article 9. The dominant underpinning method for residential construction in the United States, for good reasons:

- Immediately load-bearing (no curing time)
- Compact installation equipment (accessible in narrow spaces; can be done inside a crawl space or through a basement floor)
- Torque-to-capacity correlation provides immediate quality verification
- Can be used for both stabilization (stopping further movement) and lifting (raising the structure back toward level)
- Transferable warranty typically available

**Installation sequence for residential helical pier underpinning**:
1. Excavate at each pier location (typically 2 to 4 feet wide × 2 to 4 feet deep) to expose the existing footing
2. Install helical pier by rotating with a hydraulic torque head until the required depth and torque are achieved
3. Install a push bracket that clamps around or bolts under the existing footing; the bracket connects the pier shaft to the footing
4. Install a hydraulic jack between the pier and bracket
5. Apply lift pressure: the pier pushes down against the bearing soil at depth; the reaction raises the footing upward
6. Monitor elevation with a laser level; stop lift when target elevation is reached (or when the structure is approaching design level)
7. Lock the bracket at the loaded elevation; remove the jack
8. Backfill and compact

**Determining the lift target**: The engineer or experienced contractor sets the target elevation based on:
- The differential elevation survey (how much is this point below the surrounding slab?)
- The age and current condition of the structure (lifting aggressively on a 60-year-old house with brittle plaster and older windows may cause additional cracking — conservative lifts are sometimes preferred)
- The expected response of the structure to lifting (some structures lift cleanly and uniformly; others are too stiff and require careful sequential lifting at multiple points simultaneously)

**Over-lifting and back-sag**: A common error is lifting any given pier location to its maximum while adjacent unsupported areas remain low, creating a "hump" at the lifted location. Proper underpinning lifts all piers simultaneously in controlled increments (or in a carefully planned sequence), bringing the entire settlement zone up together.

### Method 2: Push Pier (Steel Pier) Underpinning

Similar concept to helical piers, but the pier is hydraulically pushed into the ground rather than rotated. Sections of 2 to 3-inch-diameter steel pipe (typically 3 feet long) are driven in sequence by a hydraulic ram that reacts against the structure's weight. Sections are coupled together until the pier reaches bedrock or a predetermined hydraulic pressure (indicating dense material). A bracket is installed and lifting proceeds as with helical piers.

**Advantages of push piers**:
- Very deep penetration possible (30 to 60+ feet) for reaching deep bearing
- Does not require the torque equipment of helical piers
- Less sensitive to hard cobbles and debris that can stop helical augers

**Disadvantages**:
- Requires significant building weight to react against — push piers installed at light portions of the building (garage slabs, stoops, light additions) may not be able to drive deep enough
- Capacity verification is less reliable than torque correlation — pressure at the hydraulic ram is a proxy for resistance, not a direct capacity measure
- Must reach bearing at depth — cannot develop capacity from skin friction alone as helicals can

### Method 3: Concrete Underpinning (Mass Concrete Underpinning / Pit Method)

The oldest form of underpinning, rarely used for residential work today but still appropriate in specific circumstances:

**Sequential pit underpinning**: Excavation is made under a section of the existing footing (no more than one-third of any wall span is excavated at a time, to prevent progressive collapse); a new, deeper concrete footing is poured beneath the old one; after it cures, the adjacent section is excavated and underpinned; this continues until the entire wall is underpinned.

Used for masonry buildings where other methods are impractical, for historic structures where drilling equipment cannot access, and in urban areas where vibration from driven piers is prohibited.

Time-consuming, labor-intensive, and requires skill to execute safely. Each section must be properly shored before excavation.

**Mini-pile underpinning**: Small-diameter (6 to 12 inch) drilled and grouted mini-piles (micropiles) installed at an angle through or adjacent to the existing footing. Because they are small and their equipment is small, they can be installed in very confined spaces — inside a building, in a basement, through an existing slab. Standard method for historic building underpinning in urban environments.

## Part 3: Mudjacking and Polyurethane Foam Lifting

For slabs that have settled due to void formation beneath them (soil washout, organic decomposition, subsurface drainage erosion), filling the void and lifting the slab back to elevation is the appropriate repair.

### Mudjacking (Slabjacking)

**Process**: Holes are drilled through the concrete slab at regular intervals (typically 1.5 to 2-inch diameter holes at 2 to 4-foot spacing). A slurry — typically Portland cement mixed with soil, water, and sometimes fly ash — is pumped under pressure through the holes. The slurry fills voids and lifts the slab. When the target elevation is achieved, the pump pressure is released; the holes are patched with grout.

**Appropriate uses**:
- Settled driveway sections that have sunk at control joints
- Settled sidewalk sections
- Garage slabs that have dropped from void formation
- Steps and stoops that have settled

**Not appropriate for**:
- Expansive clay movement (the slurry does not address the root cause)
- Differential structural settlement from bearing failure (the slurry cannot carry the structural loads)
- Slabs with significant structural cracking (the slab must be intact to be lifted as a unit)

**Cost**: $500 to $3,000 depending on area and number of holes.

**Longevity**: Mudjacking fills voids with a cementitious material that, once cured, is essentially permanent. It does not shrink or deteriorate. A properly executed mudjacking repair is durable.

### Polyurethane Foam Lifting (PolyLift, Urethane Foam Injection)

Similar principle to mudjacking but uses a two-component expanding polyurethane foam instead of cementitious slurry.

**Process**: Much smaller holes (5/8-inch diameter) are drilled through the slab at wider spacing (2 to 3 feet). A two-part polyurethane is injected; it expands 20 to 25× its liquid volume within seconds of injection, filling voids and exerting lift pressure. The rapid expansion allows extremely precise lift control — the operator can stop the lift within 1/8-inch of target. Holes are patched with matching color-tinted grout.

**Advantages over mudjacking**:
- Much smaller holes (less visible, faster repair)
- Faster: the foam sets in minutes, not days; the area is immediately usable
- Lighter: foam weighs 2 to 4 pcf; mudjack slurry weighs 90 to 120 pcf; less re-loading of the soil
- Better in wet conditions: foam is waterproof; cementitious slurry can wash away if groundwater is present

**Disadvantages**:
- More expensive: typically 2 to 3× the cost of mudjacking
- Not appropriate for very large voids or very heavy lifts (foam volume limits)
- Some concerns about long-term foam durability in certain chemical environments

**Cost**: $800 to $5,000 depending on area and number of holes.

**Appropriate uses**: Same as mudjacking, plus situations where rapid return to service is needed, where very precise lift control is required, or where soil is wet and slurry would be problematic.

## Part 4: Basement Wall Repair

Basement wall failures are the most serious structural problem in below-grade construction. Unlike slab settlements (which are generally slow and progressive), a bowing or cracking basement wall can fail rapidly once the deflection exceeds a threshold.

### Stage Classification for Bowing Walls

The Structural Engineering Institute has published guidance on classifying bowing basement wall severity:

**Stage 1 (Minor)**: Deflection less than 1 inch; no horizontal cracking or cracking only at mortar joints; no visible step or displacement across cracks. Monitoring recommended; repair not immediately urgent.

**Stage 2 (Moderate)**: Deflection 1 to 2 inches; horizontal cracking in the wall; step or offset at cracks possible. Repair warranted; bracing may be needed as temporary measure.

**Stage 3 (Severe)**: Deflection greater than 2 inches; significant cracking; risk of progressive failure. Immediate repair required; temporary shoring may be needed to prevent collapse.

**Stage 4 (Critical)**: Imminent failure; professional emergency response required.

### Method 1: Carbon Fiber Reinforcement Straps

**How they work**: Wide carbon fiber straps (typically 4 to 6 inches wide, 0.1 to 0.12 inches thick) are bonded to the interior face of the wall using high-strength epoxy. The carbon fiber has very high tensile strength (over 100,000 psi) and very high stiffness (modulus of elasticity ~33,000,000 psi — stiffer than steel). The straps provide a tensile reinforcement that resists further bowing.

**Critical limitation**: Carbon fiber straps stabilize the wall — they prevent further movement. They do NOT straighten the wall or restore it to its original position. If the wall is already bowed 2 inches, it will remain bowed 2 inches after carbon fiber installation (unless the straps are pre-tensioned, which some systems do with a mechanical anchor).

**Installation process**:
1. Clean and prepare the concrete wall surface (remove efflorescence, loose material, oil)
2. Apply epoxy primer; allow to tack
3. Apply epoxy adhesive to the strap location on the wall
4. Press the carbon fiber strap into the adhesive; apply pressure to consolidate
5. Apply topcoat epoxy over the strap
6. Install mechanical anchors at the top (to the floor framing or bond beam) and bottom (to the footing) to transfer the strap force to a fixed boundary

**Appropriate for**: Stage 1 and Stage 2 walls where the deflection is small, the cause of bowing is passive soil pressure (not hydrostatic), and the owner accepts stabilization without correction.

**Cost**: $300 to $600 per strap; typical repair uses 4 to 8 straps = $1,500 to $5,000.

### Method 2: Wall Anchors (Earth Anchors / Plate Anchors)

**How they work**: A round steel plate (the wall plate) is mounted on the interior face of the basement wall and connected by a steel rod through the wall to a buried anchor plate in the soil outside the wall. The anchor plate is installed in undisturbed soil at a distance from the wall (typically 8 to 12 feet). Tightening a nut on the interior wall plate draws the wall plate against the wall and the anchor plate against the soil, applying inward-to-outward compression that counteracts the inward soil pressure.

**Advantage over carbon fiber**: Wall anchors can be periodically tightened over time — as the soil changes seasonally, the anchor nuts can be turned to gradually straighten the wall back toward vertical. This is something carbon fiber cannot do.

**Disadvantage**: Requires access to the exterior soil at the appropriate distance from the wall — not feasible in tight urban lots, against garage slabs, or under patios.

**Appropriate for**: Walls with access to soil at 8+ feet from the wall; situations where the owner wants the possibility of straightening the wall over time.

**Cost**: $400 to $700 per anchor; typical repair uses 4 to 10 anchors = $2,000 to $7,000.

### Method 3: Internal Bracing (Steel I-Beam Wall Supports)

**How they work**: Steel channels or I-beams are installed vertically inside the basement, spanning from a bearing plate at the footing to a bearing plate at the floor framing above. The steel beams are set at an angle — slightly out of plumb toward the wall — and driven tight with a hydraulic ram. This pushes the bowed wall back toward plumb. Over time (weeks to months), the beam can be periodically re-tightened to continue moving the wall gradually back toward vertical.

**Advantage**: Physically moves the wall, not just stabilizes it. Can achieve significant correction of existing deflection over months of incremental tightening.

**Disadvantage**: The steel beams protrude into the basement interior — they cannot be drywalled over (they must remain accessible for re-tightening). They take up approximately 4 to 6 inches of floor space along the wall. In a finished basement, they require either living with exposed steel beams or redesigning the interior layout.

**Appropriate for**: Stage 2 and Stage 3 walls where wall correction (not just stabilization) is desired; for finished basements where steel beams can be incorporated into the design.

**Cost**: $500 to $800 per beam; typical repair uses 4 to 8 beams = $2,500 to $6,000.

### Method 4: Wall Replacement

When a basement wall is Stage 3 or Stage 4, or when it is a CMU wall with severe cracking and mortar joint failure, replacement may be the only appropriate solution.

**Process**:
1. Fully shore the first floor framing from below (the wall to be replaced is providing support — this cannot be forgotten)
2. Excavate exterior soil from the wall
3. Demolish the existing wall section by section
4. Pour new reinforced concrete wall (or install new CMU wall with full grouting and reinforcement)
5. Apply waterproofing to the new exterior wall surface
6. Install drainage composite and perimeter drain
7. Backfill
8. Remove shoring

This is a major project. For a 40-foot wall replacement: $25,000 to $60,000 including excavation, shoring, new wall, waterproofing, and backfill.

**When wall replacement is necessary**: When deflection is greater than 2 inches, when the wall has fractured (not just cracked) along horizontal lines, when there is significant differential displacement across cracks, or when the wall material has deteriorated (spalling, freeze-thaw damage, sulfate attack) to the point of insufficient structural section.

### Waterproofing Repair for Leaking Basement Walls

If the wall is structurally sound but leaks, the repair approach depends on whether exterior access is available:

**Exterior waterproofing**: Excavate exterior wall; clean surface; apply new waterproofing membrane; install drainage composite and new perimeter drain; backfill. The most effective and most expensive: $10,000 to $30,000 for a full perimeter.

**Crystalline injection**: Drill ports into the wall at the crack locations; inject crystalline waterproofing compound (Xypex, Penetron) under pressure into the cracks and through to the exterior wall face. The crystals grow outward through the wall and seal the pathway. Works well for isolated cracks and for concrete walls; less effective for CMU.

**Polyurethane crack injection**: For actively leaking cracks, two-component hydrophilic polyurethane grout is injected into the crack under pressure; the foam reacts with water and expands to fill and seal the crack. Very effective for active water intrusion at specific cracks. Not a global system — treats individual cracks only.

**Interior drainage system**: As described in Article 5, an interior perimeter drainage channel and sump collects water that enters the wall. Does not stop water entry; manages it. Appropriate when exterior access is impossible and when the leakage rate is manageable.

## Part 5: When Not to Repair — Evaluating Demolition or Full Replacement

Not every distressed foundation should be repaired. Sometimes the correct answer is demolition or building replacement.

### When Repair Is Not Cost-Effective

**The 50% rule**: A rough guideline from the field of historic preservation and real estate: if the cost of repairs exceeds 50% of the post-repair value of the structure, demolition and replacement is usually the better economic choice. This is a rough guideline, not a hard rule, and it must be applied with judgment about non-economic factors (historic value, community connection, the owner's personal attachment).

**Structural irreparability**: Some foundation failures produce irreparable consequences in the structure above:
- A house that has settled 6 to 8 inches differentially has racking throughout the framing, distorted sheathing, broken connections, and misaligned windows and doors. Even if you could lift it back with underpinning (expensive), the structure above has been so distorted that it cannot function properly without full re-framing.
- A basement wall that has failed catastrophically (collapsed or buckled to failure) may have introduced bending, cracking, and connection failures in the floor above that require complete structural replacement.

**Contamination and biological damage**: A structure that has experienced long-term moisture infiltration — years of basement flooding, long-term crawl space moisture — may have extensive wood rot, mold, and insect damage in the framing, subfloor, and wall structure. If the structural wood is extensively deteriorated, repair of the foundation alone leaves a deteriorated building on a good foundation. Full renovation — or demolition and replacement — may make more sense.

**Foundation type incompatibility**: If the original foundation type is fundamentally inappropriate for the site (a simple spread footing on organic soil; an unencapsulated crawl space on a site with perennial high water table), repair of the existing system may be addressing symptoms rather than the root cause. The right answer may be a completely different foundation type — which usually means demolition.

### The Role of a Structural Engineer in This Decision

The decision between repair and replacement should always be made with input from a licensed structural engineer who has physically inspected the structure, not just on the basis of a contractor's opinion (contractors repair things; they may not be the most objective evaluators of whether something should be replaced) or on a desk review without site inspection.

The engineer can:
- Quantify the structural damage and its consequences
- Estimate the cost range for various repair approaches
- Advise on the expected performance of the repaired structure
- Identify any conditions that make repair technically inadvisable
- Provide the written engineering evaluation that lenders, insurers, and future buyers will require

The engineer's fee for this evaluation ($500 to $3,000 for a residential structure) is the best money spent before committing to either a major repair project or a demolition decision.

## Part 6: Evaluating Foundation Repair Contractors

The foundation repair industry in the United States is partially regulated and partially not. In most states, foundation repair contractors require a contractor's license but not a structural engineering license. The engineering design of the repair (selection of method, depth, capacity requirements) may or may not involve a licensed engineer. The quality of contractors varies enormously.

### Red Flags to Avoid

**High-pressure sales tactics**: Any contractor who demands a decision "today" or offers a "today-only" discount is using a sales approach that compensates for an inability to win business on merits. Walk away.

**Guarantees of permanence without qualification**: No underpinning system is "permanent" in expansive clay without ongoing maintenance. Any contractor who implies the repair will "never need to be done again, guaranteed" is misrepresenting the reality of expansive soil behavior.

**No written engineering documentation**: For any repair involving underpinning (helical piers, push piers), a reputable contractor should provide either their own engineered design or recommend an independent structural engineer's review. "I've been doing this for 20 years" is experience, not engineering documentation.

**Unusually low bids**: Foundation repair is not a product where you can reliably save 30% by using the lowest bidder. Pier capacity, installation depth, bracket quality, and crew skill all determine performance. A bid that is 30% below two comparable bids usually means shorter piers, fewer piers, or lower-quality materials.

**Reluctance to provide references and warranty details in writing**: Any reputable contractor will provide a list of similar projects and their contact information, and will provide the warranty in writing before you commit.

### What to Ask Contractors

Before hiring any foundation repair contractor, ask:
1. Is the repair design by a licensed structural or geotechnical engineer? Can you provide the engineer's name, license number, and their written design?
2. What specific pier product are you using? What is the published capacity per pier? What installation torque/pressure will you achieve?
3. How many piers are you installing, and why that number? (They should be able to explain the load demand and the pier capacity, not just say "that's what we do for this size house.")
4. What is the warranty term, what does it cover, and is it transferable to future owners?
5. Will you provide before-and-after elevation surveys (not just before and after photos)?
6. Who will be on site during installation, and what are their credentials?
7. Can you provide references from similar projects (similar soil conditions, similar distress type) in the last 2 years?

### The Role of an Independent Engineer

For any repair exceeding $10,000, consider engaging an independent structural or geotechnical engineer:
- To review the proposed repair scope and specification before you commit
- To observe installation at key steps (pier installation, lift operation)
- To certify the completed work

The engineer is not selling you a repair — they are advising you on whether the proposed repair is correctly designed and executed. Their fee ($500 to $2,000 for a repair oversight engagement) buys you protection against undertreated problems and oversold solutions.

## Part 7: After Repair — Maintenance and Monitoring

Foundation repair does not end at the contractor's truck pulling away. The repaired foundation is going into the same environment that distressed it in the first place. Without changes to that environment — drainage, moisture management, tree management — the forces that caused the original failure will continue to operate.

### Re-establishing Benchmarks

Within one week of repair completion, establish permanent measurement benchmarks at strategic locations in the house:
- One benchmark in each major room (dining room, living room, master bedroom, kitchen)
- One at each repaired footing location
- One in the middle of the house (away from the repaired perimeter) as a stable reference

Note the elevation of each benchmark relative to the others. Measure again at 6 months and at 12 months. If the repaired areas remain stable relative to the benchmarks, the repair is performing well.

### Drainage Improvements as Part of Any Repair Program

Every foundation repair project should include a drainage assessment:
- Are gutters and downspouts functioning and directing water away?
- Is the grade sloping away from the building on all sides?
- Are there any chronic wet spots adjacent to the foundation?
- Is there any evidence of surface water pooling near the perimeter?

Drainage improvements are cheap. Neglecting them can undo a $15,000 repair in two to three seasonal cycles.

### For Expansive Clay Sites

As described in Article 9, foundation maintenance on expansive clay is an ongoing program:
- Consistent perimeter watering during dry periods
- Monitoring soil gaps adjacent to the slab
- Annual check of all drainage
- Tree management — particularly avoiding sudden removal of large established trees near the foundation

### For Crawl Space Foundations

After any crawl space foundation repair, inspect the crawl space conditions:
- Is the vapor retarder intact and fully covering the ground?
- Is the encapsulation system functioning (low humidity, no condensation on framing)?
- Any new evidence of wood decay or insect damage?
- Any movement in interior piers or girders?

Annual crawl space inspection — even a brief one — catches problems while they are small.

### For Repaired Basement Walls

After carbon fiber, wall anchor, or steel beam repairs:
- Monitor crack gauges on any repaired cracks: seasonal movement of 1/16 inch or less is typical; larger movements warrant engineer consultation
- For wall anchor systems: tighten nuts as directed by the contractor (typically annually during the fall, when the soil is at its driest and the wall is at its most deflected)
- For steel beam systems: re-tighten as directed (typically twice per year) to continue gradual wall correction

## Conclusion: The Foundation Always Wins

Foundations are passive. They do not move unless something makes them move. They do not crack unless forces on them exceed their capacity. They do not leak unless water finds a path. Every foundation problem has a cause — a specific, identifiable, addressable cause. Find the cause, address the cause, and the foundation problem goes away. Treat the symptom while ignoring the cause, and the symptom returns.

This is the underlying message of this entire series, expressed ten different ways across ten different articles:

The soil is the foundation. Everything above the soil is just structure. The structural choices must match the soil — its capacity, its chemistry, its seasonal behavior, its moisture content. The thermal and moisture protection systems must match the climate. The inspection and maintenance must match the structure and its environment.

The twelve-thousand-year record of human foundation building — from Jericho's stone-on-earth footings to Tokyo's deep drilled shafts — is a record of the gradual, occasionally painful discovery of these matches. Every foundation failure is a mismatch. Every well-performing foundation, in service for decades without incident, is a match achieved.

Your job, as a builder, buyer, owner, or engineer, is to understand what kind of match you are working with — and either build it correctly from the start or repair it toward the match it should have been.
