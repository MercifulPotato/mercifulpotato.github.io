In the first part of this series we met the budget as an idea: a plan, a law, and a political statement, made once a year, totalling for this coming year about NPR 2,124.34 billion, or roughly USD 14.16 billion. We deliberately set one thing aside, because it confuses almost everyone and deserves a post of its own. That thing is *time*.

You cannot understand a budget without understanding the clock and the calendar it runs on. A budget is a plan for a particular stretch of time — and in Nepal, that stretch of time does not begin in January, is not measured in the years most of the world uses, and is governed by a date written into the constitution itself. Worse, the budget is full of three words that look almost the same and mean dangerously different things: *estimate*, *revised estimate*, and *actual*. By the end of this post you will know exactly when Nepal's budget year runs, why, and how to read the calendar it is written in — and you will have learned a piece of accounting vocabulary that quietly explains a great deal of what goes wrong with Nepali public money.

## Two calendars, one country

Begin with a fact that surprises nearly every visitor: Nepal does not officially run on the Gregorian calendar — the January-to-December system used across most of the world. Nepal's official calendar is the **Bikram Sambat**, usually abbreviated BS. It is the calendar on government documents, on the budget, on newspapers, on school records, and in everyday Nepali life.

The Bikram Sambat is roughly 56 to 57 years *ahead* of the Gregorian calendar. That is why the budget we are studying is for the year **2083/84 BS**, even though, in the Gregorian years the rest of the world uses, it covers **2026 and 2027 AD**. When a Nepali says "the budget for 2083/84," and an international report says "Nepal's budget for 2026/27," they are talking about exactly the same document. Throughout this series we will give both, because half our readers think in one and half in the other, and translating between them is part of reading Nepal honestly.

The gap of about 56 to 57 years is not a fixed, clean number you can simply add, and here is why: the two calendars do not start their years on the same day, and their months are not aligned. The Gregorian year begins on the first of January. The Bikram Sambat year begins in the middle of April, around the 13th or 14th, with a month called Baisakh. So for part of any Gregorian year you add 57 to get the BS year, and for another part you add 56, because one calendar has already rolled over into its new year while the other has not. This is exactly the kind of small, real complication that a careful person notes and a careless one waves away — and waving it away is how dates get quietly mangled in translation.

The Bikram Sambat has twelve months, with their own names — Baisakh, Jestha, Ashadh, Shrawan, Bhadra, Ashwin, Kartik, Mangsir, Poush, Magh, Falgun, and Chaitra — and their own lengths, which do not match the Gregorian months and can even vary slightly from year to year because the calendar is tied to astronomical calculations rather than a fixed rule. You do not need to memorise these. You need only to absorb the headline fact and three particular months that this series will use constantly: **Baisakh**, which opens the BS year in mid-April; **Jestha**, the month in which the budget is presented, falling in late May and early June; and **Shrawan**, in which the fiscal year begins, in the second half of July.

## What is a "fiscal year," and why isn't it the calendar year?

Now to the more important idea, the one that does real work in understanding the budget: the *fiscal year*.

A fiscal year is simply the twelve-month period a government uses for its budgeting and accounting. It is the "year" the budget plans for and reports on. And here is the thing that trips people up: a fiscal year does not have to begin on New Year's Day. A government is free to choose when its accounting year starts, and different countries choose differently. The United States runs its federal fiscal year from October to September. The United Kingdom and India run theirs from April to March. Nepal runs its fiscal year from the middle of July to the middle of July.

Why would a country deliberately use an accounting year that does not match the calendar everyone lives by? There are practical reasons, and they differ from country to country, but the common thread is that the start of the fiscal year is chosen to fit the rhythm of the nation's real economic and natural life. In Nepal's case, the fiscal year begins in **Shrawan**, which falls in the second half of July, and ends at the close of **Ashadh** the following year, in the second half of the next July. In Gregorian terms, **fiscal year 2083/84 runs from around 16 July 2026 to around 16 July 2027.**

That timing is not an accident, and it has consequences we will return to in a moment. For now, fix the shape of it firmly: the budget presented in late May 2026 is a plan for a year that does not even *start* until the middle of July 2026, and that does not *end* until the middle of July 2027. The plan precedes the year. That sounds obvious — of course you plan before you act — but the size of the gap, nearly seven weeks between presentation and the year's start, matters, and the fact that the year then runs straight through Nepal's monsoon matters even more.

## Why the budget arrives on a fixed day: the constitution

Most countries present their budgets at roughly predictable times, but Nepal goes further than custom: the timing is written into its highest law. The **Constitution of Nepal**, adopted in 2015, requires the government to present the federal budget to Parliament on a specific date each year — **Jestha 15**, which in the Gregorian calendar falls on or about **29 May**. For the budget we are studying, that date was **29 May 2026** (Jestha 15, 2083).

Stop and appreciate how unusual and how deliberate this is. In many countries the date a budget is presented is a matter of convention, or of the government's convenience, and governments have been known to delay budgets for political advantage or simple disorganisation. Nepal removed that discretion. By fixing the date in the constitution, the framers tried to guarantee two things. First, *predictability*: businesses, lower levels of government, donors, and ordinary citizens all know exactly when the budget will appear, every year, and can plan around it. Second, and more importantly, *time to prepare*: by forcing the budget out on Jestha 15, weeks before the fiscal year begins in mid-July, the constitution intended to give the entire machinery of the state — and the contractors, suppliers, and local governments who depend on it — a running start, so that money could actually begin flowing on the very first day of the fiscal year rather than months into it.

That was the intention. Like much in this series, the intention and the reality are not the same, and it is worth being clear-eyed about both. The fixed date is a genuine piece of good institutional design, and Nepal deserves credit for it; many countries would benefit from the same discipline. But — and we will hammer this point throughout the series — *presenting* a budget on time tells you nothing about *spending* it on time. A government can table a flawless plan on Jestha 15 and still fail to break ground on a single road until the monsoon ends in autumn. A fixed presentation date solves the problem of when the plan appears. It does nothing, by itself, to solve the much harder problem of when the plan turns into a paved road, a staffed hospital, or a working bridge. Beware anyone who points to the on-time budget as if it were the achievement. The budget is the easy part. Doing it is the hard part.

It is also worth noting, for honesty's sake, that the constitutional schedule has not always been honoured smoothly in practice. Nepal has lived through stretches of acute political instability — governments rising and falling, parliaments dissolved, disputes over who held legitimate authority — during which the orderly budget calendar came under real strain. This particular budget, as we will see in the next post, arrives in the wake of exactly such an upheaval, presented by a government that took power only weeks earlier through an extraordinary election. That the budget appeared on the constitutional date at all, so soon after a change of government, is itself part of the story.

## The prologue: the Economic Survey

A day before the budget speech, on or about Jestha 14 — for this year, **28 May 2026** — the Finance Minister presents a different document to Parliament, one that gets far less attention than the budget but is arguably just as important. It is called the **Economic Survey**.

If the budget is the government's *plan* for the year ahead, the Economic Survey is the government's *account* of the year just past — a thick, official report on the actual state of the economy: how fast it grew, what happened to prices, how trade and remittances and revenue behaved, what the various sectors did. The Economic Survey for the year ending in mid-2026 is the factual ground on which the 2083/84 budget stands. And it contained a number that ought to frame everything that follows.

The government's own statistical authority estimated that Nepal's economy grew by about **3.85 percent** in the fiscal year just ending. Keep that figure. Because the very next day, the budget set a growth *target* for the coming year of **7 percent** — nearly double the rate the economy had just managed. We are not yet judging whether that target is achievable; we will examine it carefully much later in the series. But notice the structure: the Economic Survey says, in effect, "here is the modest reality," and the budget replies, in effect, "here is the ambitious dream." Reading the two documents side by side is one of the most useful things a citizen can do, because the gap between them — between what the economy actually did and what the budget assumes it will do — is precisely where optimism, and sometimes deception, lives. A budget's spending and borrowing plans are built on assumptions about growth and revenue, and if those assumptions are far rosier than recent reality, the whole plan is built on sand. The Economic Survey is the reality check that the budget's headlines would rather you skip.

## Three words that look alike and aren't: estimate, revised estimate, actual

We now arrive at a piece of vocabulary that is dull, technical, and one of the most quietly important things in this entire series. Read this section slowly, because once you understand these three words you will catch a kind of deception that fools most people most of the time.

When a budget presents figures, it almost always presents them in three forms for any given item, and they are not interchangeable.

The first is the **estimate**, sometimes called the budget estimate or the original estimate. This is the number set *at the start* of a fiscal year — the plan, the intention, the amount the government said, twelve months ago, that it would collect or spend. It is a forecast made before the year began.

The second is the **revised estimate**. Partway through the fiscal year — typically around the halfway mark — governments take stock and adjust their original numbers in light of how things are actually going. If revenue is coming in below expectations, or if a ministry is plainly not going to manage to spend what it was given, the government revises its estimate to something more realistic. The revised estimate is the *updated forecast*, made partway through the year, after reality has begun to assert itself. It is almost always closer to the truth than the original estimate, and — crucially — it is very often *lower*, because the most common way reality asserts itself in Nepal is that money budgeted for construction does not get spent.

The third is the **actual**, sometimes called the actual expenditure or the final figure. This is what *truly happened*: the money that was genuinely collected or genuinely spent by the end of the year, confirmed after the fact, eventually checked by auditors. The actual is not a forecast at all. It is history.

Here is why this matters so much. When you read that this year's budget is "25.2 percent larger than last year," your immediate question — the question we trained you to ask in Part 1 — must be: *larger than last year's which figure?* Larger than last year's original estimate, the ambitious plan? Or larger than last year's revised estimate, the deflated mid-year reality? Because those two are different numbers, and the percentage you get depends entirely on which one you compare against.

In this case, as we noted in Part 1, the 25.2 percent figure is measured against the *revised* estimate of the previous year. The previous year's *original* budget was about NPR 1.964 trillion (roughly USD 13.1 billion). Its revised estimate was lower — somewhere in the region of NPR 1.70 trillion (roughly USD 11.3 billion), a figure to confirm against the official documents — because the previous government, partway through the year, admitted it would not collect or spend what it had originally promised. Measured against that shrunken revised figure, the new NPR 2,124.34 billion budget looks like a dramatic 25 percent leap. Measured against the original NPR 1.964 trillion, it is a far more modest increase of roughly 8 percent. Same new budget. Same old year. Two completely different stories, depending on which "last year" you quietly choose as your starting line.

This is not a Nepali peculiarity, and it is not necessarily dishonest in any single instance — comparing to the revised estimate can be perfectly legitimate, because the revised estimate is closer to what actually happened. But it is a lever, and levers get pulled in whatever direction flatters the person pulling them. A government that wants to look bold compares its new budget to last year's deflated revised figure and trumpets a big percentage. A critic who wants to look unimpressed compares it to last year's original figure and shrugs at a small one. Your job, as a careful reader, is to always ask which base is being used, to look up the other one, and to notice that the choice of comparison is itself a choice that someone made for a reason.

## Why the timing shapes the dysfunction: a worked example

We said the timing of Nepal's fiscal year — beginning in mid-July, running straight through the monsoon — has consequences. Let us make that concrete, because it explains a pattern you will see again and again in this series.

Imagine a district in the hills that has, in this budget, been allocated money to build a new stretch of motorable road — a real, named line item, with a real rupee figure attached. The budget is presented on 29 May. The fiscal year, and therefore the legal authority to actually spend that money, begins around 16 July. Now consider what the rains do.

Nepal's monsoon arrives in roughly June and runs through September. It brings the heavy rains that the country's agriculture depends on, and it also brings landslides, swollen rivers, washed-out tracks, and saturated, unstable ground. You cannot pour the foundations of a road, cut a hillside, or move heavy equipment up a mountain track in the middle of a Himalayan monsoon. So the fiscal year that begins in mid-July begins in the worst possible conditions for the very construction it is meant to fund. By the time the ground has dried and the slopes have stabilised enough to build — well into autumn — months of the fiscal year are already gone.

Then comes the administrative reality. Before a single shovel moves, the project must be designed in detail, the work must be put out to tender, contractors must bid, a contract must be awarded, and any necessary land must be acquired from its owners — a process that in Nepal is famously slow and contested. Each of these steps eats weeks or months. The result is a brutal compression: a project that has the *whole fiscal year* on paper may in practice have only a few usable months of building weather and cleared paperwork, often crammed into the spring before the next monsoon arrives.

And so the pattern emerges that defines Nepali capital spending: a slow, idle start; a frantic, rushed finish as the fiscal year's deadline looms and officials scramble to spend money before the legal authority expires; and a chronic tendency for a large share of the construction budget to simply go unspent, returned at year's end as money that was allocated but never used. We will document this in detail in the post on capital spending. For now, simply understand that the calendar is not a neutral backdrop. The mid-July start, colliding with the monsoon and the slow machinery of procurement, is one of the structural reasons that the gap between *budgeted* and *actually spent* is so persistently wide. The constitution made sure the plan arrives on time. The seasons and the bureaucracy make sure the building rarely does.

## For the technically minded: converting and bounding the dates

For readers who work with data, two practical notes on handling Nepali dates, because getting them wrong is a common and embarrassing source of error.

First, here is the mapping this series relies on, laid out plainly. Treat the AD dates as approximate by a day or two, because the exact Gregorian date of a Bikram Sambat day shifts slightly from year to year.

| Event | Bikram Sambat | Gregorian (≈) |
|---|---|---|
| BS new year begins (month of Baisakh) | 1 Baisakh 2083 | mid-April 2026 |
| Economic Survey presented | ~14 Jestha 2083 | 28 May 2026 |
| Budget presented (constitutional date) | 15 Jestha 2083 | 29 May 2026 |
| Fiscal year 2083/84 begins (month of Shrawan) | 1 Shrawan 2083 | ~16 July 2026 |
| Fiscal year 2083/84 ends (end of Ashadh) | end Ashadh 2084 | ~16 July 2027 |

Second, a warning that matters for anyone tempted to write code that converts between the two calendars. You cannot convert Bikram Sambat to Gregorian dates with a simple formula, the way you might convert Celsius to Fahrenheit. The BS calendar's month lengths are not fixed by a tidy rule; they are determined by astronomical calculation and are published in official tables, and they can differ between years. The honest way to convert is to use a verified lookup table or a well-tested library that encodes those official month lengths, not arithmetic you invented. Expressed as pseudo-logic, the safe approach looks like this:

```text
function bs_to_ad(bs_year, bs_month, bs_day):
    # WRONG approach: ad_year = bs_year - 57 (off by up to a year, ignores month boundaries)
    # RIGHT approach: rely on official, verified month-length data.
    table = load_official_bs_month_lengths()      # per-year, per-month day counts
    reference = known_anchor_date()               # e.g. 1 Baisakh of some year -> a known AD date
    days_offset = count_days_from_anchor(bs_year, bs_month, bs_day, table)
    return reference.ad_date + days_offset
```

The lesson generalises beyond calendars: when a quantity is governed by published, irregular, authoritative data — whether it is the length of a Nepali month or the exact figure in a budget line — you look it up from the authority, you do not guess it from a pattern that feels about right. That instinct, to defer to the verified source rather than the plausible formula, is the same instinct that separates careful budget analysis from confident nonsense.

## How budgets deceive: the base-year switch

The technique of deception for this post follows directly from the three words we just learned, and it is one of the most common in all of public finance: **the base-year switch.**

Here is the move. To describe a change — in the budget's size, in spending on a sector, in revenue, in anything — you must compare two numbers: the new figure and some earlier figure. The earlier figure is the *base*. And because, as we have seen, there are several legitimate candidates for that base — last year's original estimate, last year's revised estimate, last year's actual outcome, the figure from two years ago, the figure adjusted for inflation or not — the person describing the change gets to *choose* which base makes their story look best, and then present the resulting percentage as if it were simply *the* fact.

A government wanting to advertise generosity toward, say, health spending can compare this year's health allocation to a year when health spending happened to be unusually low, producing an impressive percentage increase. A government wanting to claim it has *cut* waste can compare this year's spending on some category to a year when it was unusually high. A critic wanting to attack the same budget can do the reverse. None of them is necessarily lying about the individual numbers. They are choosing the comparison, and the comparison does the deceiving.

The defence is simple to state and requires a little discipline to practise. Whenever you are handed a percentage change — "25 percent larger," "doubled," "cut by a third," "the highest ever" — ask three questions. *Change from what base, exactly?* Insist on knowing the specific earlier figure, not just a vibe. *Why was that base chosen?* A revised estimate and an original estimate tell different stories; an unusually low or high prior year can be cherry-picked. And *what does the comparison look like against the other reasonable bases?* If a change looks dramatic against one base and trivial against another equally reasonable base, then the drama is coming from the choice of base, not from anything that actually happened.

Apply this to the headline we keep returning to. "Twenty-five point two percent larger than last year" is true against the revised estimate and misleading if you assume, as most listeners do, that it means twenty-five percent larger than last year's actual plan. Against the original budget it is roughly eight percent. A reader armed with the base-year question is not fooled by either framing; they simply note both, understand why each was chosen, and move on to the questions that matter more — such as whether any of this money will actually be spent. Which is exactly where the rest of the series is headed.

We have settled time. You now know that Nepal runs on the Bikram Sambat calendar, roughly 56 to 57 years ahead of the Gregorian one; that the budget we are studying is for the year 2083/84 BS, which is 2026/27 AD; that Nepal's fiscal year runs from around 16 July to around 16 July, straight through the monsoon; that the constitution forces the budget out on Jestha 15, about 29 May, weeks before the year it governs begins; that the Economic Survey the day before is the reality check the budget's optimism stands on; and that the difference between an *estimate*, a *revised estimate*, and an *actual* is the difference between a plan, an updated plan, and the truth. ## A Note on Numbers and Assumptions

All US dollar figures in this post are approximations for intuition, not accounting, using a single assumed rate of **1 US dollar = 150 Nepalese rupees** — a round figure close to the Nepal Rastra Bank reference rate in mid-2026. The Nepalese rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee at a fixed 1.60 rupees per Indian rupee, so its value against the dollar moves only when the Indian rupee's does, and it drifts daily. Treat no dollar figure here as exact.

All rupee figures are nominal — in the money of the day, not inflation-adjusted — unless stated otherwise. The previous year's revised estimate (cited here as roughly NPR 1.70 trillion) is the figure implied by the official 25.2 percent comparison and should be confirmed against the Ministry of Finance's own documents; the previous year's original budget of about NPR 1.964 trillion is the published headline. The Gregorian equivalents of Bikram Sambat dates are accurate to within a day or two and shift slightly year to year.

And the assumption underneath all the others, repeated until it is automatic: a figure that has been *announced* in a budget is not the same as one *enacted* into law, which is not the same as money actually *spent* and turned into something real. The constitution guarantees the plan arrives on time. It guarantees nothing about whether the plan is carried out.
