We have examined how this budget taxes individuals and how it taxes consumption. Now we turn to how it treats the third great object of tax and policy: businesses, and the investment that creates them. This is a subject close to the heart of the new government's promise. A party swept to power on a wave of frustration about jobs and opportunity, led by figures who present themselves as modern and enterprise-friendly, has filled this budget with measures aimed at making Nepal a better place to start, run, fund, and invest in a business. There is a corporate tax framework, generous incentives for exporters, reforms to the laws governing companies and bankruptcy, a new legal vehicle for venture capital, promises to foreign investors, and a one-stop system to cut through red tape.

It is an ambitious, coherent, pro-investment agenda, and parts of it are genuinely promising. But it arrives in a country with a long and sobering history of announcing business reforms that never quite become reality — where the binding obstacles to investment have less to do with the headline policies in any single budget than with deeper, more stubborn constraints that no budget speech can wish away. So this post will do two things. It will explain, from first principles, what these business measures are and why they might matter — corporate tax, export incentives, company and insolvency law, the investment-promotion machinery. And it will then apply the hard, recurring Nepali question: will any of this actually translate into investment and jobs, or is it another round of *reform-by-announcement*, where the speech is bold, the law is slow, and the reality is unchanged? By the end you will be able to read a business-reform package and judge not its ambition but its likely effect.

## What is corporate tax, and what does this budget do with it?

Begin with the tax that businesses pay. **Corporate income tax** is a tax on the *profits* of companies — the money a business has left after its costs are subtracted from its revenue. It is the business counterpart to the personal income tax we examined two posts ago: where personal income tax taxes individuals' earnings, corporate tax taxes companies' profits. It is a significant source of government revenue and an important lever of policy, because the rate of corporate tax affects how much of a company's profit it keeps to reinvest or distribute, and therefore affects the incentive to do business in the country at all.

Nepal's corporate tax has a tiered structure that this budget broadly maintains, and understanding the tiers reveals something about policy priorities. The *standard* corporate tax rate is **25 percent** — the rate most ordinary companies pay on their profits. But certain industries pay a *higher* rate of **30 percent**: banks and financial institutions, insurance companies, telecommunications companies, and the producers of tobacco and alcohol. Why these particular sectors at a higher rate? The logic differs by industry but the pattern is recognisable. Banks, insurance, and telecom are highly profitable, often near-monopolistic or oligopolistic sectors earning large returns, which governments judge can bear a higher rate. Tobacco and alcohol are the "sin" industries we met in the excise discussion — taxed heavily both for revenue and to reflect the social costs of their products. So the corporate tax structure encodes a judgement: ordinary business at 25 percent, highly profitable financial and telecom sectors and socially-costly sin industries at 30 percent. This budget broadly preserves that structure, which means corporate tax is not where the dramatic action is — unlike personal income tax, which was cut sharply, the corporate rates are largely held steady. The action for business is elsewhere: in targeted incentives and in legal reforms.

## The export incentives

One area where the budget does offer significant tax relief to business is *exports*, and the rationale connects to everything we learned about Nepal's economic vulnerabilities. This budget provides a **50 percent income-tax exemption on income from exports** — meaning a business that earns profit from exporting goods or services pays tax on only half of that export income, a substantial reduction. There is also a parallel **50 percent exemption for income from information-technology exports** specifically, and an exemption related to "sweat equity" in the IT sector, which we will examine more fully in the post on technology. Why this focus on exports?

The answer lies in Nepal's structural problem, which we have traced throughout this series: the country imports far more than it exports, depends on remittances and borrowing to pay for those imports, and lacks a strong base of its own productive, foreign-currency-earning industry. Exports are the cure that economists and policymakers most often prescribe for this condition — if Nepal could *export* more, it would earn foreign currency by its own productive effort rather than relying on the wages of migrants, build domestic industry, create jobs at home, and reduce its dangerous dependence on imports and remittances. So incentivising exports through tax relief is a coherent and well-motivated strategy: it tries to tilt the economy toward producing things the world will buy, which is exactly what Nepal most needs. The IT-export focus is particularly notable, because software and IT services are an export Nepal could plausibly grow — they require talent and connectivity rather than the ports and heavy infrastructure that landlocked Nepal lacks, making them one of the few export paths not blocked by geography. So the export incentives are among the more strategically sound elements of the budget, aimed squarely at a genuine national weakness.

But the recurring caution applies even here. A tax exemption *reduces the cost* of exporting for businesses that are already able to export, but it does not, by itself, *create* the capacity to export — the productive industries, the quality, the connectivity, the skills, the access to markets. A 50 percent tax exemption is valuable to a company that already has a viable export business; it does far less for the much harder problem of *building* export industries where they do not yet exist, which depends on all the deeper factors — reliable power, good logistics, skilled labour, stable policy, access to finance — that a tax break alone cannot supply. So the export incentives are a sound and welcome piece of the puzzle, but they are an incentive at the margin, not a transformation, and their effect depends on whether the deeper constraints on Nepal's productive capacity are also addressed. A tax break makes exporting more profitable for those who can already do it; it does not make a non-exporting economy into an exporting one.

## The legal reforms: company law, insolvency, and the rules of the game

Beyond taxes, the budget promises a set of reforms to the *laws* that govern how businesses operate — and these are, in principle, important, because the legal environment in which businesses exist often matters as much as the tax rate. Let us understand what these reforms are and why they could matter, while keeping our skepticism ready for whether they will actually happen.

The budget promises amendments to **company law** — the body of law governing how companies are formed, run, and dissolved. The specific reforms mentioned include changes around *conflicts of interest* (rules governing how company directors and insiders must handle situations where their personal interests conflict with the company's) and around making it *easier to dissolve* companies (the process by which a company that is no longer needed or viable is formally wound up and closed). Why do such reforms matter? Because the ease or difficulty of these legal processes shapes the whole life cycle of a business. Strong conflict-of-interest rules protect investors and build trust, which encourages investment; cumbersome dissolution processes, conversely, trap entrepreneurs in zombie companies they cannot legally close, discouraging the risk-taking that a dynamic economy needs (if starting a business that might fail means being unable to ever cleanly exit, fewer people will start). So reforming company law to protect investors and to make the formation and dissolution of companies cleaner can genuinely improve the business environment.

The budget also promises a new law for **limited liability partnerships** aimed at *venture capital and private equity* — and this is worth explaining because it targets a specific gap. First, "limited liability": this is the foundational legal concept that lets people invest in or run a business while risking only the money they put in, not their personal assets beyond that — if the business fails, the investors lose their investment but not their houses. Limited liability is what makes large-scale investment possible, because it lets many people pool money into a venture without each risking everything they own. A "limited liability partnership" is a particular legal *form* combining the flexibility of a partnership with this protection of limited liability — a form especially useful for the kind of pooled, professional investment done by *venture capital* (funds that invest in young, high-growth, high-risk companies, especially startups) and *private equity* (funds that invest in established companies). Why does Nepal need a law for this? Because the absence of a suitable legal vehicle for such pooled, professional investment has been a real obstacle to the development of a venture-capital and startup-funding ecosystem in Nepal — without the right legal form, the funds that finance young companies elsewhere struggle to organise themselves in Nepal. So a law enabling limited liability partnerships for venture capital and private equity addresses a genuine structural gap and could, if well-designed and actually implemented, help build the financing ecosystem that Nepal's aspiring entrepreneurs need. This connects to the technology and startup ambitions we will examine in Part 18.

The budget further promises amendments to **insolvency law** — the law governing what happens when a business (or, in some reforms, an individual) cannot pay its debts. A good insolvency regime provides an orderly process for dealing with failure: it lets a viable-but-struggling business restructure and survive, lets a hopeless one be wound up fairly, and protects both creditors (who are owed money) and debtors (who owe it) according to clear rules. Why does this matter for investment? Because the way a country handles failure shapes the willingness to take risk in the first place. If failure means chaos, indefinite legal limbo, or ruin, people invest and lend cautiously; if failure is handled by a clear, fair, predictable process, people are more willing to lend and to take entrepreneurial risk, knowing the consequences of failure are bounded and orderly. Reforming insolvency law — including, as the budget mentions, provisions touching consumers and smaller enterprises — can therefore improve the climate for both lending and entrepreneurship. A sound bankruptcy regime is, paradoxically, pro-investment, because it makes the risk of failure manageable.

Taken together, these legal reforms — to company law, to enable venture-capital vehicles, to insolvency — address real gaps in the legal infrastructure of Nepalese business, and they reflect a sophisticated understanding of what a modern investment ecosystem requires. On paper, they are among the more thoughtful elements of the budget. The question, as always, is whether they will move from paper to reality.

## The promises to foreign investors

The budget also reaches outward, with measures aimed at attracting *foreign* investment — money invested in Nepal by individuals and companies from abroad, which a poor, capital-scarce country needs because its own domestic savings are too small to fund all the investment its development requires. Foreign investment brings not only capital but often technology, management expertise, and access to international markets. The budget's measures to attract it include several distinct elements.

There are mentions of **bilateral investment protection agreements** and **double taxation avoidance agreements** — treaties between Nepal and other countries. A bilateral investment protection agreement is a treaty in which two countries promise to protect each other's investors from unfair treatment, expropriation without compensation, and the like, giving foreign investors confidence that their investments will be legally secure. A double taxation avoidance agreement is a treaty that ensures income is not taxed twice — once in the country where it is earned and again in the investor's home country — which removes a major disincentive to cross-border investment. Pursuing such agreements is standard practice for a country seeking foreign investment, and signals an intent to make Nepal a safer and more attractive destination for foreign capital.

There is the promise of an **"Investment Express" one-stop system** — a single point through which an investor can complete all the approvals, registrations, and permissions needed to invest, rather than navigating many separate agencies. This addresses one of the most notorious obstacles to investment in Nepal (and many developing countries): the maze of bureaucratic approvals an investor must obtain from numerous separate offices, each slow, each with its own requirements, which collectively can take months or years and which deter investors who could put their money somewhere easier. A genuine one-stop system that actually consolidated these approvals into a single, fast, predictable process would be a significant improvement — *if* it actually worked as promised, which is precisely the question.

There is the notable measure of permitting **foreign citizens to buy apartments** in Nepal, under certain conditions — a liberalisation of the rules on foreign ownership of property, aimed at attracting foreign money into real estate and signalling openness. And there is the measure allowing **non-resident Nepalis** — the large diaspora of Nepalis living abroad — to participate in the **secondary securities market**, meaning they would be able to buy and sell shares on Nepal's stock exchange, channelling some of the diaspora's wealth into Nepali capital markets. Both measures aim to draw foreign and diaspora money into the Nepali economy. Both, like the others, depend entirely on the conditions attached and on actual implementation.

These outward-facing measures, like the legal reforms, are coherent and aimed at real obstacles. A country that genuinely made itself easier and safer to invest in — through investment protection, tax treaties, a working one-stop system, and openness to foreign and diaspora capital — would indeed be more likely to attract the investment it needs. The agenda is sound in its direction. The entire question is whether the direction will be travelled.

## The recurring question: reform-by-announcement

Now we apply the skepticism that this series exists to supply, and that Nepal's history makes essential. Because here is the sobering pattern that hangs over every business-reform package in every Nepali budget: a long record of bold reforms *announced* that never became reality. Year after year, Nepali budgets have promised improved investment climates, one-stop systems, legal reforms, and openness to investors — and year after year, the actual experience of doing business and investing in Nepal has changed far less than the announcements promised. Investment-promotion events have showcased grand commitments that did not materialise into actual investment. One-stop systems have been announced more than once and have repeatedly fallen short of the seamless experience promised, as the underlying agencies and their delays persisted behind the new façade. Legal reforms have been promised in budgets and then languished, undrafted or unpassed, for years. This is not a partisan observation about the current government; it is a structural feature of how Nepali reform has so often worked — bold in the speech, slow in the law, unchanged in the reality.

There are deep reasons for this gap, and understanding them is the key to judging whether *this* budget's reforms will fare differently. The first is that a budget *announcement* is, as we established in Part 4, only the first stage of a long journey — many of these reforms require subsequent *legislation* (new laws drafted, debated, and passed) and *regulation* (detailed rules written by ministries) before they take effect at all, and that subsequent work is frequently slow, incomplete, or never done, so the announced reform never actually becomes operative law. The second reason is that even reforms that do become law often fail at *implementation*, because the bureaucracy that must carry them out is slow, under-resourced, sometimes resistant, and accustomed to the old ways — a one-stop system announced and even legally established may still, in practice, route investors through the same slow agencies if those agencies are not genuinely consolidated and reformed. The third and deepest reason is that the *real binding constraints* on investment in Nepal often lie outside the scope of any tax break or legal reform a budget can offer.

This third point deserves emphasis, because it is the most important and the most often ignored. The obstacles that most deter investment in Nepal — the ones that businesses themselves consistently cite — are frequently not the things a budget changes. They include *unreliable infrastructure*, above all electricity and the logistics of moving goods in a landlocked, mountainous country with limited and congested transport links to the sea through India. They include *policy instability and unpredictability* — the frequent changes of government, the shifting rules, the uncertainty about whether today's policy will survive tomorrow's politics, which makes long-term investment risky regardless of any single year's incentives. They include *weak enforcement and the slow, uncertain functioning of courts and contracts*, which makes investors doubt whether their rights will actually be protected when it matters, whatever the law on paper says. They include the difficulty of *access to finance*, *bureaucratic discretion and corruption*, and a *small domestic market*. These constraints are deep, structural, and largely *outside the reach of a budget's tax breaks and announced legal reforms* — and so long as they persist, even a budget full of well-designed business-friendly measures may produce far less actual investment than its ambition suggests, because investors weigh the deep constraints, not just the headline incentives. A 50 percent export exemption and a promised one-stop system are nice, but they do not fix the power shortages, the policy uncertainty, or the weak courts that may be what actually keeps investment away.

So the honest assessment of this budget's business agenda is double-edged, and a citizen should hold both edges. On one edge, the agenda is genuinely thoughtful and well-aimed: the legal reforms address real gaps, the export incentives target a real weakness, the investment-promotion measures tackle real obstacles, and the overall direction — toward a more open, modern, investment-friendly Nepal — is sound. A new government with a strong mandate and serious economic thinking at the helm might, just possibly, have the political will and the majority to actually carry through reforms that previous governments only announced. On the other edge, the weight of history and the depth of the binding constraints counsel caution: these are *announcements*, most of them requiring legislation and regulation that may not come, implementation that may falter, against deep structural obstacles that no budget can remove. The reforms could matter, if this government proves able to do what others have not — to convert announcement into law into implementation, and to address the deep constraints over time. Or they could join the long list of bold business-reform announcements that changed little. Which it will be is not determined by the budget speech; it will be determined over the coming years by whether the laws are actually passed, the systems actually built, and the deep constraints actually tackled. A citizen who understands this watches not the announcement but the follow-through — and treats a budget full of business reforms as a statement of intent to be tested, not an improvement already achieved.

## A worked example: a foreign investor meets the one-stop system

Make the reform-by-announcement gap concrete by following a hypothetical foreign investor through the promise and the reality of the "one-stop" investment system.

Imagine an investor abroad — a company that has heard Nepal is "open for business," that has read of the new one-stop Investment Express system, the investment protection, the tax incentives. Attracted by the announcements, the investor decides to explore investing in Nepal. The promise, as announced, is simple: a single point of entry through which all approvals, registrations, and permissions can be obtained smoothly and quickly. The investor expects, based on the announcement, an experience like the one the phrase "one-stop" implies — submit once, be approved promptly, begin investing.

Now follow what may actually happen, if the pattern of the past repeats. The investor discovers that, behind the one-stop façade, the underlying approvals still flow through multiple separate agencies — a registration body, a tax authority, sectoral regulators, perhaps local-government permissions, environmental clearances, and more — each of which still has its own requirements, its own queue, its own delays, because the "one-stop" system, though announced and perhaps even formally established, did not actually consolidate or speed up the real underlying processes. The investor finds that the legal reforms promised in the budget — the investor protections, the cleaner company and insolvency rules — are still being drafted, or have not yet passed, or lack the detailed regulations needed to make them operative, so the protections that attracted them are not yet real. The investor encounters the deep constraints the budget never addressed: uncertainty about whether the electricity supply will be reliable enough for their operation, doubts about how goods will move through the congested logistics to and from a landlocked country, worry about whether the policy and incentives will survive the next change of government, and unease about whether the courts would actually enforce their rights in a dispute. Faced with the gap between the announced ease and the actual difficulty, and with the deep constraints unaddressed, the investor may well decide to put their money in an easier country instead — and the investment, and the jobs it would have created, do not materialise, despite the budget's ambitious announcements.

The example reveals why reform-by-announcement so often fails to produce investment. The investor responds not to the *announcement* but to the *actual experience and the actual constraints* — and if the announcement is not backed by real legislation, real implementation, and real progress on the deep obstacles, the actual experience disappoints and the investment does not come. It also shows what *would* be required for the reforms to work: not just the announcement, but the unglamorous follow-through of passing the laws, writing the regulations, genuinely consolidating the agencies, and chipping away at the deep constraints of power, logistics, stability, and enforcement over years. A citizen who understands this judges the budget's business agenda not by how good the announcements sound but by whether, over the following years, the follow-through actually happens — whether the investor in our example would, two or three years from now, actually find a different and better reality than the pattern of the past would predict.

## For the technically minded: the reforms as a status table, and an approval workflow

For readers who think in terms of systems, the budget's business reforms are usefully tracked by their *status* — because, as we have stressed, the announcement is only the first stage, and what matters is how far each reform actually travels. Here is the agenda as a status table; the point is the third column, which a citizen should fill in over the coming years from the actual record:

| Reform | What it does | Status (to track over time) |
|---|---|---|
| Corporate tax 25% / 30% | Taxes company profits (higher for banks, insurance, telecom, sin goods) | Largely unchanged — in effect |
| 50% export income exemption | Halves tax on export profits | Announced — verify enactment in Finance Act |
| 50% IT-export exemption + sweat-equity | Tax relief for IT exporters | Announced — verify enactment (see Part 18) |
| Company law amendments | Conflict-of-interest rules, easier dissolution | Announced — requires legislation |
| LLP law for VC/PE | Legal vehicle for pooled professional investment | Announced — requires new law |
| Insolvency law amendments | Orderly handling of failure; consumers, MSMEs | Announced — requires legislation |
| Investment protection / tax treaties | Protect and de-double-tax foreign investors | Announced/ongoing — requires treaties |
| Investment Express one-stop system | Single point for investment approvals | Announced — requires real consolidation |
| Foreign apartment purchase | Allows foreign property ownership (conditional) | Announced — requires rules; check conditions |
| NRN secondary-market access | Diaspora can trade listed shares | Announced — requires rules |

The discipline the table enforces is the announced-enacted-implemented distinction from Part 4, applied to business reform: almost every item is, at budget time, merely *announced*, and the honest tracking is to follow each one through enactment (does the law actually pass?), implementation (is the system actually built, the agency actually consolidated?), and effect (does investment actually rise?). Now an approval-workflow sketch, showing the gap between the one-stop promise and the multi-agency reality:

```text
# The PROMISE: one-stop.
def invest_promised(application):
    return single_window.approve(application)   # one submission, prompt approval

# The REALITY that reform-by-announcement leaves unchanged:
def invest_reality(application):
    approvals = []
    for agency in [company_registrar, tax_authority, sector_regulator,
                   local_government, environment, foreign_investment_board]:
        approvals.append(agency.process(application))   # each: own queue, own delay
        if agency.is_slow_or_discretionary(): wait()     # the real bottleneck
    # And none of these touch the DEEP constraints:
    if not reliable_electricity(): risk += 1
    if not good_logistics():       risk += 1
    if not policy_stability():     risk += 1
    if not enforceable_contracts(): risk += 1
    return approvals, risk

# A "one-stop system" is only real if it actually replaces invest_reality with
# invest_promised -- by genuinely consolidating the agencies and speeding the process,
# not merely placing a single web page in front of the same slow machinery.
# And even a real one-stop system does nothing about the deep-constraint risk.
```

The workflow makes the point precisely. A one-stop system that merely puts a single front door in front of the same unchanged agencies behind it is reform-by-announcement; a one-stop system that genuinely consolidates and speeds the underlying approvals is real reform — and the difference is invisible in the announcement and visible only in the investor's actual experience. And even genuine procedural reform leaves untouched the deep-constraint risks — power, logistics, stability, enforcement — that may be what actually deters investment. A developer or analyst modelling Nepal's investment climate would not model the announced reforms; they would model the *actual* approval process and the *actual* deep constraints, because those, not the speech, determine whether investment comes. That is exactly how a citizen should read this budget's business agenda: by what it will actually change in the investor's real experience, tracked over years, not by how good its intentions sound today.

## How budgets deceive: reform-by-announcement

The technique of deception for this post is the one we have been circling, now stated fully, because it is among the most important in all of governance and especially in Nepal: **reform-by-announcement** — the accumulation of political credit for *announcing* reforms, while the reforms never actually become reality, and the reality that deters investment stays unchanged.

Here is the move, and it is a special, business-flavoured case of the announcement-as-achievement deception from Part 4. Reforms — especially business-friendly reforms — are popular with important audiences: investors, the business community, the media, international observers, and the public hungry for jobs and growth. A government earns credit, attention, and a reputation for being "pro-business" and "reformist" simply by *announcing* an ambitious slate of reforms in its budget — one-stop systems, legal modernisation, openness to investment. And because reforms require a long, slow, unglamorous journey through legislation, regulation, and implementation before they change anything real — a journey that frequently stalls — the government can bank the reputational credit for the announcement immediately, while the actual reforms quietly fail to materialise over the following years, by which time attention has moved on. The deception works because the *announcement* is vivid, immediate, and reported, while the *failure to implement* is diffuse, slow, and largely invisible — no one reports that a reform announced two budgets ago was never actually passed, or that the one-stop system never actually consolidated the agencies. So a government can be "reformist" in reputation while being unchanged in reality, accumulating the credit of announcement after announcement, none of which need ever become real, especially when the deepest obstacles to investment lie outside what any budget reform could touch.

The defence is to *track reforms from announcement to reality, and to keep your eye on the deep constraints*. When a government announces business reforms, treat each as a *promise to be verified*, not an *achievement to be credited*, and ask, over the following months and years: Was the law actually drafted and passed? Were the regulations actually written? Was the system — the one-stop window, the consolidated agency — actually built and actually working, or merely re-branded? Did the reform actually change the experience of doing business, as measured by those who do it? And, crucially, ask whether the reforms even address the *binding constraints* — the power, the logistics, the policy stability, the enforcement — or whether they are tinkering at the margins while the real obstacles remain untouched, in which case even fully-implemented reforms would change little. A citizen who tracks the follow-through, refuses to credit announcements as achievements, and keeps asking whether the deep constraints are being addressed cannot be deceived by reform-by-announcement — and is positioned to give a government real credit if and when it actually delivers, which is itself the incentive most likely to produce delivery. The deception thrives on the public crediting intentions; the defence is to credit only results, tracked over time, against the constraints that actually matter.
