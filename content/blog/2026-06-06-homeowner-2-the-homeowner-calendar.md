## Part 1: Why a Calendar Changes Everything

There is a fundamental difference between the homeowner who maintains proactively and the homeowner who repairs reactively. The proactive homeowner spends less money, experiences fewer crises, and sleeps better. The reactive homeowner spends more money, often at the worst possible times — emergency HVAC service on the hottest day of summer costs two to three times more than scheduled spring maintenance. A roof leak discovered after a storm costs three times as much to repair as the same damage caught in an annual inspection.

The difference between these two homeowners is not luck, income, or skill. It is a calendar.

This post is that calendar. It covers every month of the year with specific, actionable tasks organized by urgency, frequency, and system. Some tasks appear every month. Some appear once a year. Some appear once every several years. We have organized them all so you know exactly what to do when.

A few important notes before we begin:

**Climate matters.** This calendar is written for a temperate North American climate with four distinct seasons. If you live in a climate without hard freezes — coastal California, south Florida, Hawaii, the Gulf Coast — some of the winterization tasks are reduced or eliminated. If you live in a climate with extremely harsh winters — northern Minnesota, upstate New York, northern Canada — some tasks need to happen earlier. Adjust for your climate.

**Your home's age and systems matter.** A 1980 home with original plumbing, an older HVAC system, and a 20-year-old roof needs more frequent attention than a 2018 home with new systems throughout. The tasks listed here are baseline minimums. Add more inspection and service as your home's age and condition require.

**Track what you do.** Every time you complete a maintenance task, write it down — date, what you did, anything you noticed, any work that was recommended for the future. This is your maintenance log, and it is genuinely valuable. It tells you how long ago the filter was last changed, when the HVAC was last serviced, when the gutters were last cleaned. Without a log, you are guessing.

## Part 2: The Monthly Non-Negotiables

Before we go month by month, there are tasks that should happen every single month. Do these without fail.

### Every Month: HVAC Filter Check

Check your HVAC air filter every single month. If it is visibly dirty or clogged — gray, fuzzy, thick with dust — replace it immediately. If it still looks relatively clean, check again next month.

Standard 1-inch fiberglass filters should be replaced every 30 days. Standard 1-inch pleated filters can last 60 to 90 days depending on conditions. Thick 4- to 5-inch media filters can last 6 to 12 months. Homes with pets, with dusty environments, or with people who have allergies need more frequent replacement across all filter types.

A clogged filter is the single most common cause of HVAC system problems. The filter is there to protect the system's internal components — specifically the evaporator coil — from dust accumulation. When the filter is clogged, the system has to work harder to move air, which stresses the blower motor, reduces efficiency, and can cause the evaporator coil to ice over, which can flood your system with water when the ice melts. Replacing the filter costs $2 to $15. Cleaning a flooded air handler or replacing a burned-out blower motor costs $200 to $1,500.

Buy a year's supply of filters at once. Put a recurring reminder in your calendar.

### Every Month: Visual Check Under All Sinks

Open every cabinet under every sink in the house — kitchen, bathrooms, utility room — and look inside. Look for:

- Any standing water or water stains
- Any drips or moisture on the pipes
- Any corrosion on the supply lines or shutoff valves
- Any swelling or discoloration on the cabinet floor (indicating past water intrusion)
- Any musty smell

Most plumbing leaks under sinks start small — a slow drip, a weeping fitting, a slightly loose supply line connection — and grow over time. They are invisible inside the cabinet unless you look. When found early, they are a 15-minute repair. Left for months, they can rot the cabinet floor, grow mold, and damage the subfloor.

This check takes 45 seconds per sink. Do it every month.

### Every Month: Test Smoke and CO Detectors

You should be testing your smoke and carbon monoxide detectors monthly. Press the test button on each one. It should sound. If it does not sound, or sounds weak, replace the battery immediately and test again. If replacing the battery does not fix it, replace the detector.

Most smoke detectors should be replaced every 10 years. Most CO detectors should be replaced every 5 to 7 years. Write the installation date on the back of each detector in permanent marker so you know when to replace it.

### Every Month: Walk the Exterior

Once a month, walk around the outside of your home and look at everything with fresh eyes. Look for:

- Changes in the roofline (sagging, missing shingles visible from the ground)
- Damaged or missing flashing around chimneys, vents, or skylights
- Damaged or peeling paint on siding or trim
- Gaps or cracks in caulking around windows, doors, and penetrations
- Debris accumulation against the foundation
- Signs of pest activity (mud tubes for termites, holes in wood, burrows near the foundation)
- Cracks in the driveway or walkways that have grown since last month
- Gutters pulling away from the fascia or visibly sagging

This walk takes five minutes and gives you a continuous baseline. When something changes, you notice it before it becomes a serious problem.

## Part 3: The Full 12-Month Calendar

### January

**January is the heart of winter in most of North America.** The house is under thermal stress, heating systems are running at maximum load, and water is the dominant hazard — frozen pipes, ice dams, and condensation. January is also the month when most people are exhausted and broke from the holidays, which makes it tempting to defer maintenance. Do not.

**Heating System Check**
Your heating system — furnace, boiler, or heat pump — has been running hard since fall. This month:
- Check and replace the air filter if you have not already done so since the heating season began.
- Listen for unusual sounds from the furnace or air handler: rattling, banging, squealing, or rumbling are all signs that something needs attention.
- Check that all supply vents and return air grilles are open and unobstructed. Furniture moved for the holidays sometimes blocks vents.
- If you have baseboard electric heat or radiators, dust the fins and check that they are not blocked.
- If you have a steam boiler, check the water level in the sight glass (should be halfway up). Drain a cup of water from the low-water cutoff to flush sediment.
- If you have a hot water boiler (hydronic), check the pressure gauge (should be 12 to 15 PSI when cold). Check the expansion tank by pressing the Schrader valve — if water comes out, the bladder has failed and the tank needs replacement.

**Frozen Pipe Prevention and Response**
In cold climates, frozen pipes are the most expensive plumbing failure. A single frozen and burst pipe can cause $5,000 to $30,000 in water damage.
- Keep the thermostat at 55°F or higher even when the house is empty.
- Know where your vulnerable pipes are: pipes on exterior walls, pipes in unheated spaces (garage, crawl space, unheated basement areas), pipes near exterior doors.
- Open cabinet doors under kitchen sinks on exterior walls to allow heated air to reach the pipes.
- If you have a vacant property in a cold climate, either shut off the water and drain the lines or maintain heat throughout.
- If a pipe freezes (you turn on a faucet and nothing comes out, or a toilet tank does not fill), locate the frozen section and apply a hair dryer or heating pad — never an open flame. Work from the faucet end toward the frozen section so steam can escape. If you cannot locate the frozen section or cannot thaw it, call a plumber.

**Ice Dam Prevention**
An ice dam is a ridge of ice that forms at the edge of a roof when heat from the attic melts snow on the upper portion of the roof, which then refreezes at the cold eaves. As the ice dam grows, water backs up behind it, finds gaps in the roofing, and enters the attic and ceiling.
- After heavy snowfall, if you can safely do so from the ground, use a roof rake to remove snow from the lower three to four feet of the roof. Do not go on a snow-covered roof.
- If you are getting recurring ice dams despite raking, the root cause is typically inadequate attic insulation and air sealing — warm house air is escaping into the attic and melting the snow. This needs to be addressed in spring.
- If you already have an ice dam forming: you can apply calcium chloride ice melt (not rock salt — rock salt damages shingles) in nylon stockings laid perpendicular across the dam to create channels for water to drain. Do not chip at ice dams with hammers or picks — you will damage the shingles.

**Check for Condensation on Windows**
Condensation on the interior surface of windows in winter indicates high indoor humidity. Some condensation on very cold days is normal, but persistent or heavy condensation can indicate:
- An excessive moisture source inside the house (too many plants, a humidifier set too high, cooking or shower steam not venting properly)
- Inadequate ventilation
- Failing insulated glass (condensation between the panes means the seal has failed)

**Other January Tasks**
- Test the garage door auto-reverse mechanism: place a 2x4 flat on the ground in the door's path. When the door contacts it and does not reverse within 2 seconds, the auto-reverse mechanism needs adjustment.
- Check the dryer vent for ice blockage if the vent exits through the wall in a cold location.
- Stock emergency supplies: batteries, flashlights, water, and a manual can opener in case of a prolonged power outage.

### February

**February is typically the coldest month and the highest-risk month for interior damage from frozen pipes, ice dams, and heating system failures.** Maintenance priorities are similar to January.

**Chimney and Fireplace Inspection (if not done in fall)**
If you use a wood-burning fireplace or stove and did not have the chimney inspected and cleaned in the fall, do it now before the remainder of the heating season. Creosote — the tarry byproduct of burning wood — accumulates on the interior of the flue and is highly flammable. Chimney fires are violent, fast, and can spread to the structure of the house.
- The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) recommends annual inspection of fireplaces, chimneys, and vents.
- If you burn wood regularly, the chimney should be swept annually regardless of visual inspection results.
- If you burn occasionally, a visual inspection may reveal whether sweeping is needed.
- Hire a Chimney Safety Institute of America (CSIA) certified chimney sweep.

**Attic Inspection**
February is a good month to inspect the attic because winter stress makes problems visible. Look for:
- Signs of ice dam water intrusion: staining on the roof decking or rafters, wet or matted insulation near the eaves
- Frost formation on the underside of the roof decking (indicates warm moist air from the living space reaching the attic — an air sealing problem)
- Mold or mildew on wood surfaces (another indicator of moisture intrusion or inadequate ventilation)
- Animal intrusion: squirrel nests, mouse droppings, gnawed wiring, disturbed insulation
- Adequate insulation depth: in most climates, attic insulation should be R-38 to R-60 (roughly 12 to 20 inches of blown fiberglass or cellulose)

**Water Heater Check**
Check around the base of the water heater for any rust stains, corrosion, or moisture. Check the temperature and pressure relief (T&P) valve — it should not have mineral buildup or corrosion around the outlet. If the tank is more than 8 years old, start budgeting for replacement.

**Test GFCI Outlets**
Press the test button on every GFCI outlet in the house. It should trip (power off to the outlet). Press reset. If any outlet does not trip or does not reset, replace it.

### March

**March is transition month — winter is loosening its grip, but not reliably.** March is also when winter's damage becomes visible. This is an inspection and planning month, not yet a repair month (weather can still prevent exterior work in many climates).

**Post-Winter Exterior Inspection**
Walk the exterior carefully after the snow melts. Look for:
- **Foundation:** New or widened cracks, any signs of heaving or settling that changed over winter, efflorescence (white mineral deposits on concrete or masonry indicating water movement through the wall)
- **Driveway and walkways:** Frost-heaved sections, new cracking, deteriorated asphalt or concrete
- **Exterior walls:** Paint that has bubbled, peeled, or cracked (may indicate moisture behind the siding), caulking gaps around windows, doors, and penetrations
- **Roof (from the ground):** Missing shingles, lifted flashing, damaged ridge cap, sagging sections
- **Gutters:** Gutters that have pulled away from the fascia, gutters that are visibly bent or have separated at seams, downspouts that are detached or missing splash blocks

**Schedule Spring HVAC Maintenance**
Call your HVAC contractor now to schedule a spring tune-up of the cooling system (air conditioner or heat pump). Do not wait until June — every other homeowner is calling then. March appointments are easier to schedule, often faster, and the technician is less rushed.

Spring AC tune-up typically includes:
- Checking refrigerant charge and looking for leaks
- Cleaning the evaporator coil (inside) and condenser coil (outside)
- Testing capacitors, contactors, and other electrical components
- Lubricating moving parts
- Checking the condensate drain and pan
- Measuring airflow and static pressure
- Testing the thermostat

This is also the time to change the batteries in your thermostat if it is battery-powered.

**Inspect and Clean Window Wells**
If you have basement windows below grade (in window wells), clean out the wells. Debris — leaves, dirt, dead plants — accumulates over winter and can block drainage, causing water to pool against the window and eventually enter the basement.

**Check Crawl Space (if applicable)**
After winter, inspect the crawl space for moisture accumulation, pest activity, insulation displacement, or plumbing issues. Replace any vapor barrier sections that have been disturbed. Ensure foundation vents are operational (in vented crawl spaces, vents are typically closed in winter and open in spring).

**Other March Tasks**
- Service the lawn mower and other outdoor power equipment: fresh oil, new spark plug, clean air filter, sharpen the blade. Do this before you need it in April.
- Test outdoor hose bibs: turn them on and check for leaks inside the wall (a sign that a pipe burst behind the wall during a freeze but has not yet caused visible damage).
- Begin planning summer projects — request contractor quotes now before the spring rush.

### April

**April is when maintenance becomes outdoor work.** Weather is generally cooperative, the ground has thawed, and it is time to begin the seasonal exterior tasks that protect the house through spring rains and summer heat.

**Gutter Cleaning — Spring**
Clean gutters and downspouts in spring after tree pollen and debris have fallen. (You will clean them again in fall after the leaves drop.) Use a ladder to access the gutters, scoop out debris, then flush with a garden hose. Check that water flows freely through downspouts. Each downspout should discharge at least 4 to 6 feet away from the foundation — use downspout extensions or splash blocks as needed.

Look for gutters that are holding standing water after flushing — they are not pitched correctly and need adjustment. Look for gutters with holes, separated joints, or separated seams — these need repair or replacement.

**Inspect and Re-caulk Exterior**
Walk the exterior with a caulk gun and a tube of exterior paintable caulk. Caulk any gaps or cracks you find:
- Around all window and door frames where the frame meets the siding
- Around any penetrations through the exterior walls (pipes, conduit, hose bibs, dryer vents, electrical boxes)
- At the joint between the sill plate and foundation (the very bottom of the wood framing where it meets the concrete)
- At any corner trim joints or seams in siding

Use the right caulk for the application. Exterior paintable acrylic-latex caulk for most gaps in wood or composite surfaces. 100% silicone for joints that experience significant movement or that will be exposed to water. Fire-rated caulk for any penetrations through fire-rated walls (common at the garage-house interface).

**Lawn Wake-Up**
April is when the lawn begins growing in most temperate climates. Tasks:
- First mowing of the year — set blade high (3 to 4 inches), do not scalp
- Apply pre-emergent herbicide for crabgrass prevention if you use one — timing is critical, must be applied before soil temperature reaches 55°F consistently
- Apply first application of fertilizer if you fertilize — a slow-release nitrogen fertilizer
- Overseed bare patches in cool-season grass (Kentucky bluegrass, tall fescue, ryegrass)
- Inspect irrigation system heads if you have in-ground irrigation; start up the system and check each zone

**Deck and Patio Inspection**
If you have a wood deck:
- Inspect every board for rot, splitting, or loose nails or screws. Pay special attention to the ledger board (where the deck attaches to the house) and the posts at grade (where wood meets or is near soil)
- Check for loose or missing handrail balusters
- Check for structural movement or settlement
- Power wash if needed, then seal or stain if the wood is showing wear (water should bead on properly sealed wood; if it soaks in, it needs a coat)

**Pest Control Preventive Treatment**
Spring is the season when insect colonies become active. If you use a pest control service, schedule your first treatment. Check the perimeter of the house for any signs of termite activity: mud tubes on foundation walls, hollow-sounding wood, small piles of wood pellets (frass) near wood structures.

### May

**May is the spring push — the most productive outdoor maintenance month of the year.** Weather is good, days are getting longer, and there is still time before summer heat makes outdoor work unpleasant.

**AC Start-Up and Test**
If your spring HVAC service was not done in March or April, do it now — but do it before you actually need the cooling. Turn on the AC for the first time on a mild day and observe: Does it start without unusual sounds? Does cool air come out of all the vents? Does the outdoor unit run without excessive vibration or noise? Does the air handler drain properly (check the condensate pan under the air handler — it should be dry; any standing water indicates a clogged condensate drain)?

**Clean Dryer Vent**
The dryer vent — the duct that runs from the dryer to the exterior of the house — accumulates lint regardless of how well you clean the lint trap. This accumulated lint is a fire hazard. According to the National Fire Protection Association, dryers and washing machines were involved in roughly 13,820 home fires per year in the United States during the most recent reporting periods.

Clean the dryer vent annually:
- Disconnect the dryer from the duct (usually a clamp-on flexible metal duct at the back)
- From the laundry room, insert a dryer vent cleaning brush (a long flexible brush available at hardware stores for $15 to $30) as far as it will go and work it back and forth
- From the exterior, remove the vent cover and use the brush from that end as well
- Vacuum up all debris
- Reconnect the duct, making sure there are no kinks that could trap lint
- The dryer vent run should be as short and straight as possible — every 90-degree elbow adds the equivalent of several feet of straight run to the restriction. The maximum allowed run varies by dryer model but is typically 25 feet of straight duct minus 5 feet for each 90-degree elbow.

**Attic Ventilation Check**
In May, before summer heat arrives, confirm your attic ventilation is adequate:
- Soffit vents (at the eave) should be open and unobstructed — look from inside the attic to confirm daylight is visible through them, and that insulation has not been pushed against them
- Ridge vent or gable vents should be open
- Power attic ventilators (if present) should be operational
An inadequately ventilated attic can reach 150°F or more in summer, dramatically shortening roof shingle life (heat is one of the primary degradation mechanisms for asphalt shingles) and increasing cooling costs.

**Exterior Paint Inspection**
In May light, walk the exterior and look critically at painted surfaces — siding, trim, fascia, soffit. Any paint that is chalking heavily, peeling, blistering, or cracking needs attention. Exposed wood without paint protection will rot. Plan any needed painting for this season while weather is good.

**Water Heater Flush**
Sediment — mineral deposits from the water supply — accumulates at the bottom of tank water heaters. This sediment reduces efficiency (the burner or element has to work through the sediment layer to heat the water), creates hot spots that can damage the tank, and makes a rumbling or popping noise when heated. Flush the tank annually:
1. Connect a garden hose to the drain valve at the bottom of the tank
2. Route the hose to a floor drain or outside
3. Turn off the cold water supply to the heater
4. Open the drain valve and let the tank empty (this may take 30 to 60 minutes for a 50-gallon tank)
5. Briefly open the cold water supply to stir up and flush remaining sediment
6. Close the drain valve, open the cold water supply, let the tank refill, then restore power (electric) or relight (gas)

Note: If the drain valve has never been opened and the water heater is more than 5 years old, the valve may leak slightly after being opened. Have a replacement valve on hand or be prepared to call a plumber.

### June

**June marks the beginning of summer and the transition to cooling-season priorities.** In hot climates, the air conditioner is now the most important system in the house.

**Outdoor Faucet (Hose Bib) Check**
Turn on each outdoor hose bib and check the water pressure. Check inside the walls behind each hose bib (in basements, crawl spaces, or utility areas) for any signs of a slow leak that may have developed during winter.

**Lawn Irrigation System Check**
If you have in-ground irrigation, check every zone, every head. Look for:
- Broken or cracked heads (they will spray water inefficiently or not at all)
- Heads that are tilted or have sunk below grade
- Heads that spray onto driveways, sidewalks, or the house rather than the lawn (wasted water and can cause staining)
- Zones that do not activate (electrical fault, solenoid failure, or wiring issue)
- Controller settings — are you watering at appropriate times (early morning is best) and for appropriate durations?

**Air Conditioning Efficiency Check**
Check the outdoor condenser unit:
- Clear any vegetation that has grown up around the unit — there should be at least 18 to 24 inches of clearance on all sides for adequate airflow
- Gently clean the condenser fins with a garden hose (spray from inside out — do not use a pressure washer)
- Make sure the pad the unit sits on is level (settling can stress refrigerant lines)

**Basement and Crawl Space Moisture Check**
As outdoor humidity rises in summer, basements and crawl spaces become vulnerable to condensation and mold. Check:
- Dehumidifier operation and setting (typically 50% relative humidity is the target in a basement)
- Sump pump function — pour a bucket of water into the sump pit and confirm the pump activates
- Crawl space vapor barrier integrity
- Any signs of water seepage along foundation walls after recent rain

**Check and Clean Bathroom Exhaust Fans**
Bathroom exhaust fans remove moisture and odors. A fan clogged with lint and dust does neither efficiently, which means moisture stays in the bathroom and feeds mold.
- Remove the grille and wash it with warm soapy water
- Use a vacuum to clean the fan housing and blades
- Check that the exhaust duct (usually flexible duct in the attic) is connected to the fan housing and terminates at the exterior — exhaust that terminates in the attic creates a serious moisture problem

**Prepare for Severe Weather Season**
June is the beginning of severe weather season in many parts of the country — tornado season in the Midwest and Plains, hurricane season for coastal areas, severe thunderstorm season almost everywhere.
- Know your home's severe weather shelter location
- Check your emergency kit — batteries, water, first aid supplies, radio
- If you are in a hurricane zone, review your window and door protection options (shutters, plywood) and know the local evacuation routes

### July

**July is peak cooling season.** The primary focus is HVAC maintenance, managing indoor humidity, and preventing heat-related damage.

**HVAC Filter — Replace Without Fail**
In summer, the HVAC is running more hours than at any other time of year. The filter loads faster. If you are on a 90-day replacement schedule, summer conditions may require 60-day or even 30-day replacement. Check it this month regardless of your schedule.

**Check Condensate Drain Line**
The air conditioner's evaporator coil removes humidity from the air as it cools it, and that water has to go somewhere — it drips into a condensate pan and drains through a condensate drain line (usually a white PVC pipe that exits through the wall or drains to a floor drain). This drain line can become clogged with algae and mold.

Signs of a clogged condensate drain: water in the condensate pan, water dripping from the air handler, high indoor humidity. To prevent clogs:
- Pour a cup of diluted bleach or white vinegar into the drain line access port (usually a capped pipe near the air handler) every few months
- If the line is already clogged, a wet/dry vacuum can clear it from the exterior exit point, or a plumber can clear it

**Inspect Attic for Heat Damage**
In July heat, the attic may reach extreme temperatures. This is an opportunity to check:
- Are attic ventilation paths clear? (If not, temperatures are even higher)
- Is any electrical wiring or equipment up there showing heat stress? (Discolored insulation on wires, cracked conduit)
- Is the attic insulation deep enough? (Thin insulation means more heat transfers into the living space)

**Exterior Wood Check**
Hot, dry summer conditions cause wood to expand and then crack as it dries. Check exterior trim, door frames, window sills, and deck boards for new cracks or splitting. Seal any cracks promptly to prevent water infiltration in the next rain.

**Refrigerator Coil Cleaning**
The refrigerator's condenser coils — located either behind the unit or underneath it — accumulate dust and pet hair, which impairs heat dissipation and reduces efficiency. Dirty coils make the compressor work harder and shorten the appliance's life.
- Unplug the refrigerator
- Remove the rear panel or the front kick plate (depending on the coil location)
- Use a vacuum with a brush attachment and a refrigerator coil brush to clean the coils
- Plug the refrigerator back in

Do this annually — July works well because it is hot and the refrigerator is working hardest.

### August

**August is often the hottest month, and the HVAC system is under maximum stress.** It is also when HVAC failures are most common and most expensive. Have backup plans for any HVAC failure — know who to call and where your family can go if the system fails on a 95-degree day.

**HVAC Filter — Check Again**
Running continuously in August heat, the filter may need replacement. Check it.

**Check All Window and Door Seals**
Summer heat makes gaps in window and door weatherstripping obvious — you can feel the hot air coming in around them. Now is the time to note which seals need replacement before the fall and winter heating season.

**Pest Inspection**
Late summer is when many pest species are at peak activity. Check:
- Foundation perimeter for ant trails, termite mud tubes, or rodent burrows
- Wood structures (deck, fence, window trim) for carpenter ant galleries or bore holes from other wood-boring insects
- Attic vents for wasp or hornet nests
- The crawl space entrance

**Irrigation System Efficiency Audit**
With summer water bills in hand, it is a good time to assess whether your irrigation is efficient:
- Are you watering in the early morning? (Reduces evaporation loss compared to midday)
- Are you watering too frequently? (Most established lawns need 1 to 1.5 inches per week; too-frequent shallow watering produces shallow roots)
- Do you have a rain sensor that shuts off irrigation after rainfall? (If not, this $25 device saves significant water and money)

**Begin Planning Fall Projects**
Contractors are busy in spring and summer. August is a good time to begin soliciting quotes for fall exterior projects — painting, driveway sealing, gutter replacement, roofing — so you are on the schedule before the fall rush.

---

### September

**September is the pivot point — the cooling season is ending and the heating season is about to begin.** This is one of the two most important maintenance months of the year (the other is March).

**HVAC Fall Service**
Schedule your HVAC fall service now if you have not already. The fall tune-up covers the heating system:
- For a gas furnace: clean the burners, check the heat exchanger for cracks (a cracked heat exchanger is a CO hazard), test the ignition system, check the flue venting, lubricate moving parts, test safety controls
- For a heat pump: the same equipment handles both heating and cooling, so fall service focuses on heating mode performance — testing the reversing valve, checking the defrost cycle, verifying backup heat operation
- For an oil furnace: this is even more important than gas — oil furnaces need annual cleaning and nozzle replacement for efficient operation

**Clean Gutters — Leaves Are Starting**
In many climates, the first significant leaf drop begins in late September. If your trees drop leaves early, clean gutters now and again in late fall. Gutters blocked with leaves cannot drain, and water that overflows gutters runs down the exterior walls and collects at the foundation.

**Service the Chimney**
If you have a wood-burning fireplace or stove, have the chimney inspected and swept before the heating season begins. This is a non-negotiable safety measure. Schedule now — chimney sweeps are busy in October and November.

**Irrigation System Winterization**
In climates with freezing winters, the in-ground irrigation system must be winterized before the first hard freeze. If the system is not blown out with compressed air, water remaining in the underground lines will freeze and crack the pipes, heads, and manifolds. Repair costs for a freeze-damaged irrigation system can be $500 to $3,000 or more.

Most homeowners hire an irrigation professional to blow out the system with compressed air — this costs $50 to $150 and is money well spent. The timing depends on your climate: before the first frost is too conservative in most cases; before the first hard freeze (28°F or lower) is the correct target.

**Exterior Door and Window Weatherstripping**
Inspect all exterior door weatherstripping and window gaskets. Close each door and window and check for daylight around the perimeter. Run your hand around the edges on a windy day — you will feel any air infiltration. Replace worn or cracked weatherstripping before winter. This is an easy DIY project and the energy savings are immediate.

**Rodent Prevention**
Fall is when mice and other rodents seek warmth inside structures. Before they move in:
- Seal any gaps in the foundation or exterior walls larger than a dime (mice can fit through a 1/4-inch gap)
- Check that utility penetrations through the exterior walls are sealed
- Inspect the garage door seal (bottom sweep) for gaps
- Remove wood piles, debris, and dense vegetation near the house perimeter (rodent habitat)
- Consider placing snap traps in the garage and basement as a preventive measure before evidence of intrusion appears

### October

**October is exterior finishing month — the last window before cold weather makes exterior work difficult or impossible.** Anything that needs to be done outside that was not done in September should happen in early October.

**Final Exterior Painting and Caulking**
Any exterior painting or caulking that was planned must happen before temperatures drop below 50°F (most exterior paints and caulks require above-50°F temperatures for proper cure). October is often the last viable month in many climates.

**Gutter Cleaning — Primary Fall Cleaning**
After the majority of the leaves have fallen (but before the first hard freeze), do the primary fall gutter cleaning. This is arguably the most important gutter cleaning of the year — the gutters must be clear before winter so snowmelt can drain properly.

Clean gutters, flush downspouts, check that splash blocks are in place, and verify that all downspout extensions are directing water away from the foundation.

**Disconnect and Store Garden Hoses**
Before the first frost, disconnect all garden hoses from outdoor hose bibs and store them. A connected hose traps water in the hose bib and the interior pipe, which can freeze and burst even if the hose bib has a frost-free design. Frost-free hose bibs work by having the actual shutoff valve set back inside the wall, away from the cold. But if a hose is connected and traps water in the pipe, that protection fails.

**Check Roof from the Ground**
With leaves off the trees, October provides one of the clearest views of the roof. Use binoculars from the ground to inspect the shingles, the flashing, and the ridge cap. Look for:
- Missing, cracked, or curled shingles
- Shingles with bare spots where granules have worn away
- Flashing that has lifted or separated from the chimney, valleys, or pipe penetrations
- Any debris on the roof (branches, built-up moss or lichen)

If you see anything concerning, call a roofing contractor for a professional inspection before winter. A small flashing repair in October costs $150 to $500. The same issue discovered after a winter of water intrusion costs many times that.

**Test and Inspect the Heating System**
Before cold weather arrives and you actually need the heat, turn the thermostat up and run the heating system for 15 to 20 minutes. Listen and smell:
- A burning smell for the first few minutes is usually normal (dust burning off the heat exchanger after months of disuse)
- A persistent burning smell, a sulfur smell (possible CO issue — call the HVAC company immediately), or a smell of natural gas (leave the house and call the gas company) are not normal
- Unusual sounds — banging, rattling, squealing — need investigation

Check that heat is coming from all vents and that the thermostat is operating correctly.

**Prepare the Fireplace for the Season**
If you use the fireplace:
- Ensure the chimney has been swept (see September)
- Check the damper — it should open and close freely and seal completely when closed (close it when the fireplace is not in use to prevent heat from escaping)
- Check the firebox for cracks in the firebrick or mortar
- Ensure you have a proper-fitting screen or glass doors
- Stock hardwood firewood — softwood (pine, fir) produces more creosote and should not be the primary fuel

---

### November

**November brings the first hard freezes in most of North America and the beginning of the holiday season.** The house transitions fully to heating mode, and the focus is on winterization and preparation.

**Drain and Shut Down Outdoor Water Features**
Fountain pumps, outdoor irrigation that was not winterized in September/October, outdoor ponds with submersible pumps — all of these need to be shut down and drained before hard freezes. Remove pump impellers and store them inside. Most pump manufacturers specify indoor storage for the winter.

**Final Leaf Cleanup**
Leaves left on the lawn over winter kill the grass underneath (light and air deprivation, plus disease). Do your final leaf cleanup in November and ensure the lawn goes into winter clean.

**Check Weatherstripping and Door Sweeps on Garage**
The garage is often a weak link in winter energy performance. Check the door sweeps at the bottom of the door between the garage and the house (this is also a fire-rated assembly — the sweep is part of the fire separation). Check the weatherstripping on the main overhead garage door.

**Stock Winter Supplies**
- Ice melt (calcium chloride for concrete, not rock salt which damages concrete and harms plants)
- Sand for traction
- Snow shovels
- Backup propane or kerosene heater for emergencies (with adequate ventilation)
- Extra water, canned food, and emergency supplies in case of prolonged outage

**Fire Extinguisher Inspection**
Check the fire extinguisher(s) in your home. Every home should have at minimum one 5-pound or 10-pound ABC dry chemical extinguisher. Check that the pressure gauge reads in the green zone, the safety pin is intact, and there is no visible damage. Fire extinguishers should be inspected professionally every 6 years and replaced or hydrostatically tested every 12 years per NFPA 10.

---

### December

**December is the holiday month — which means the house faces elevated fire risk from decorations, cooking, and increased electrical load.** It is also the time to close out the year's maintenance and plan for the next one.

**Holiday Safety Audit**
- Check all holiday light strings before hanging — discard any with cracked insulation, broken sockets, or missing bulbs. Do not exceed the manufacturer's maximum string connections (typically 3 strings end-to-end for standard strings).
- Use only UL-listed lights and decorations
- Never leave lit candles unattended
- Do not use extension cords as permanent wiring — they are for temporary use only
- Turn off decorative lights before going to bed or leaving the house

**Review the Year's Maintenance Log**
What did you do this year? What did you mean to do but did not? What problems emerged that need follow-up? December is the time to close out the year and plan the next one.

Make a list of:
- Maintenance items that were deferred and need to be scheduled early in the new year
- Projects that are planned for next spring or summer
- Systems that are aging and will need replacement planning soon
- Service providers who performed well (keep their information) and any who did not (note the issues)

**Update Your Home File**
Add all service records, receipts, warranty registrations, and maintenance notes to your home file. Note anything that was installed this year with the date and installer information.

**Check Sump Pump**
Pour a bucket of water into the sump pit and confirm the pump activates. Winter snowmelt can produce significant water volumes in basements and crawl spaces, and you want to know your sump pump is working before you need it.

**Plan the Next Year**
Look at the major capital items in your home. What is aging? What might need replacement in the next 3 to 5 years? Start researching and budgeting. A roof replacement or an HVAC replacement is much less stressful when you have saved for it over time than when it is an emergency.

## Part 4: The Master Maintenance Schedule at a Glance

Use this summary table to build your own calendar. Mark completed tasks and move uncompleted tasks forward.

### Weekly or As-Needed
- Run the garbage disposal with cold water for 30 seconds to prevent odors and buildup
- Test smoke and CO detectors if any alarm went off unexpectedly
- Check any known problem areas (slow drain, dripping faucet, etc.) for progress or worsening

### Monthly (Every Month)
- HVAC filter: check and replace if needed
- Check under all sinks for leaks or moisture
- Test smoke and CO detectors
- Walk the exterior perimeter (5-minute visual)

### Spring (March–May)
- Post-winter exterior inspection
- Schedule and complete spring HVAC (AC) tune-up
- Clean gutters (spring)
- Test and activate outdoor irrigation system
- Service lawn mower and outdoor power equipment
- Caulk exterior gaps and inspect weatherstripping
- Clean dryer vent duct
- Flush water heater sediment
- Inspect attic (insulation, ventilation, moisture)
- Check and clean window wells
- Inspect deck and patio for winter damage

### Summer (June–August)
- Begin running AC; check condensate drain
- Clean refrigerator condenser coils
- Check attic ventilation
- Check and clear all exhaust fan vents
- Outdoor hose bib check
- Pest inspection and preventive treatment
- Clean dryer lint trap after every load (ongoing)
- Prepare for severe weather (hurricane / tornado season)

### Fall (September–November)
- Schedule and complete fall HVAC (heating) tune-up
- Clean gutters (fall — primary)
- Chimney inspection and sweep
- Winterize irrigation system
- Disconnect and store garden hoses
- Inspect roof from ground
- Replace exterior weatherstripping and door sweeps
- Rodent prevention measures
- Final exterior painting and caulking (before 50°F)
- Stock winter supplies (ice melt, sand, shovels)

### Winter (December–February)
- Holiday safety audit
- Frozen pipe prevention
- Ice dam monitoring and prevention
- Attic inspection for moisture
- Heating system performance monitoring
- Fireplace and chimney safety checks
- Review and update home file
- Inspect sump pump operation

### Annually
- Water heater flush
- Dryer vent full cleaning (not just lint trap)
- HVAC professional service (twice — spring and fall)
- Chimney inspection and sweep (if used)
- Refrigerator coil cleaning
- GFCI outlet testing
- Smoke/CO detector testing and battery replacement
- Pest control preventive treatment
- Roof inspection (professional, every 2–3 years; visual from ground every year)
- Foundation inspection (visual walk, every year)
- Sump pump test

### Every 2–5 Years
- Professional roof inspection
- Septic system pumping (every 3–5 years depending on tank size and household size)
- Chimney video inspection (if you use the fireplace heavily)
- Recoat or reseal deck
- Reseal asphalt driveway
- Professional pest inspection (termite focus)

### Every 5–15 Years (Capital Planning)
- HVAC system (plan at 12–15 years)
- Water heater (plan at 8–12 years)
- Roof (plan at 15–25 years depending on material and condition)
- Appliances (variable)
- Windows (plan at 20+ years)
- Exterior paint (every 5–10 years)

## Part 5: Using Technology to Support Your Maintenance Routine

A maintenance calendar is only useful if you use it. Here are practical approaches to keeping on schedule.

**Phone calendar reminders.** The simplest system: create recurring events in your phone's calendar for each maintenance task. Monthly filter check on the first of every month. Spring HVAC service in March. Fall gutter cleaning in October. The reminder costs nothing and takes thirty seconds to set up.

**Home maintenance apps.** Several apps — BrightNest, Centriq, HomeZada, and others — can help you track maintenance schedules, store appliance manuals and warranty information, and manage your home file. These range from free to a modest annual subscription. Use one if it helps you stay organized; they are not necessary if you have a good system already.

**A physical binder.** Some homeowners prefer a physical three-ring binder with tabs for each system, where they store all maintenance records, receipts, and notes. This works extremely well and has the advantage of always being findable (unlike an app on a phone you lost or replaced).

**A shared note or spreadsheet.** If you have a household partner, keeping the maintenance log in a shared note (Apple Notes, Google Keep, Notion) or shared spreadsheet (Google Sheets) means both people have access and can contribute. This is particularly important for households where one person handles most of the maintenance — if something happens to that person, the other needs to know where to find everything.

The system does not matter. The consistency does.
