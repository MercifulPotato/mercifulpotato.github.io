Twenty installments ago, we began with the simplest possible question: why is Nepal poor? Not in the finger-pointing, blame-assigning sense, but in the analytical sense — what does the evidence actually show about the mechanisms that have kept a country of thirty million people, sitting astride rivers with enormous hydropower potential, surrounded by two of the world's three largest economies, with a diaspora that sends home billions of dollars a year, from achieving the kind of sustained development improvement that comparable countries have managed? The answer we arrived at, over the course of examining the legislation, the sector context, the international precedents, and the institutional landscape, is not a single cause but a cluster: the financing gap between the infrastructure Nepal needs and the capital it can currently mobilize; the governance gap between the institutional quality required to deploy public capital wisely and the institutional quality currently available; and the structural gap between the market conditions needed for private investment at scale and those that currently exist. The Alternative Development Finance Fund Act 2081 is an attempt to begin closing all three of these gaps simultaneously. Whether it succeeds is genuinely uncertain, genuinely consequential, and genuinely worth caring about.

This final installment does four things. It synthesizes the series into the clearest possible summary of what the Act actually does and why it matters. It offers an honest verdict on the law's design — where it is strong, where it is incomplete, and what work remains. It provides a practical guide for citizens, journalists, and civil society organizations who want to track the Fund's performance rather than simply trust that it is performing as intended. And it identifies the five questions whose answers will determine, over the next decade, whether the Alternative Development Finance Fund becomes a transformative institution or another entry in the long catalogue of promising initiatives that did not deliver.

**The Series in Summary**

We began by establishing Nepal's financing challenge in concrete terms. The country needs somewhere between 50 and 100 billion dollars of infrastructure investment over the next decade to reach middle-income status — roads that connect rural communities to markets, energy systems that power industry and reduce dependence on imported fuel, airports and urban infrastructure that support a services economy. The domestic banking system, constrained by short deposit maturities and limited capital, cannot provide this capital at the required scale, tenor, or cost. The government budget, under perpetual pressure, cannot allocate the capital needed without starving other essential services. International private capital has been reluctant to commit to Nepal in large quantities because the sovereign risk environment, governance concerns, and market infrastructure have not met its minimum requirements. The gap between what is needed and what has been available is structural, persistent, and growing.

We then examined Nepal's legislative process and the specific provisions of the Act. We understood how parliament functions, why a dedicated act rather than an executive order was the appropriate mechanism, and what the specific provisions of the legislation actually require — the governance structure chaired by the Finance Ministry secretary, the CEO selection process involving the Civil Service Commission, the board composition, the investment mandate covering energy, transport, airports, railways, and urban infrastructure, and the financial instruments authorized including equity investment, bonds, guarantees, blended finance, remittance mobilization, asset monetization, and fund-of-funds structures.

We explored the sectoral context — what Nepal's infrastructure gap actually looks like in numbers, why hydropower is the central opportunity, how public-private partnerships work and why they fail, what remittances mean for Nepal's economy and how a diaspora financing instrument could work, and why Nepal's capital markets are currently too shallow to support the financing volumes the Fund will need. We examined how the Fund's specific governance mechanisms — the independent CEO process, the Auditor General oversight, the international evaluation, the parliamentary review — are designed to prevent the capture and mismanagement that have plagued comparable institutions elsewhere.

And we examined the international evidence — what happened in Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Ethiopia, and Mongolia when comparable institutions were created, what worked, what failed, and why the same basic mistakes tend to recur regardless of how much precedent experience is available. We then laid out, unsparing, the specific risks in Nepal's context: political capture, debt sustainability concerns, environmental and social impact, the expertise gap in the Nepali public sector, and the difference between nominal compliance with oversight requirements and genuine accountability.

**The Verdict on the Law's Design**

Having read the legislation carefully, traced its provisions through their implementation implications, and compared it to the international evidence, the honest assessment is this: the Alternative Development Finance Fund Act is well-designed legislation for Nepal's context. It is better designed, on average, than comparable legislation in countries at similar development stages. The governance mechanisms are thoughtfully constructed and address the most common failure modes of comparable institutions. The financial instruments authorized are appropriate and comprehensive. The oversight framework is more robust than most developing-country infrastructure finance institution legislation provides.

The law is not perfect. Several specific aspects warrant attention as weaknesses or gaps.

The transparency provisions are adequate but not strong. The legislation requires annual reports and Auditor General oversight, but it does not specify the granularity of investment disclosure — whether the Fund must publish individual investment decisions with the analysis supporting them, or whether aggregate portfolio reporting is sufficient. International best practice for public development finance institutions increasingly requires project-level disclosure: the name of the project, the investment amount, the key financial and environmental analysis, and periodic performance updates. Without this level of disclosure, the accountability mechanisms in the legislation cannot function effectively because the parliament, the press, and civil society cannot evaluate what they cannot see. This is a gap that should be addressed in the Fund's implementation regulations, even if the Act itself does not require it.

The environmental and social provisions are thin. As discussed in earlier installments, the Act refers to applicable approvals and compliance with relevant laws, but does not adopt international-standard environmental and social performance requirements as binding conditions for Fund investments. Given Nepal's ecological sensitivity and the complexity of social impacts in mountain infrastructure, this is a significant omission that the Fund's management should address voluntarily through investment policy.

The staffing provisions, while better than standard civil service rules, may not be sufficient to attract and retain the talent the Fund requires. A fund with ambitious investment ambitions in blended finance, project finance, and capital market development needs people who have done those things in practice, not only people who have studied them. Whether the compensation framework, the professional culture, and the political environment will allow the Fund to build such a team is unknown.

The coordination mechanism with the Investment Board of Nepal — the entity responsible for facilitating large investment projects — is not addressed in the legislation. The Fund and the Board have complementary mandates that could be powerfully aligned or could produce friction, duplication, and confusion if their working relationship is not designed deliberately. This is an administrative gap that should be addressed early.

Despite these gaps, the overall verdict is positive. The legislation is a serious effort by a government that understood what it was trying to create and drew on substantial international expertise in designing it. Its quality reflects both the seriousness of the problem it is trying to address and the improving capacity of Nepal's legislative and executive institutions to engage with complex economic policy.

**A Citizen's Guide to Tracking the Fund**

Good law is only the beginning. The real work of holding a public institution accountable happens after the legislation is passed, through the sustained attention of people who care about outcomes rather than intentions. Here is a practical guide to what to watch, where to find information, and what questions to ask.

Watch the CEO appointment. It will likely happen within the first year of the Fund's establishment. The formal process should involve the Civil Service Commission, the Nepal Rastra Bank Governor, and the Infrastructure Bank CEO. Watch not just who is appointed but how the process was conducted. Who nominated candidates? Was a public advertisement issued? What criteria were specified and applied? The selected individual's profile — their experience in development finance, infrastructure investment, or capital markets; their lack of obvious political affiliation; their reputation in the professional community — is the earliest available signal of whether the governance framework is being implemented in the spirit intended.

Track investment approvals. As the Fund begins deploying capital, a public record of investment decisions — which projects received investment, in what amounts, with what rationale — should be available through annual reports and ideally through real-time disclosure mechanisms. If the Fund does not publish individual investment decisions, that absence of disclosure is itself a significant concern that warrants public attention. When investment decisions are published, compare the projects approved to the pipeline of projects awaiting development — are the selected projects the ones that independent analysis would identify as highest-value and most ready for investment, or do political connections of the project sponsors appear to correlate with selection?

Monitor the annual audit. The Auditor General's annual audit is a formal oversight mechanism that should be publicly available. The quality of the audit — its depth, its willingness to identify problems rather than simply verify procedural compliance, its follow-up on prior-year findings — is a meaningful indicator of whether oversight is functioning. An audit that raises no significant concerns about an active development finance institution is more likely evidence of inadequate auditing than of perfect institutional performance.

Engage with the triennial international evaluation. When the first international evaluation is commissioned, approximately three years after the Fund begins operations, public attention should focus on the selection of evaluators — whether they are genuinely independent organizations with relevant expertise or firms with prior relationships with the Nepali government — and on the findings and whether they are substantively engaged rather than simply acknowledged. The government's response to critical findings is especially revealing: dismissing concerns rather than addressing them is a red flag.

Follow the parliamentary review. The five-year parliamentary review required under the legislation should be a meaningful exercise in democratic accountability. Prepare questions in advance: What is the Fund's return on investment across its portfolio? How many projects have experienced cost overruns or delays, and how have those been managed? Has the Fund achieved its remittance mobilization targets? What are the environmental and social outcomes of its investments? Has the Fund operated within its authorized capital limits, and if not, what is the plan for fiscal sustainability?

**The Five Defining Questions**

The following five questions, whose answers will emerge over the next decade, will collectively determine whether the Alternative Development Finance Fund becomes a transformative institution in Nepal's economic history.

The first is the governance question: will the Fund's decision-making remain genuinely independent of political pressure, or will it become an instrument of patronage? This question does not have a clear answer at the time of the Fund's creation. It will be answered by the accumulation of decisions — appointment decisions, investment decisions, staffing decisions — over the first three to five years of operation. A Fund that by its fifth year has a track record of investment decisions that reflect technical and financial merit, an operating team with genuine expertise, and no pattern of politically connected project selection will have answered this question affirmatively. A Fund that by its fifth year has a pattern of investments in politically connected projects with below-expectation returns will have answered it negatively.

The second is the financial performance question: will the Fund's investments actually generate the returns needed to service its liabilities and remain financially sustainable? This will depend on the quality of project selection and monitoring, the trajectory of Nepal's hydropower and infrastructure sectors more broadly, and the macroeconomic environment. By ten years in, the first cohort of investments will be mature enough to evaluate. Projects that are generating operating revenue as projected will be evidence of financial performance. Projects that are delayed, impaired, or generating below-projected returns will require honest accounting of why and what will be done differently.

The third is the mobilization question: will the Fund actually succeed in mobilizing capital from the Nepali diaspora and from international development finance partners at anything approaching the scale its authorization contemplates? The theoretical potential — billions of dollars in remittance savings, co-investment from international development banks — is real. The practical mobilization of that potential requires marketing, distribution infrastructure, regulatory reform, and trust-building that will take years. If the Fund can demonstrate, by its fifth year of operation, meaningful diaspora bond issuances that have attracted thousands of investor accounts and hundreds of millions of rupees, that is a significant achievement. If the remittance mobilization mechanism remains theoretical while the Fund relies primarily on its initial government capitalization, the most innovative element of the legislation will have failed in implementation.

The fourth is the market development question: will the Fund's activity catalyze the development of Nepal's capital markets in ways that outlast the Fund itself? If the Fund's bond issuances help establish a functioning secondary market for long-duration securities, if the Fund's equity co-investments help create listed infrastructure companies that attract institutional investors to NEPSE, if the Fund's guarantee instruments build a market for project finance that commercial banks eventually enter on their own — then the Fund will have performed the market-development function that is potentially its most durable contribution. If the Fund operates as a standalone institution without catalytic effects on the broader market, its impact is limited to the projects it directly finances.

The fifth is the environmental and social question: will the infrastructure investments facilitated by the Fund leave Nepal's communities and ecosystems better or worse than they found them? This is not a question that can be answered by financial statements or audit reports. It requires ongoing monitoring of actual environmental outcomes, genuine engagement with affected communities, and honest accounting of both the benefits and the costs of development. A Fund that finances energy infrastructure that improves lives while implementing best practices for environmental management and community engagement will have demonstrated that infrastructure development and environmental responsibility are compatible. A Fund that leaves communities displaced without adequate compensation, rivers degraded, and ecosystems fragmented in the name of economic development will have imposed real costs that do not appear in its balance sheet.

**The Larger Context**

The Alternative Development Finance Fund exists in a Nepal that is simultaneously more capable and more fragile than it appears from outside. More capable because a generation of Nepalis educated in international institutions, experienced in international organizations, and deeply committed to their country's development has brought real expertise and real ambition to bear on Nepal's public problems. The people who drafted this legislation are serious professionals. More fragile because the political system that must implement it has chronic vulnerabilities — frequent government changes, patronage pressures, a bureaucracy not yet fully aligned with the meritocratic values that good governance requires — that create persistent headwinds against institutional quality.

Whether Nepal's infrastructure deficit is reduced materially in the coming decade will not depend on this legislation alone. It will depend on the broader political environment, on the trajectory of India-Nepal power trade negotiations, on the stability of the global capital market conditions that affect the cost of international borrowing, on monsoon patterns and seismic activity that affect both hydropower generation and infrastructure integrity. But it will also depend on whether the Alternative Development Finance Fund is built carefully, staffed with talent, governed with independence, and held accountable by citizens, journalists, and parliamentarians who maintain the expectation that public institutions must demonstrate performance rather than simply assert good intentions.

Nepal has been, in various forms and with various institutions, attempting to solve the infrastructure financing problem for decades. The Act 2081 is the most sophisticated and best-designed attempt in that history. It deserves to be taken seriously — which means both supporting its potential and scrutinizing its implementation with the rigor that any serious attempt at governance deserves.

The rivers still fall. The potential is still there. Whether this time is different depends on choices that will be made in the next few years by specific people in specific roles, with all the pressures and incentives and courage that entails. The vigil begins now.
