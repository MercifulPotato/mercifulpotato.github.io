For six posts we have mostly looked at the spending side of the budget — the total, and how it divides into running the state and building it. But every rupee a government spends, it must first get from somewhere, and where it gets its money is just as revealing as how it spends it. A government that raises most of its money from a broad, stable base of its own is on solid ground; a government that depends on a narrow, fragile, or borrowed source is building on sand, however impressive its spending plans. So we now turn the budget over and examine the other side: where the money comes from.

This is the first of two posts on that question. In this one we tackle the largest source by far — **revenue**, the money the government raises chiefly through taxes, targeted in this budget at **NPR 1,405.31 billion**, about **USD 9.37 billion**. We will learn what government revenue actually is, the crucial difference between tax and non-tax revenue, and the kinds of taxes Nepal relies on. Then we will uncover a fact that surprises almost everyone and that lies at the heart of Nepal's public finances: the degree to which the government's tax revenue depends, indirectly but profoundly, on the wages that millions of Nepalis working abroad send home. And finally we will examine the central tension of this budget's revenue plan — a government that is *cutting* tax rates while *promising to collect more* — and ask whether that promise is realistic or whether it is the kind of hopeful number that quietly unravels over the year.

## What is government revenue?

Start, as always, with the plain meaning. **Revenue** is the money a government takes in from its own ongoing sources — principally taxes, but also various fees and earnings — as distinct from money it *borrows* (which must be repaid) or money it *receives as a gift* (grants). The key feature of revenue is that it is the government's *own* income, raised from the economic activity of the country itself, not borrowed against the future and not dependent on the generosity of others. It is, in the household analogy we keep returning to, the family's *wages* — the money it actually earns — as opposed to money it borrows on a credit card or receives from a relative.

This matters enormously for judging a budget's health, because the share of spending that a government can cover from its own revenue, as opposed to from borrowing and grants, is a measure of how sustainable its finances are. A government that pays for nearly all its spending from revenue is living within the means its economy generates and could, in principle, continue indefinitely. A government that must borrow heavily to cover ordinary spending is, to that extent, living beyond its means and storing up obligations for the future. In this budget, revenue of NPR 1,405.31 billion is set against total spending of NPR 2,124.34 billion — meaning revenue covers only about two-thirds of what the government plans to spend, and the remaining third must come from grants and borrowing. We will examine that gap, the deficit, in the next post. For now, fix the headline: the government plans to *earn*, from its own sources, about NPR 1.4 trillion (USD 9.37 billion), and to spend half again as much.

## Tax revenue and non-tax revenue

Government revenue divides into two broad kinds, and the distinction is worth understanding because they behave very differently and because the overwhelming majority of Nepal's revenue comes from one of them.

**Tax revenue** is money raised through *taxes* — compulsory payments that citizens and businesses are legally required to make to the government, with no direct individual return in exchange. When you pay tax, you do not receive a specific good or service in return for that specific payment; you contribute to the общий pool from which all public spending is drawn. Taxes are compulsory and general, and they are by far the largest source of revenue for almost every government, Nepal included. The great bulk of Nepal's NPR 1.4 trillion revenue target is tax revenue.

**Non-tax revenue** is everything else the government earns that is not a tax: fees charged for specific government services (passport fees, licence fees, court fees, and the like); royalties and charges for the use of public resources; dividends and profits the government receives from the public enterprises and companies it owns; interest on money the government has lent; fines and penalties; and similar earnings. The defining feature is that non-tax revenue is typically tied to a specific service, asset, or activity, rather than being a general compulsory levy. Non-tax revenue is real and useful, but in Nepal it is a much smaller share of the total than tax revenue. So when we ask where Nepal's revenue comes from, the honest answer is: overwhelmingly from taxes. And that raises the next question — *which* taxes?

## The taxes Nepal relies on, and the import problem

Taxes come in several major kinds, and the *mix* a country relies on tells you a great deal about its economy and about how stable and how fair its revenue is. The major tax heads in Nepal are these, and we will meet most of them in much greater detail in the dedicated tax posts to come; here we need only the shape.

There is **income tax** — tax on the income of individuals and the profits of businesses. There is **value-added tax**, or VAT — a tax on consumption, levied on the sale of goods and services, which we will explain fully in a later post but which you can think of for now as a tax you pay, often without noticing, every time you buy something, built into the price. There is **customs duty** — tax charged on goods as they are *imported* into the country, at the border. And there is **excise duty** — a tax on the production or import of particular goods, often those a government wishes to discourage or to tax heavily, such as alcohol, tobacco, and vehicles. Together, these are the workhorses of Nepal's revenue.

Now here is the crucial structural fact about Nepal's tax mix, and it shapes everything that follows: a very large share of Nepal's tax revenue is tied, directly or indirectly, to *imports*. This is true in a way that is easy to miss. Customs duty, obviously, is charged on imports — so it rises and falls directly with the value of goods brought into the country. But the connection runs deeper than customs alone. A large portion of Nepal's VAT and excise is also collected *at the border, on imported goods*, because Nepal imports so much of what it consumes — fuel, vehicles, machinery, electronics, and a great range of finished products — and these imports are taxed as they enter. The result is that a strikingly large fraction of Nepal's entire tax revenue depends on the volume and value of imports flowing into the country. When imports are strong, the customs houses are busy and the revenue flows; when imports weaken, a large part of the government's tax base weakens with them. This is a fragile foundation for a treasury, and understanding *why* imports rise and fall in Nepal leads us to the most important and least appreciated fact in the country's public finances.

## The remittance engine

We met this fact briefly in Part 1, and now we give it the weight it deserves, because you cannot understand Nepal's revenue without it. For decades, one of Nepal's largest sources of foreign income has not been a product it sells abroad, but the *labour of its people* abroad. Millions of Nepalis work overseas — in India, in the Gulf states, in Malaysia, and increasingly further afield — and the money they send home to their families is called **remittance**. In recent years, remittances have amounted to something on the order of the equivalent of nine to eleven billion US dollars a year, a sum equal to roughly a quarter or more of Nepal's entire economy, and in some recent years approaching or even exceeding the entire revenue collected by the government. It is no exaggeration to say that the wages of Nepal's absent workers are one of the principal forces holding up the whole economy.

Now follow the chain that connects those distant wages to the government's tax revenue, because it is the key to everything. A Nepali family receives remittance money from a son or daughter working abroad. What does the family do with it? A great deal of it is *spent* — on food, clothing, housing, education, healthcare, vehicles, electronics, and the countless goods of daily life. And a large share of those goods is *imported*, because Nepal produces relatively little of what it consumes. So the remittance money, spent by families, flows into the purchase of imported goods — and those imported goods are *taxed* as they enter the country and as they are sold, through customs duty, VAT, and excise. In this way the wages earned in Dubai or Kuala Lumpur or Qatar end up, after passing through a Nepali family's spending, as tax revenue in the Nepali treasury. Remittances drive consumption; consumption drives imports; imports drive tax revenue. The chain is indirect, but it is enormous, and it means that the health of Nepal's government revenue is bound, through this chain, to the fortunes of its migrant workers and the economies they work in.

This dependence is a double-edged sword, and an honest analysis must hold both edges. On one edge, remittances have been a stabilising blessing: they have lifted millions of families out of poverty, financed education and healthcare, kept consumption and therefore revenue buoyant, and supplied the foreign currency the country needs to pay for its imports. Without remittances, Nepal's economy and its public finances would be far weaker. On the other edge, this dependence is a profound vulnerability. A revenue base that rests on remittance-fuelled imports is hostage to forces entirely outside Nepal's control: an economic slowdown in the Gulf or Malaysia, a fall in global oil prices that shrinks Gulf economies, a change in those countries' policies toward migrant labour, or any disruption to the flow of Nepalis abroad or the money they send back. Should remittances falter, the chain runs in reverse: families spend less, imports fall, and a large part of the government's tax revenue contracts — precisely the scenario that haunts Nepali finance ministers. And there is a deeper, longer-term worry: an economy and a treasury that depend on exporting labour and taxing the imports that labour's wages buy is not an economy that is building its own productive capacity at home. The remittance economy can sustain consumption and revenue for a long time, but it is not, in itself, development; it can even mask the absence of development by keeping the lights on with money earned elsewhere. So when you read this budget's confident revenue target, remember what a large part of it ultimately rests on: not on a thriving domestic productive economy, but on the wages of Nepalis working far from home, spent on goods made even farther away. That is the foundation, and it is both remarkably resilient and genuinely fragile.

## The central tension: cutting rates while raising the target

With that foundation understood, we can examine the puzzle at the heart of this budget's revenue plan, and it is a genuine puzzle that deserves hard scrutiny. This budget *cuts taxes* in several significant ways — we will detail them in the tax posts, but the headlines are a large reduction in personal income tax (doubling the income exempt from tax and cutting the top rate), a simplification and reduction of customs duties, and the abolition of excise on many goods. And yet, at the same time, the budget *targets higher revenue* than before: NPR 1,405.31 billion, an increase over the previous year. How can a government cut tax rates and simultaneously plan to collect more? This is not impossible, but it requires a story, and the soundness of that story is exactly what a careful citizen must examine.

There are several ways the government's revenue arithmetic could, in principle, hold together despite the rate cuts, and it is worth laying them out fairly before we apply skepticism. First, there is *economic growth*: if the economy grows — and the budget assumes a robust 7 percent — then even at lower rates, a larger economy generates more taxable activity, more imports, more sales, and more income, so revenue can rise even as rates fall. Second, there is *base broadening and formalisation*: this budget contains several measures aimed at pulling more economic activity into the tax net and improving collection — incentives for digital payments, a VAT-bill lottery to encourage people to demand receipts, and administrative reforms — and if these succeed in capturing activity that previously escaped tax, revenue can rise without rate increases. Third, there is the *stimulus argument*, sometimes made for tax cuts: that lower rates encourage more activity, more compliance, and more investment, partly offsetting the direct revenue loss. Each of these is a coherent mechanism, and a government cutting rates while targeting higher revenue is, in effect, betting on some combination of them.

Now the skepticism, which this series exists to supply. The trouble is that each of these mechanisms is *uncertain and optimistic*, and the revenue target depends on them all going right. The growth assumption of 7 percent is, as we have noted and will examine in detail, far above the 3.85 percent the economy actually managed last year; if growth disappoints, the larger-economy-generates-more-revenue mechanism weakens or fails. Base broadening and formalisation are genuinely worthwhile but notoriously slow and difficult; they rarely deliver large revenue gains quickly, and assuming they will fill the gap left by rate cuts within a single year is hopeful. And the stimulus argument, while not baseless, is frequently overstated; tax cuts usually do lose revenue, at least in the near term, the direct loss only partly offset by any boost to activity. So the honest assessment is that this budget's revenue target rests on a stack of optimistic assumptions, any of which could fall short, and that the combination of cutting rates and targeting higher revenue is a *bet* — a defensible bet, perhaps, with real upside if the formalisation and growth materialise, but a bet nonetheless, and one whose failure would widen the deficit and force either more borrowing or mid-year spending cuts. We are not saying the target is wrong. We are saying it is ambitious and contingent, and that a citizen should watch the actual revenue figures over the year against the target, because revenue shortfalls are one of the most common ways a confident budget quietly unravels.

## The realism check: revenue and the recent record

To judge whether a revenue target is realistic, the single most useful thing you can do is compare it to the recent record — not to the previous *target*, but to what was actually *collected*. This is the revenue version of the announced-versus-actual discipline we built in earlier posts, and it matters because governments have a persistent tendency to set revenue targets above what they then manage to collect.

In recent years, Nepal's revenue collection has frequently *fallen short* of the budget's targets. The reasons are familiar from the structural picture we have drawn: when imports have weakened — sometimes because of deliberate restrictions imposed to protect the country's foreign-currency reserves, sometimes because of a slowdown in the remittance-fuelled consumption that drives imports — the import-dependent tax base has contracted, and revenue has come in below the optimistic figure pencilled into the budget. A government that has missed its revenue target in recent years, and that is now cutting rates while assuming strong growth and successful formalisation, is setting a target that a skeptical observer should regard as a *hopeful ceiling* rather than a confident floor. This does not mean the target will certainly be missed; a genuine recovery in imports and a real success in formalisation could meet or even exceed it. But it does mean the prudent expectation is that revenue may well fall somewhat short, that the deficit may therefore be somewhat larger than planned, and that the gap will have to be covered by additional borrowing or by cutting spending partway through the year — which, given the execution patterns we have discussed, often means cutting the capital budget that was already too small. The revenue target is the optimistic promise; the actual collection, reported through the year by the revenue administration and the Financial Comptroller General Office, is the truth, and the two have a history of diverging.

## A worked example: how a slowdown abroad reaches the treasury

To make the fragility of the revenue base vivid, let us trace a single disruption from its origin abroad all the way to a hole in the Nepali budget, because seeing the chain in motion is the best way to understand why it matters.

Suppose that, partway through the fiscal year, the economies of the Gulf states slow — perhaps because the global price of oil falls sharply, shrinking the revenues of the oil-exporting states where so many Nepalis work. The consequences ripple outward along the chain we have described. Some Nepali workers abroad lose work, or see their wages stagnate, or find fewer opportunities to migrate in the first place. The flow of remittances back to Nepal slows. Nepali families, receiving less, spend less — they postpone buying a motorcycle, an appliance, building materials for a house. Because so much of what they would have bought is imported, *imports fall*. And because so much of Nepal's tax revenue — customs duty, plus the VAT and excise collected at the border on imported goods — depends on the volume and value of those imports, the government's revenue falls with them. A shock that began in the oil markets of the Gulf arrives, after passing through migrant wages, family spending, and the customs houses, as a shortfall in the Nepali treasury. The revenue target of NPR 1,405.31 billion, set in the optimistic calm of May, is missed; the deficit widens; and the government must scramble to borrow more or to cut spending — quite possibly the capital spending the country could least afford to lose.

Notice what this example reveals. Nepal's government revenue is exposed to risks that have nothing to do with Nepal's own policies or economy — risks in the price of oil, in the health of Gulf and Malaysian economies, in the politics of migrant labour in distant countries. A budget built on this base is, to a significant degree, a hostage to fortune abroad. This is not a flaw the current government created, and it is not one any single budget can fix; it is a deep structural feature of an economy that depends on exporting labour and taxing the imports that labour's wages buy. But it is a feature a citizen must understand, because it means that confident revenue targets rest on foundations the government does not control, and that the prudent reader treats those targets as contingent on a benign external environment that may or may not hold. The fragility is real even when nothing goes wrong; it is simply waiting for something to.

## For the technically minded: revenue composition and a projection

For readers who work with numbers, two exercises capture the analytical content of this post: seeing the revenue mix as structured data, and seeing how dependent the projection is on a single assumption.

First, the composition. The precise breakdown should be taken from the Ministry of Finance's revenue tables, but the *shape* is what matters, and it is import-heavy. Here is the structure in schematic form (the percentages are illustrative of the well-known shape of Nepal's tax mix and should be replaced with the official figures for any precise statement):

| Revenue source | Type | Linked to imports? | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Value-added tax (VAT) | Tax | Heavily (much collected at the border) | A consumption tax; large share of total |
| Customs duty | Tax | Directly | Charged on goods as they are imported |
| Excise duty | Tax | Substantially (much on imported goods) | On alcohol, tobacco, vehicles, etc. |
| Income tax (personal and corporate) | Tax | Less directly | Being cut for individuals in this budget |
| Non-tax revenue (fees, dividends, royalties) | Non-tax | No | Smaller share of the total |

The decisive observation is in the third column: most of the largest revenue sources are tied, directly or substantially, to imports, which is why the whole revenue base rises and falls with import volumes, which are in turn driven by remittance-fuelled consumption.

Second, a projection sketch showing how the revenue target hangs on the import and growth assumptions:

```text
# Revenue depends heavily on imports, which depend on remittance-driven consumption.
baseline_revenue        = 1405.31     # NPR billion, the TARGET
import_linked_share     = 0.55        # ~share of revenue tied to import volumes (illustrative; verify)
assumed_import_growth   = 0.10        # the budget implicitly assumes imports recover/grow

# Optimistic case: imports grow as assumed
import_linked_revenue   = baseline_revenue * import_linked_share
domestic_revenue        = baseline_revenue * (1 - import_linked_share)
optimistic = domestic_revenue + import_linked_revenue * (1 + assumed_import_growth)

# Pessimistic case: a slowdown abroad makes imports FALL instead
shock_import_change     = -0.10
pessimistic = domestic_revenue + import_linked_revenue * (1 + shock_import_change)

# The gap between optimistic and pessimistic is large, and it is driven almost entirely
# by one assumption Nepal does not control: the direction of imports.
# A revenue target is only as reliable as its weakest external assumption.
```

The lesson of the projection is the lesson of the whole post: Nepal's revenue target is not a fixed quantity the government can simply decide to collect; it is the output of assumptions — about growth, about imports, about formalisation — several of which depend on forces beyond Nepal's borders and beyond any budget's control. A reader who understands this does not ask merely "is the target big?" but "on what assumptions does the target rest, and how likely are they to hold?" — which is exactly the question the next post extends to the part of the budget the revenue cannot cover: the gap that must be borrowed.

## How budgets deceive: revenue optimism

The technique of deception for this post is one of the most consequential in all of budgeting, because it sits at the very foundation of the document: **revenue optimism** — the setting of a revenue target higher than is realistically likely, in order to make the rest of the budget add up more comfortably than it should.

Here is why the trick is so tempting and so common. A budget must, on paper, show how all its spending will be paid for. The more revenue a government assumes it will collect, the less it appears to need to borrow, and the smaller and more responsible its deficit looks. So there is a constant, structural incentive to pencil in an optimistic revenue figure — to assume strong growth, a robust recovery in imports, and great success in formalisation — because every extra rupee of *assumed* revenue is a rupee less of *apparent* deficit. The revenue target thus becomes a kind of pressure valve: when the spending plans are ambitious and the government does not wish to show an alarming deficit, the temptation is to relieve the pressure by assuming more revenue will arrive. The figure can be defended with genuine-sounding arguments — growth, formalisation, the stimulus effect of tax cuts — but the underlying function is often to make the arithmetic look better than the likely reality. And because the shortfall, if it comes, only becomes visible months later when actual collections fall short, the optimism pays its political dividend immediately (a respectable-looking deficit at budget time) while its costs (a widened deficit, more borrowing, or mid-year cuts) arrive too late and too quietly to spoil the launch.

The defence is to treat every revenue target as a *forecast to be interrogated*, not a fact to be accepted. Ask: what growth rate does this revenue assume, and is it in line with recent reality or far above it? What is assumed about imports, and does that assumption depend on forces outside the country's control? Does the government have a recent record of meeting its revenue targets, or of missing them? And — the sharpest question — does this budget cut tax rates while assuming higher revenue, and if so, exactly which optimistic mechanisms are being relied on to square that circle? A revenue target that assumes growth far above the recent record, a strong recovery in import-driven taxes, and rapid success in formalisation, all at once, and all while cutting rates, is a target stacked with optimism, and the prudent expectation is that it will fall at least somewhat short. The defence is not to assume the target is fraudulent — it may be met — but to hold it as contingent and to watch the actual collections against it, because the gap between optimistic revenue assumptions and disappointing revenue reality is one of the most reliable ways that confident budgets come undone, and it is the seed of the deficit and the borrowing we turn to next.

## Where we go from here

We have followed the money to its largest source. You now know what government revenue is, and why the share of spending it can cover is a measure of fiscal health; the difference between tax and non-tax revenue, and that Nepal depends overwhelmingly on taxes; the major taxes Nepal relies on, and the crucial fact that most of them are tied to imports; and the remittance engine that drives those imports and thus the revenue — a foundation that is at once remarkably resilient and genuinely fragile, exposed to forces far beyond Nepal's borders. You understand the central tension of this budget's revenue plan — cutting rates while targeting more — and the optimistic assumptions on which squaring that circle depends. And you have met the deception of revenue optimism, and the discipline of interrogating every target as a contingent forecast rather than a fact.
