# The Unfinished Tapestry: Tolkien's Revisions, Contradictions, Open Questions, and the Mythology That Never Stopped Growing

*This is Part Eight — the coda — of the comprehensive in-universe chronological history of Middle-earth. The previous seven articles told the story chronologically, from before creation to the Grey Havens. This final article does something different: it examines the mythology from the outside, as the product of a living mind that kept changing its views, that left many threads deliberately open, that contradicted itself in fascinating ways, and that produced a secondary world so internally rich that scholars have spent decades mapping its inconsistencies and Tolkien himself spent six decades unable to stop revising it.*

*This is not a catalogue of errors. It is an examination of what it means for a mythology to be alive — to grow and change and contradict itself the way that real traditions do, the way that all the great mythologies of the world contain internal contradictions because they were not designed by a single hand in a single sitting but accumulated through generations of telling and retelling. Tolkien was one man, not generations, but he worked for sixty years, and that time left marks.*

---

## Part 1: The Mythology That Grew in the Telling

When Tolkien began what would become Middle-earth's mythology, he was writing in a notebook in 1914, a young man of twenty-two at the University of Oxford. The earliest text is the Book of Lost Tales — a frame narrative in which a sailor named Eriol (later Ælfwine) visits the island of Tol Eressëa and hears the stories of the Elves from those who lived through them. This frame narrative was eventually abandoned, but the stories within it — the Music of the Ainur, the Fall of Gondolin (the very first to be written at length), the Tale of Tinúviel (Beren and Lúthien in their earliest form) — were the seeds from which everything else grew.

The key insight for understanding Tolkien's mythology is that it was never finished and was never intended to be finished in the sense of being sealed off from further revision. He described it in letters as a "mythology for England" — implying an ongoing, living tradition, not a completed monument. He kept returning to earlier parts of the mythology and revising them in light of what he had written later. He never stopped.

This created a body of texts that are internally inconsistent in ways that are sometimes trivial and sometimes deeply significant. Christopher Tolkien, editing the materials for publication, had to make countless decisions about which version of a story to use, how to reconcile contradictions, when to note variants and when to simply choose. The published Silmarillion (1977) represents Christopher's editorial synthesis, not any single Tolkien manuscript. The History of Middle-earth (twelve volumes, 1983–1996) publishes the drafts and revisions in detail, allowing readers to see how the stories changed.

What follows is a tour through the major areas where the mythology is unresolved, contradictory, or deliberately mysterious — and why each of these is interesting rather than merely frustrating.

## Part 2: The Origin of Orcs — The Problem That Never Had a Solution

The single most persistent unresolved problem in Tolkien's mythology is the origin of Orcs. The published Silmarillion states the theory clearly: Orcs were Elves, captured by Morgoth in the earliest ages before Oromë found the Elvish peoples, corrupted and twisted in Morgoth's pits until they were no longer recognizable. This is the dominant theory and it has strong textual support in multiple places.

But Tolkien himself was deeply troubled by this theory, and he never stopped troubling at it in unpublished notes. The problems are several, and they are genuine:

**The theological problem:** Elves are immortal. Their souls — their fëar — do not die when their bodies die. If Orcs were made from Elves, their fëar were Elvish fëar. Did those fëar go to Mandos when Orcs died? If so, what happened to them there? Were they eventually rehoused as Elves? If Morgoth's torture could not destroy the fëa, was every Orc secretly an imprisoned Elvish spirit, suffering in a corrupted body? This is deeply uncomfortable, and Tolkien felt its discomfort acutely.

**The free will problem:** If Orcs are corrupted Elves who have been so thoroughly remade that they cannot choose otherwise than evil — if the corruption is so complete that they genuinely cannot help being what they are — then condemning them for their evil seems unjust. But if they retain some form of free will, why do none of them ever choose differently? The books show occasional Orc cowardice and self-preservation, but never anything that could be called virtue.

**The breeding problem:** Corruption of Elves explains the first Orcs. But Orcs breed. They have populations, families (implied, never explicitly shown), generations. Do their descendants inherit the full corruption? How does corruption transmit genetically? Does a newborn Orc-child choose evil or is it born to it?

Tolkien's attempts to solve these problems in his late writings took several directions:

*Theory 1: Corrupted Elves* — the dominant published theory, as above. Problems as described.

*Theory 2: Corrupted Men* — a later theory, in which Morgoth used Men rather than Elves as his raw material, since Men were available earlier than the published timeline suggests and Men's mortality might explain why Orcs are mortal while Elves are not. This theory also has problems: Men did not appear until the First Age's beginning, but Orcs are present well before that.

*Theory 3: Corrupted Maiar* — in some very late notes, Tolkien considered whether the most powerful Orcs — the great Orc-captains, the Uruk-hai-like creatures — might be Maiar who had entered Orc-bodies, the way that Sauron and the Balrogs were Maiar in corrupted form. Ordinary Orcs might then be animals — beasts bred to vaguely human form — animated by the spirit of these Maia-captains in the same way that the Nazgûl animated their wraith-shapes. This would solve the theological problem entirely: no Elvish fëar in Orc-bodies, no Elvish souls trapped in corrupted flesh.

*Theory 4: Beasts given human form and malicious spirit* — Morgoth, unable to create life, gave animal minds a semblance of rational form through his own corrupting power. They were not Elves or Men but something made in mockery of them, without true fëar, animated by Morgoth's will. This would make them fundamentally different from the Children of Ilúvatar and their fate in death would simply be dissolution, not the halls of Mandos.

None of these theories is fully satisfactory, and none was formally adopted in the texts Tolkien prepared for publication. Christopher Tolkien, editing the Silmarillion, went with the corrupted-Elves theory as the best-supported in the available texts. It remains the canonical position. But it remains a problem.

The significance of this is not merely academic. The nature of the Orcs determines whether the War of the Ring — in which thousands of Orcs are killed — is morally simple or morally complex. If Orcs have Elvish souls, then every Orc death is, in some sense, a tragedy. If they are beasts without true souls, then killing them is no more tragic than killing wolves. Tolkien felt this deeply and never resolved it, which is why the mythology's most powerful villain is a being who corrupted others into servitude and the moral weight of that servitude is never quite distributed clearly.

## Part 3: Glorfindel — One Elf or Two?

In The Fellowship of the Ring, the Elf who rescues Frodo on the road to Rivendell and commands the flood of the Bruinen is named Glorfindel. He is clearly a being of great power — Strider/Aragorn is in awe of him, the Nazgûl flee from his presence, he has a quality of light about him that is different from ordinary Elvish grace.

In the Fall of Gondolin — whether in the Book of Lost Tales or the later accounts — one of the great lords of Gondolin who dies fighting a Balrog on the mountain pass of Cirith Thoronath is also named Glorfindel. He dies and takes the Balrog with him, and the Eagles carry him away.

For a long time, most readers assumed these were two different characters who happened to share the name. Tolkien himself apparently assumed this initially. But in later writings — specifically in some notes published in The Peoples of Middle-earth — he decided they were the same elf: the Glorfindel who died in the First Age was reembodied in Valinor, as the Elves of the Silmarillion are said to be eventually rehoused in new bodies when they die in Middle-earth. Then he was sent back to Middle-earth specifically because of his great deeds and his courage.

This makes Glorfindel the only named character who definitely dies in the First Age and is confirmed to return in the Third Age. It also explains why he is so extraordinarily powerful — he has been through the Halls of Mandos, been reembodied, been sent back with something of Valinor's blessing on him. He is not merely a great Elf of Middle-earth; he is a great Elf who has been through death and returned.

This identification also has a nice narrative circularity: Glorfindel the Gondolin-lord was saved by Thorondor's eagles when they carried him after his fight with the Balrog. Glorfindel in the Third Age summons the Eagles, in effect, when Gandalf calls for Gwaihir. The Eagles keep saving people.

Tolkien did not put this identification into The Lord of the Rings directly — it appears only in late notes. Christopher Tolkien has confirmed that his father intended the identification. It is now generally accepted as canonical even though it is not stated in any of the published narrative texts.

The variant: there are readers and scholars who prefer the two-Glorfindel reading on the grounds that making them the same character requires the reader to import information from external notes not in the texts themselves. This is a legitimate position. Tolkien's mythology is so large that readers necessarily choose which parts of it they treat as canonical.

## Part 4: Tom Bombadil — The Most Deliberately Mysterious Being

Tom Bombadil is, by design, the thing that cannot be explained within the mythology. He appears in The Fellowship of the Ring, rescues the Hobbits from the Old Man Willow and from the Barrow-wights, tells them he is the oldest, that he was here before the Dark Lord came from outside and before the Elves came and before the first raindrop fell. He cannot be defined or categorized by any of the existing categories of beings in the mythology. He is not a Vala (too cheerful, too particular, too focused on his small domain). He is not a Maia (the comparison doesn't quite work either). He is not a Man or an Elf or a Dwarf. He just is.

He is also immune to the Ring. When Frodo gives him the Ring to examine, Bombadil holds it up and it does not disappear him. He can see Frodo when Frodo is wearing it. The Ring has no power over him whatsoever. At the Council of Elrond, Gandalf addresses the question of whether the Ring could be given to Bombadil for safekeeping — and concludes no, not because Bombadil would be corrupted by it, but because he would lose interest in it. He would put it down somewhere and forget where he had put it, and then the enemy would find it.

Tolkien was asked about Bombadil many times and gave answers that are both illuminating and deliberately partial. In Letter 144, he describes Bombadil as representing something important: the spirit of nature that is not part of the moral conflict, that exists simply as the world exists, that is neither for nor against either side because it has not entered that story. He is older than the moral conflict, older than the Rings, older than Sauron, and the moral conflict simply does not touch him because he is not in it.

This is a theological position: that there is more to existence than the moral conflict, that the world contains things that are not evil or good in the relevant sense but simply are. The Old Forest, the willow-songs, the flowering primroses in March — these things will continue whether Sauron wins or loses. Tom Bombadil is the name of that persistence.

Tolkien also said, more than once, that he was not sure whether Bombadil was a mistake — whether including a being who was fundamentally outside the mythology's categories made the mythology less coherent. He decided to leave him in. The argument for leaving him in is exactly his inexplicability: a mythology that contains only things that can be categorized and explained is a poorer mythology than one that has at its edges something that cannot be.

Theories about what Bombadil actually is:

*A Maia who took a unique form* — possible, but the text's suggestion that he predates the Valar's entry into the world seems to contradict this.

*Ilúvatar's embodiment in the world* — a popular fan theory, not supported by any Tolkien text, but appealing in its suggestion that the creator is present in his creation in the most ordinary and most joyful form possible.

*A pre-Ainur spirit* — something that existed before the Ainulindalë, before the Music, a spirit of a different order entirely. This is perhaps the most consistent with what the texts say about his age, but it creates theological problems about what kinds of beings could exist before the Music.

*Simply a mystery, and meant to remain one* — the reading Tolkien himself seemed most comfortable with. The world has things in it that cannot be explained, and this is one of them.

## Part 5: The Blue Wizards — The Missing Istari

Two of the five Wizards — Alatar and Pallando (or, in Tolkien's later naming, Morinehtar and Rómestámo, meaning Dark-slayer and East-helper) — went into the east of Middle-earth in the Third Age and never returned to the northwest. In The Lord of the Rings and the published Silmarillion, they are essentially footnotes: present in the list of five, mentioned as having gone east, not heard from again.

This is a significant gap. Two of the five Maiar sent by the Valar to assist the peoples of Middle-earth are unaccounted for throughout the entire narrative. What did they do? Did they succeed or fail in their mission?

In a late letter (to Robert Murray, 1958), Tolkien speculated that they "had done more harm than good" — that they had established cults and secret traditions in the east that became corrupted, much as Saruman had become corrupted. This is a dark reading and it leaves the eastern peoples of Middle-earth (who are often treated as largely antagonistic in The Lord of the Rings itself) in an even more problematic position.

But in The Peoples of Middle-earth, Tolkien offered a different account, giving them the names Morinehtar and Rómestámo and suggesting they had gone to the east specifically to counteract Sauron's influence there — to provide aid to those peoples who were under Sauron's shadow, to encourage resistance, to preserve what could be preserved. He suggested they "had been very important" in keeping alive the resistance to Sauron in the east. This is a much more positive reading.

The two readings are not entirely contradictory: the Blue Wizards might have had mixed results, doing some good and some harm, without the narrative having a clean verdict either way. But the published texts don't give enough to form a judgment. They remain the most significant lacunae in the mythology of the Third Age.

Their absence from the War of the Ring raises a question: if they were still alive (and presumably they were — the Istari were Maiar in physical form, hard to kill), why did they not come? One answer is that their mission was specific to the east, that their role was to help peoples of the east and not to intervene in the northwestern war. Another is that they were already too absorbed in their own eastern affairs, for good or ill, to be mobilized. A third is that even Gandalf, who was the most effective of the five, barely managed to coordinate the strategy that defeated Sauron, and two additional Istari might not have helped as much as they would seem to from the outside.

## Part 6: The Palantíri — A Complete Accounting

The Seeing Stones are one of the most carefully detailed elements of The Lord of the Rings — Tolkien wrote an essay on them published in Unfinished Tales that provides far more information than the novels contain — and they deserve a full accounting because their role in the story's events is more extensive than many readers realise.

The palantíri were made by Fëanor in Valinor — another instance of his extraordinary craft, the making of objects that are not merely useful but represent a genuine extension of the possible. There were at least eight: seven were given to the Númenóreans as gifts from the Elves, and one remained in the Tower of Avallónë on Tol Eressëa, the Elven-island in the Bay of Eldamar, from which the others could in principle be contacted. After the Downfall, the seven that went to Middle-earth with the Faithful were distributed across Arnor and Gondor.

The seven palantíri of Middle-earth: the Stone of Minas Tirith (Anor-stone, held by Gondor, last used by Denethor to disastrous effect); the Stone of Orthanc (Isengard, taken by Saruman and used by him to communicate with Sauron, later recovered by Aragorn); the Stone of Minas Ithil (Ithil-stone, captured by Sauron when he seized Minas Morgul, used by him as the primary point of communication); the Stone of Annúminas (in Arnor, lost); the Stone of Elostirion (at the Tower Hills, not used for communication but set to look permanently west, visible only from the Elvenhome direction); the Stone of Amon Sûl (Weathertop, the greatest and most powerful of the northern stones, shattered when the tower was destroyed by Angmar, lost in the sea with Arvedui); and the Stone of Orthanc (counted above).

The use of the palantíri by Sauron to corrupt Denethor and to influence Saruman is one of the most elegant plots in the narrative: by controlling the Ithil-stone, Sauron controlled what anyone looking into any other stone could see when they attempted to contact Ithil. He could not lie outright — the palantíri showed what was actually there to see — but he could choose what portion of reality to reveal. He showed Denethor the overwhelming strength of Mordor; he did not show him the Ring-bearer in Mordor or the possibility that the Ring could be destroyed. He showed Saruman enough to convince him that resistance was futile, that alliance with Sauron was rational.

This use of the stones as instruments of selective truth-telling is one of Tolkien's most interesting treatments of the nature of propaganda and despair: the enemy does not need to lie. He only needs to control what you see of the truth. Denethor was not deceived about Mordor's strength; Mordor's strength was real. What he was deceived about was whether that strength was the end of the story.

Aragorn's use of the Orthanc stone — he reveals himself to Sauron, which terrifies Sauron because it confirms the line of Isildur is still alive and active — is also carefully thought through. Aragorn uses the Stone as Isildur's heir, claiming authority over it. What he shows Sauron is not false, but it is also not the full picture: it implies Aragorn has the Ring, or has access to it, which accelerates Sauron's decision to launch the attack on Gondor before his preparations are fully complete. This is, in effect, using the same selective-truth technique against Sauron that Sauron had been using against Denethor.

## Part 7: The Children of Húrin's Variants — Which Version Is Canon?

The story of Túrin Turambar exists in more versions than any other single narrative in Tolkien's mythology. The major versions, in rough order of composition:

*The Lay of the Children of Húrin* (early 1920s): An alliterative poem, unfinished, covering the story through Beleg's death. In this version, the character of Glómund (Glaurung's early name) is somewhat different, and the geography of the story is less fixed than in later versions.

*The prose version in the Book of Lost Tales* (1919-1920): A shorter prose account, the earliest prose telling.

*The Sketch of the Mythology* (1926): A brief summary, important as a document of Tolkien organizing his mythology into a coherent shape.

*The Quenta Noldorinwa* and *Quenta Silmarillion* (1930s): Increasingly full prose versions incorporating the story into the larger mythology.

*The Narn i Hîn Húrin* (late 1950s, published in Unfinished Tales): The longest and fullest prose version, written contemporaneously with The Lord of the Rings revisions, and the one that Christopher Tolkien used as the primary basis for the 2007 Children of Húrin book.

*The Grey Annals* (late 1950s, published in War of the Jewels): Annalistic entries covering the same events with different emphasis.

Key variants between versions:

**Beleg's sword:** In some versions, the sword given to Beleg is Anglachel/Gurthang. In others, its name and origin differ. The Eöl connection — that Anglachel and its twin Anguirel were made from a meteorite by the Dark Elf Eöl — is a relatively late addition to the mythology and is not present in the earliest versions.

**Nienor's encounter with Glaurung:** The exact nature of the spell Glaurung casts on Nienor changes across versions. In early versions, it is simpler — a memory-wipe. In later versions, it is more complex, involving false visions.

**The death of Mîm:** In some versions, Mîm is killed at Nargothrond by Húrin. In others, the encounter happens differently. The 2007 Children of Húrin synthesizes the versions, but readers of Unfinished Tales will notice differences from the condensed Silmarillion account.

**Túrin's parentage of Nienor's child:** In the earliest versions, Tolkien was vaguer about whether Nienor was pregnant when she died. Later versions make this explicit, which deepens the tragedy further.

The 2007 Children of Húrin represents Christopher Tolkien's best scholarly judgment about what his father's "final" version of the story would have looked like. It is not any manuscript that Tolkien wrote; it is an editorial construction. This does not make it unreliable — Christopher was the world's leading authority on his father's manuscripts — but it means that readers should understand it as an interpretation rather than a transcription.

## Part 8: The Shape of the World — Flat Earth to Round Earth

One of the most significant cosmological revisions in Tolkien's mythology is the change from a flat earth to a round earth. In the earliest versions of the mythology, the world was genuinely flat — a disc floating in an outer sea, with Valinor literally reachable by sailing west. In the published Silmarillion, the world is made spherical at the Downfall of Númenor, when Valinor is removed from the circles of the world.

But in very late writings — in The Nature of Middle-earth, published posthumously in 2021 — Tolkien was reconsidering even this. He began to wonder whether the world might always have been round, whether the mythology's earlier flat-earth cosmology was a theological error by the peoples of Middle-earth rather than the actual cosmological truth. He started working toward a version in which the world's spherical nature was always real and the "flat earth" of the earlier mythology was how it appeared to its inhabitants before they had the perspective to understand its actual shape.

This revision was never completed, and it would have required changes to the Akallabêth (the account of the Downfall of Númenor) that Tolkien did not live to make. Christopher Tolkien, editing the Silmarillion in the 1970s, used the flat-earth cosmology that was consistent across the most complete texts his father had prepared. The round-earth revision remains a tantalizing what-if.

The implications of a round-earth cosmology throughout the mythology's history would be significant. The straight road to Valinor would need to be reconceived. The nature of the Doors of Night and the Walls of the World would need revision. The cosmological position of Arda within Eä would be different. It is one of those cases where Tolkien's late mind was working on a problem that his remaining time was insufficient to solve.

## Part 9: Galadriel's History — The Most-Revised Character

Galadriel has the most complicated publication history of any character in the mythology, and the versions of her story contradict each other in significant ways.

In the published Silmarillion (1977), Galadriel is mentioned briefly as the only female of the Noldorin princes and is included in the rebellion of the Noldor, presumably sharing their exile. Her entry into Middle-earth and her movements during the First Age are not detailed.

In Unfinished Tales (1980), the essay "The History of Galadriel and Celeborn" presents a much more detailed account — and one that conflicted significantly with what Christopher Tolkien had already published in the Silmarillion. Key points:

*Her role in Eregion:* In some versions, Galadriel founded Eregion. In others, Celebrimbor founded it and she was present but not the founder. In still others, she and Celeborn left Eregion before Annatar (Sauron) arrived, which would explain why she was not seduced as Celebrimbor was. In other versions, she was there and rejected Annatar's overtures.

*Her ban from Valinor:* The nature of Galadriel's exile is different in different accounts. In some, she was refused permission to return after the War of Wrath because of her role in the Noldorin rebellion, making her return at the end of the Third Age a reward for her later service. In others, she refused to return voluntarily, out of pride, and earned pardon only by refusing the Ring when it was offered to her. In still others, the two accounts are combined in different proportions.

*Her powers and their source:* Tolkien at different times attributed Galadriel's extraordinary power to her Vanyarin blood (her mother Eärwen was of the Vanyar, the most gifted of the Elvish kindreds in terms of their closeness to the Valar), to her special training by Melian in Doriath, to her ring Nenya, and to some combination of all three.

*Her founding of Lothlórien:* The date and circumstances of Galadriel and Celeborn's establishment in Lothlórien change across versions.

Christopher Tolkien has been explicit in his editorial notes that he made mistakes in the published Silmarillion regarding Galadriel — that some of what he published does not represent his father's final thinking. The essay in Unfinished Tales represents a later and more developed view, but is itself unfinished and inconsistent internally.

For readers of the published texts, the safest position is that Galadriel is one of the greatest Elves who ever lived, was in Middle-earth from the First Age, refused the Ring, and sailed west at the Third Age's end — and that the precise details of her First Age biography remain genuinely uncertain.

## Part 10: The Two Trees and the Sun — A Cosmological Problem

In the published Silmarillion, the Two Trees of Valinor — Telperion and Laurelin — give light to the world until they are destroyed by Melkor and Ungoliant. After their destruction, Yavanna makes the Moon from Telperion's last flower and the Sun from Laurelin's last fruit. The Sun and Moon are described as lesser things than the Trees — copies, reflections, not the originals.

This creates a cosmological problem: if the Sun was made at the end of the First Age's early period, what gave light to the world before it? The Trees gave light only to Valinor, not to Middle-earth. Middle-earth before the Trees' making was lit only by the stars that Varda had made.

Tolkien was aware of this problem, and in very early versions of the mythology, the Trees actually did give light to all the world directly. Later, he confined their light to Valinor, which created the starlit-world-before-Sun setting of the awakening of the Elves that became so thematically important (the Elves' love of stars, their name Eldar meaning People of the Stars, and so on).

But the scientific problem of a world with no star illuminating it remains. Tolkien was a sophisticated enough thinker to know that a planet with no sun would be not merely dark but frozen, since solar radiation is what warms the Earth. His mythology's physics are not consistent with what we know about planetary science — but then, Arda was specifically not formed by natural processes. It was shaped by the Valar from material that Ilúvatar made, which means the physical laws governing it may simply be different from ours.

In some late notes, Tolkien considered whether the original physical form of Arda — the world as made before the Lamps were destroyed — was self-luminous, inherently bright from its own nature, only becoming dark when the Lamps fell. This would explain how life grew during the Spring of Arda without solar radiation. But this speculation was never developed into a full cosmological account.

The Ainulindalë itself, as published, has Ilúvatar speaking "Eä! Let these things Be!" and the universe coming into existence — which means the universe, including all physical laws, was created by an intentional act. The laws of physics in Tolkien's world are what Ilúvatar willed them to be, which gives Tolkien considerable latitude in departing from our physics when his narrative requires it.

## Part 11: The Fate of the Dwarves — Aulë's Children and the Afterlife

The Dwarves occupy a uniquely uncertain theological position in Tolkien's mythology. They were made by Aulë, not by Ilúvatar, and were given true life by Ilúvatar only when Ilúvatar saw Aulë's love for them and chose to animate them. The question of what happens to Dwarves after death is given an answer in the Silmarillion — one that differs significantly from both Elvish and Mannish fate.

According to Dwarvish tradition (and Tolkien does not completely endorse this tradition as literal truth, framing it as what the Dwarves believe), Aulë will gather the souls of dead Dwarves and hold them separately in his keeping. At the Last Battle — the Dagor Dagorath, the final war at the end of the world — the Dwarves will be resurrected and work with Aulë in the remaking of Arda that Tolkien associates with the world's renewal. They will be present at the end and at the making of the new beginning.

Whether this is literally what happens or is simply the mythology the Dwarves tell about themselves is left open in the published texts. The Valar do not confirm it; neither do they deny it. Elves are deeply uncertain about Dwarvish fate — they call the Dwarves Naugrim, the Stunted People, and say that the Dwarves "go back to the stone" when they die, meaning perhaps that their fëar return to the earth from which Aulë made their bodies.

The Seven Fathers of the Dwarves — the seven original Dwarves made by Aulë — were given a particular kind of immortality: they are said to be reincarnated, reborn repeatedly, each in a new body but retaining the same spirit. This is different from either Elvish reembodiment (which requires passing through Mandos and a long wait) or from Mannish mortality. Durin himself — Durin the Deathless, the eldest of the Seven Fathers — is the most famous example: Durin "reappears" at intervals throughout Dwarvish history, each time apparently a new individual but understood by the Dwarves as the same great soul born again. This is why the Kings of Moria bear the name Durin — not as a title of office but as an identity claim: each Durin is held to be literally Durin reborn.

## Part 12: Sauron's Repentance — Was It Real?

After the War of Wrath, when Morgoth was expelled and Eönwë summoned all who had served Morgoth to face judgment, Sauron came and appeared to submit. The Silmarillion's account says he "repented" in fear — that his submission was driven by fear of punishment rather than genuine contrition. He was told he would have to return to Valinor for formal judgment by the Valar, and he refused and fled.

But in some later writings — particularly in essays in Morgoth's Ring (History of Middle-earth Vol. 10) — Tolkien explored a darker and more complex possibility: that Sauron's repentance at the War of Wrath's end was, at least partially, genuine. That some part of Sauron did regret what he had become and did want to be different. And that it was the requirement to go to Valinor and submit to formal judgment — the humiliation of it, the exposure of what he had done under Morgoth's service — that he could not bear. His pride was intact even when his regret was real. He could not accept judgment. He turned back.

If this reading is correct, Sauron's subsequent career — the rebuilding in Mordor, the forging of the Ring, the destruction of Númenor — was the result of a being who had had a genuine chance at redemption and could not take it because he could not be humble enough to accept the terms. This makes him more tragic and less simply evil than he would otherwise be. It also makes him more like Melkor than he would want to admit: both were corrupted partly by pride, both had their evil begin in the refusal to submit to something larger than themselves.

This reading is supported by the pattern of Sauron's Second Age career. In his approach to the Elvish smiths of Eregion, he came as Annatar — not as a conqueror, not as a tyrant, but as a teacher and a sharer of knowledge. His desire to give the Elves gifts was, at some level, genuine: he really did want to help them preserve and beautify their realm. The corruption was that his help came with control built into its foundation — but the desire to help was not entirely false.

Tolkien in his letters described Sauron's earliest motivations as a desire for order — for the bringing of all things under the governance of a wise plan, for the elimination of the waste and chaos that he saw in a world where free will operated without coordination. This desire for order was itself not evil at its root; it became evil when the order became his order, when the plan became his plan, when the coordination became subjugation.

## Part 13: The Númenórean Kings — Longevity, Decline, and the Numbers

The lifespans of the Númenórean kings are given in the Appendices to The Lord of the Rings, and they show a striking pattern: the early kings lived hundreds of years, while the later kings lived progressively less. Elros lived five hundred and ten years. The kings of the middle period lived three to four hundred. The kings of the corrupt later period — when the Númenóreans were most obsessed with avoiding death — lived the shortest lives.

This is not accidental. Tolkien was making a specific point: the longing for immortality, when it becomes consuming, does not extend life but shortens it. The Númenóreans who accepted their mortality gracefully and devoted their long lives to good work lived the longest. The Númenóreans who spent their lives resisting death and seeking extension of it died sooner. The obsession with the very thing you fear most brings the fear upon you.

This pattern has been noted by commentators as a version of the Augustinian observation about desire: that the possession of what you have been pursuing often destroys the thing you were pursuing. The Númenóreans who feared death became in their fear the kind of people who were worth less alive — people who could not enjoy what they had because they were always looking at what was being taken from them.

The numbers themselves are also interesting as evidence of Tolkien's consistency. He worked out the full genealogy of the Númenórean kings and their lifespans and kept them consistent across revisions. This kind of systematic internal consistency is one of the marks of how seriously Tolkien took his secondary world — he checked the arithmetic, he made sure the generations added up, he kept records.

## Part 14: Shelob's Nature — Spider, Demon, or Something Else?

The Silmarillion describes Shelob as "the last child of Ungoliant to trouble the unhappy world." This is a very specific claim. Ungoliant was not an ordinary creature — she was a being of darkness that consumed light, of uncertain origin and extraordinary power. The question of whether she was an Ainur — a fallen Maia of some kind — or something older and stranger is left open in the texts.

If Ungoliant was a spirit of some kind, and Shelob is her "child," then what is Shelob? Is she also a spirit, incarnated in giant spider-form? Is she a physical creature descended from a spirit in the way that ordinary animals could be descended from Maiar who chose particular forms? Is the claim "child of Ungoliant" metaphorical — meaning of Ungoliant's kin or following — rather than literal?

Tolkien's published texts do not answer this. What they show through narrative is that Shelob is ancient beyond any physical spider's lifespan, that she has enough will and intelligence to choose and to plan (she and Gollum had a relationship of mutual use that required negotiation of a kind), and that she is not simply an animal driven by instinct. Whether she has a fëa in the Elvish sense — a spirit that would survive her body's death — is not addressed.

The question matters because Sam's attack on Shelob is one of the narrative's moments of greatest heroism, and the heroism is different depending on what Shelob is. If she is effectively an immortal spirit in spider-form, Sam's driving her back is an act of courage comparable to Gandalf facing the Balrog. If she is a very ancient but purely physical creature, the courage is of a different, more ordinary kind — still extraordinary, but not quite in the same register.

Given the care with which Tolkien establishes Shelob's antiquity — she is described as having been there longer than Sauron occupied Mordor, having predated his arrival — the most consistent reading is that she is something more than a physical animal, some spirit or remnant of an ancient spirit that had taken permanent spider-form in the First Age or even before.

## Part 15: The Dagor Dagorath — The Last Battle

At the very end of the Silmarillion's cosmological section, there is a fragment of prophecy about the end of the world: the Dagor Dagorath, the Battle of All Battles, in which Morgoth will return from the Void, the world will be shaken to its foundations, and a final conflict will be fought. The prophecy — called the Prophecy of Mandos — includes the statement that Túrin Turambar will be present and will kill Morgoth with Gurthang.

This prophecy is in the very earliest versions of the mythology. It was not developed in later texts, and Christopher Tolkien has indicated that his father came to question whether it fit the mythology as it had evolved — whether the concept of Morgoth returning and being killed by a resurrected Túrin was consistent with the theological framework Tolkien had developed by the time of his late writings.

In the late cosmological writings published in Morgoth's Ring, Tolkien developed the idea of Arda Marred and Arda Healed — the concept that the world as it exists is permanently damaged by Morgoth's original discord in the Ainulindalë, and that the eventual healing of the world is something that Ilúvatar has prepared from the beginning. The question of whether this healing requires a final battle is not clearly answered. Some versions suggest that the void into which Morgoth was cast is not simply imprisonment but a form of dissolution — that he has already been reduced to a shadow that cannot truly return. Others maintain the older idea of his eventual return and destruction.

The Tolkien Estate and Christopher Tolkien have generally treated the Dagor Dagorath prophecy as part of the mythology's early phase that was superseded by later thinking, without being formally replaced. It remains in the published Silmarillion as the last words of the "Of the Rings of Power and the Third Age" section, a tantalizing incomplete prophecy that Tolkien never fully integrated into the finished framework.

## Part 16: Languages as Archaeology — What the Elvish Tongues Reveal

Tolkien's Elvish languages are not decorative features of the mythology; they are a record of the mythology's development that in some cases contradicts the narrative texts. Understanding the linguistic archaeology helps explain many of the mythology's apparent inconsistencies.

Tolkien began constructing Elvish languages before he began constructing the mythology. Quenya and Sindarin (then called Qenya and Goldogrin or Gnomish) existed as linguistic projects before Tolkien had a full story to attach them to. As the mythology developed, the languages evolved, and sometimes the narrative adapted to the languages rather than the other way around.

For example: the distinction between Quenya and Sindarin — between the High Elvish of Valinor and the Grey Elvish of Middle-earth — is partly motivated by Tolkien's linguistic interest in having two related but distinct Elvish languages that had diverged over a long separation. The mythology then explains that separation: the Noldor went to Valinor and developed Quenya there, while the Sindar stayed in Middle-earth and developed Sindarin from the common Elvish root. The narrative is shaped by the linguistic project.

The word "Elvish" itself — in the sense of Tolkien's constructed languages — went through multiple phases:

*Phase 1 (1910s-1920s):* Qenya (influenced by Finnish) and Gnomish/Goldogrin (influenced by Welsh). These are the languages of the Book of Lost Tales.

*Phase 2 (1930s-1940s):* Noldorin (which would become Sindarin) and a more developed Quenya. The mythology of the Great Journey and the splitting of the languages becomes more fixed.

*Phase 3 (1950s):* The languages as they appear in The Lord of the Rings — mature Quenya and Sindarin. Tolkien retconned the old Noldorin as Sindarin and made the linguistic history consistent with the revised mythology.

The Elvish inscriptions in The Lord of the Rings — the Ring inscription, the Doors of Durin, the fragments of poetry — are in these mature languages. Earlier texts sometimes have different Elvish names or words for the same things, reflecting the earlier phases of language development.

## Part 17: The Men of Middle-earth — The Easterlings and Haradrim

The peoples who fight on Sauron's side in the War of the Ring — Easterlings, Haradrim, Variags of Khand — are thinly characterized in the published texts, and this has been a point of criticism of Tolkien's mythology: that the peoples of the east and south are largely presented as antagonists without individual characterization, without history, without the kind of complex motivation that the peoples of the northwest receive.

Tolkien was aware of this and addressed it briefly in a late essay (published in The Peoples of Middle-earth) in which he noted that these peoples were not inherently evil — they were Men who had been under Sauron's influence for long ages, who had been deceived or coerced into his service, and that among them were those who were "loyal to Sauron against their will" and those who had genuine traditions of their own that Sauron had corrupted.

Sam's thought at the Battle of the Pelennor Fields — looking at a dead Haradrim warrior who was young and far from home, and wondering what his name was and whether he had wanted to come — is Tolkien's most direct acknowledgement in the published texts that the enemy's soldiers are people too, not merely obstacles. It is a small moment but a significant one.

The mythology as published does not give us the history of the east and south in the depth it gives the northwest. This is partly a narrative choice — the story is focused on the northwest — and partly a consequence of Tolkien's method of building the mythology outward from a specific cultural standpoint (Anglo-Saxon England, northern European tradition). The gaps are real and acknowledged.

Some of Tolkien's post-publication notes, and the writings published in The Peoples of Middle-earth and Unfinished Tales, suggest that he was aware of these peoples as having genuine histories — that the Wainriders and the Balchoth and the various Easterling confederacies had their own internal politics and their own reasons for the choices they made. But he did not live to write those histories in the detail he gave to Beleriand.

## Part 18: The Question of Death — What Happens to Men

Perhaps the deepest unresolved question in all of Tolkien's mythology is what happens to Men after death. The mythology tells us explicitly:

- The fëar (spirits/souls) of Elves who die go to the Halls of Mandos, where they wait and eventually are rehoused in new Elvish bodies or dwell in Valinor until the world's end.
- The spirits of Dwarves (by their own tradition) are held by Aulë until the Last Battle.
- The fate of Men is unknown — even to the Valar. Men die and their spirits leave the world entirely, passing "beyond the circles of the world." Where they go, the Valar cannot follow.

This is the Gift of Men — the freedom from the world. The Elves are bound to the world until its ending; they will be there when it ends and will participate in whatever comes after. Men are not. They pass beyond, to something or somewhere that Tolkien consistently refuses to describe.

Why does he refuse? Because to describe it would be to claim knowledge of the afterlife, and that claim would be theological overreach even within a mythology. He said in letters that this was deliberately kept mysterious: "The doom (or gift) of Men is mortality, freedom from the circles of the world. What this means for each individual, I do not know." The mythology does not answer the question because the question cannot be answered from within the world. It requires the perspective of Ilúvatar, and that perspective is not given to the characters or, through them, to the reader.

What the mythology does suggest is that the Gift of Men is connected to the mystery of Ilúvatar's larger purpose — that Men's freedom from the world is the freedom for something that will be greater than the world. The Elves, bound to the world, will help make it beautiful and will be part of its final healing. Men, free of the world, will be part of what comes after the healing. What that is, Tolkien does not say. Perhaps he did not know.

## Part 19: The Music and the Free Will — The Ultimate Theological Question

We return, at the end of this examination, to where the mythology begins: the Ainulindalë and the problem of Melkor's rebellion. Ilúvatar says, when Melkor's theme has been incorporated into the Music, that Melkor's rebellion has added something — has made the Music richer, more complex, more profound. That even the evil that Melkor introduced was not outside the Music but was taken up into it and turned.

This raises the hardest question in Tolkien's theology: if Ilúvatar was always going to take up Melkor's themes and turn them, if the suffering and evil of Middle-earth was always going to be incorporated into something greater, then was Melkor's rebellion actually evil? Did it actually cause real harm? Or was it simply a necessary step in a larger plan, in which case "evil" is a mischaracterization of what Melkor did?

Tolkien wrestled with this in his letters and essays. His answers are careful and nuanced:

First, the fact that Ilúvatar can take Melkor's themes and use them does not mean that the themes were good, or that Melkor's choice was good. A surgeon can take a wound and heal it; this does not mean the wound was not real. The suffering in Middle-earth — the Elves enslaved to become Orcs, Túrin's doomed life, the drowning of Númenor, the deaths at the Battle of the Pelennor Fields — was real. Ilúvatar's ability to turn evil to good does not retroactively un-real the evil.

Second, Melkor had genuine freedom. He was not following a script. He chose his rebellion freely, and that freedom makes him genuinely responsible for the evil he chose.

Third, the fact that Ilúvatar can turn evil to account does not mean that He caused the evil or endorsed it. It means that His creative power is sufficient to incorporate even evil into the larger pattern — which is a statement about Ilúvatar's power, not a statement about evil's acceptability.

This is the framework within which the mythology's ethics operate. Evil is real. Suffering is real. The free choices that produce evil are genuinely free. And the larger pattern of the world is such that these real evils are incorporated into something that is, at the scale of Ilúvatar's vision, greater than they are. This does not make the evil comfortable or the suffering acceptable in any human sense. It makes it bearable — just barely bearable — in the same way that Tolkien's fiction consistently makes the darkest things bearable: by showing that they are not the end of the story.

The story is longer than any age. It is longer than the Third Age, longer than the First. It is still going.

## Part 20: A Primer on the Key Texts — Where to Go from Here

For readers who want to go deeper into the mythology after this series, here is a guide to the primary texts in recommended reading order:

**Start here:**
- *The Hobbit* — the entry point, the most accessible, and more thematically important than it looks.
- *The Lord of the Rings* — the main event, in three parts plus Appendices (the Appendices alone contain enormous amounts of history and genealogy not in the narrative).

**The mythological foundation:**
- *The Silmarillion* — the creation myth, the First and Second Ages. Dense but essential. The chapter "Of the Rings of Power and the Third Age" is the best single-chapter summary of the Second Age and provides the context for The Lord of the Rings.

**The deeper cuts:**
- *Unfinished Tales of Númenor and Middle-earth* — contains fuller versions of key stories (Tuor and Gondolin, the history of Galadriel and Celeborn, the quest of Erebor from Gandalf's perspective, the palantíri essay). Accessible to readers who've finished The Lord of the Rings and the Silmarillion.
- *The Children of Húrin* — the standalone Túrin narrative. Dark, complete, essential if you want to understand the First Age.
- *Beren and Lúthien* — the evolution of the central love story from earliest drafts to the final published version. A literary as well as mythological document.
- *The Fall of Gondolin* — the same treatment for Gondolin's story.

**The scholarly deep dive:**
- *The History of Middle-earth* (12 volumes, edited by Christopher Tolkien) — for serious students of the mythology. Volumes 1-2 (Book of Lost Tales) for the earliest versions; Volume 9 (Sauron Defeated) for the drafts of The Lord of the Rings's ending; Volumes 10-11 (Morgoth's Ring, War of the Jewels) for the late cosmological essays.
- *The Nature of Middle-earth* (edited by Carl F. Hostetter, 2021) — the most recent volume of posthumous Tolkien, containing late essays on cosmology, Elvish rebirth, the nature of time, and many other topics. The most recent window into Tolkien's late thinking.
- *The Letters of J.R.R. Tolkien* — Tolkien's own explanations of his mythology, his intentions, his faith, and his craft. Letters 131, 144, 181, 211, and 246 are the most important for understanding the mythology's meaning.

**Critical scholarship:**
- Tom Shippey, *The Road to Middle-earth* and *J.R.R. Tolkien: Author of the Century* — the essential critical works.
- Verlyn Flieger, *Splintered Light: Logos and Language in Tolkien's World* — on the mythology's use of light and language.
- Dimitra Fimi, *Tolkien, Race and Cultural History* — on the sources and the problems.

## Conclusion: The Tapestry, Unfinished and Complete

Tolkien died in September 1973 with the mythology unfinished. There were texts he had not completed, contradictions he had not resolved, questions he had not answered. The published Silmarillion is Christopher Tolkien's loving reconstruction — a completed text assembled from an incomplete one. The History of Middle-earth shows us the workshop.

And yet the mythology is also, in a different sense, complete. It is complete in the way that any living tradition is complete: not by having answered all questions but by having asked the right ones. It asks what creation is for. It asks what evil is and where it comes from and whether it can be redeemed. It asks what courage means when success is uncertain, and what love means when it costs everything. It asks why the ordinary matters.

These are questions without final answers, and a mythology that answered them conclusively would be lesser for the completeness. The unfinished tapestry is not a failure. It is the honest record of a mind that kept thinking, kept revising, kept finding new things to say about the world it had made — or rather, the world it had found, because Tolkien always described his work as discovery rather than invention.

The last ship has sailed from the Grey Havens. The Third Age is over. The mythology goes on.
