# The Curse That Would Not Break: Túrin Turambar, Glaurung the Dragon, and the Children of Húrin

*This is Part Three of the comprehensive in-universe chronological history of Middle-earth. Part Two covered the Wars of Beleriand — the coming of Men, the great battles from the Dagor Aglareb to the Nirnaeth Arnoediad, and in full the tale of Beren and Lúthien. We closed with Húrin captured on Thangorodrim and cursed by Morgoth to watch his family's destruction through the Black Enemy's twisted eyes. We continue now with the story of what Morgoth's curse actually did — and what it cost.*

*A note before we begin: The Children of Húrin is the most unrelentingly tragic story in Tolkien's legendarium. It contains no eucatastrophe — the sudden turn for the better that Tolkien considered the hallmark of genuine fairy-story. There is grief here without redemption, catastrophe without rescue, and a darkness that does not lift within the story's own frame. This is intentional. It is Tolkien's meditation on the nature of doomed heroism and on what happens when great gifts are combined with pride and with the malice of a power that will not let go. Understanding this darkness is essential to understanding why the light of the later ages means as much as it does.*

## Part 1: Morwen Eledhwen — The Woman Left Behind

When Húrin was taken and the Nirnaeth Arnoediad was lost, Morwen his wife was at home in Dor-lómin, the land in Hithlum that had been Húrin's holding. She was not a passive figure waiting for news — she was one of the most formidable women in Tolkien's world, and her response to catastrophe was characteristically proud and characteristically costly.

Morwen of the House of Bëor was called Eledhwen — Elfsheen — because she had the beauty and the bearing of an Elf despite being fully mortal. She was tall and dark-haired, clear-eyed, of a pride that was so deep it sometimes resembled coldness even to those who loved her. She was not cold. She was terrified, and her pride was the only weapon she had against terror.

After the Nirnaeth, Hithlum was given by Morgoth to the Easterlings — Men from the eastern parts of Middle-earth who had served Morgoth and who had no love for the Edain. They moved into Dor-lómin in their thousands, displacing and subjugating the Edain who remained. Morwen survived because the Easterlings feared her, in the instinctive way that bullies fear those who will not show fear. She also had the Ring of Barahir — or something like it, various of Tolkien's texts differ on the details of what talisman she held — and she was known as the wife of Húrin, the great warrior, who was not confirmed dead and whose reputation was still formidable.

She had two children at the time of the Nirnaeth. Túrin, who was eight years old — born in approximately the year 464 of the First Age. And Lalaith, "Laughter," a daughter of three, whose real name was Urwen. Lalaith was so called because she laughed and ran and sang in the streams like water, and everyone who saw her was charmed. She died that same year of a plague that Morgoth sent from Anfauglith — one of his cruelest weapons, disease carried on the east wind. Morwen bore this grief with the same terrible pride she bore everything, outwardly unmoved, inwardly changed forever.

She was pregnant again when Húrin was taken, and that winter she bore a daughter: Nienor, which means Mourning, and the name was prophetic in the most devastating way. Nienor would never know her father, would spend her life in the shadow of her brother's curse without knowing it was her brother's curse, and would die in one of the most harrowing episodes in all of Tolkien's writing.

Morwen's decision about Túrin was the most important she ever made, and she made it out of love and pride in equal measure. Dor-lómin was becoming increasingly dangerous. The Easterlings were pressing harder on the Edain households. She feared for her son's safety, and more, she feared for his spirit — that growing up under Easterling domination would break the pride and the fire that were his inheritance from both his parents. She sent him away to Doriath, to Thingol's court, with a letter asking Thingol to foster the child of Húrin, who had served Thingol's cause in the Nirnaeth.

Túrin was eight years old. He left with two companions — an old servant named Sador, whom he called Labadal, "Hopafoot," who was lame and whom Túrin had loved since childhood; and Gethron, an old retainer who knew the paths. He never saw his mother again.

## Part 2: Túrin in Doriath — The Making of a Character

Thingol received Túrin and honored the request. He took the boy into his court and treated him as a fostered son, giving him a place of honor in Menegroth. This was a genuine act of generosity from a king who had many concerns of his own — Doriath was still intact, still prosperous, still protected by the Girdle of Melian, but the outside world was worsening and Thingol knew it.

Túrin grew. He was not an easy child and he did not become an easy man. He had his mother's pride — the kind that does not yield, that would rather be destroyed than diminished — and his father's physical gifts: he was tall and dark and strong and extraordinarily skilled with weapons from an early age. He also had his own particular quality that was neither parent's gift exactly: a tendency to make the wrong choice when pride and love were in conflict. He was generous to those he chose to be generous to, fiercely loyal, capable of deep affection. He was also capable of a reckless anger that he could not always control and that he could not always direct at appropriate targets.

He made one great friend in Doriath: Beleg Cúthalion, the captain of Thingol's marches, the greatest archer among the Elves. Beleg was everything Túrin was not — patient, steady, capable of the long view, as skilled with his judgment as he was with his great bow Belthronding. The friendship between them was one of genuine complementarity: Túrin supplied fire and courage and physical power; Beleg supplied wisdom and precision and the willingness to wait. Together they were formidable beyond what either could achieve alone.

But the catastrophe began, as it so often does in Tolkien, not with malice but with something more recognisable: a young man's pride, an old man's resentment, and an evening that went wrong.

Saeros was a Nandorin Elf of Doriath, one of Thingol's counsellors, who had never liked Túrin. His reasons were the usual ones that small men give for disliking great ones who have not earned their greatness by the rules the small men recognise — Túrin was a mortal, a Man, favored by the king beyond what Saeros felt his kind deserved. At a feast in Menegroth, Saeros made a mocking comment about Mortal Women — suggesting that they ran like animals in their own lands, barely clothed. It was a precise insult: aimed at Morwen, Túrin's most sacred connection, the mother he had been forced to leave and had never been able to return to.

Túrin threw a goblet at Saeros. The next morning, Saeros came at Túrin with weapons. Túrin disarmed him, stripped him, and drove him through the forest, taunting him as he ran — a cruel payback for a cruel insult, but one that went too far. Saeros, running in panic through unfamiliar forest, fell into a ravine and was killed.

The death was accidental. Túrin had not meant to kill him. But the form of it — the stripping, the driving, the hunting — made it look like a deliberate humiliation followed by murder. And Túrin, rather than returning to Doriath to face judgment, fled. He did not know whether he would receive a fair hearing; he knew he had done wrong even if he had not done the worst; and his pride could not bear the idea of being judged and perhaps condemned in the halls of a king whose favour he had relied on. He ran.

This choice — to run rather than face judgment — is the first of Túrin's great pattern-choices, the one that sets the template for everything that follows. He was never guilty of the worst he was accused of, but he was never innocent of the pride that put him in the situation where the accusation could be made. And when faced with the need to submit to authority — to trust that others would judge him fairly, to accept the vulnerability of being assessed — he ran. Every major catastrophe of his life follows from this pattern.

Thingol's judgment, rendered after investigation, was mercy — Saeros's provocation was taken into account, and Túrin was not condemned. But the judgment came after Túrin had already gone.

## Part 3: The Outlaw Band — Mîm the Petty-dwarf

In the wild outside Doriath, Túrin fell in with a group of outlaws — men and elves dispossessed by the wars, living by raiding and scavenging. He became their leader, by the natural authority that he projected and the skill that backed it. Under him they were more disciplined than such groups usually were, less likely to prey on the innocent, capable of striking actual blows against Morgoth's servants. He was not a renegade leading criminals; he was a chieftain of desperate people trying to survive in a landscape increasingly controlled by the enemy.

But surviving was hard, and the winter was harder. In desperation, the outlaw band captured a Petty-dwarf — one of the Noegyth Nibin, a diminished and bitter people who were the original Dwarves of Middle-earth, those who had come from the east in the earliest ages and had been diminished by the long years and the losses of the First Age until they were smaller than true Dwarves, paranoid, hostile, hoarding their small claims to territory and dignity against a world that had largely forgotten them.

This Petty-dwarf was named Mîm, and he was the last of his direct line, living in the caves of Amon Rûdh with two sons. Túrin's band captured him and threatened to kill him unless he showed them shelter. He agreed — he had no real choice — and brought them to Bar-en-Danwedh, the House of Ransom, in the hill of Amon Rûdh. There they wintered, and Mîm served them with a grudging hospitality that covered a grudging affection — he grew to respect Túrin, in the way that lonely, bitter people sometimes grow to respect those who treat them with rough fairness when they expected nothing but contempt.

But Mîm also had grievances that he could not let go. One of the outlaws — Andróg — had killed one of his sons with an arrow. The other son, Khîm, died of that wound. Mîm's loss was real and his hatred for Andróg was real, and in the end it was Mîm's willingness to carry that hatred further than personal grief that brought the first disaster.

Beleg found them. He had been searching for Túrin since Túrin fled, and he was one of the best hunters in Beleriand, and eventually he tracked the band to Amon Rûdh. He came with the pardon of Thingol — with proof that Túrin was not condemned, that he could return to Doriath if he wished. He brought gifts: the great sword Anglachel, which was one of the two swords made from a single mass of iron meteorite by the Dwarf-smith Eöl the Dark Elf (the other was Anguirel), given to Thingol as part of Eöl's tribute. Anglachel was a black blade, cold and keen, and it had a kind of malice of its own — Melian warned Thingol of this when he received it, and Thingol gave it to Beleg to bear. Beleg also brought lembas — the waybread of the Elves, made by Melian herself, which gave extraordinary sustenance.

Túrin refused to return to Doriath. His pride would not let him. He accepted the pardon but would not accept the position of returning under pardon — it felt to him like being brought back in mercy rather than in right. Instead he asked Beleg to stay with him in the wild, and Beleg, because he loved Túrin and because his judgment about where he was most needed may have been affected by that love, agreed.

The band of Túrin and Beleg operated from Amon Rûdh for a time, and they were effective. They cleared the region of Orcs, maintained a safe haven for the scattered people of the area, and functioned as something between a guerrilla resistance and an outlaw stronghold. Túrin was known in Beleriand during this period as Gorthol, the Dread Helm, because he had a great dwarvish war-helm — the Dragon-helm of Dor-lómin, his father's heirloom — and in it he was unrecognizable and terrible.

Morgoth noticed. Any concentration of effective resistance in Beleriand would draw his attention eventually, and the activity of Túrin and Beleg was too effective to ignore. He sent spies. One of them was Mîm.

Mîm, nursing his hatred of Andróg and his bitterness at the whole world, made contact with Morgoth's Orcs and betrayed the location of Amon Rûdh and the disposition of the band for a reward — a price of gold that the Orcs would not actually pay him, because Morgoth's servants were not in the habit of keeping faith with their informants. He received nothing. But he had still done it.

The Orcs attacked at night. Most of the outlaws were killed. Túrin was captured alive — Morgoth had standing orders that Túrin was not to be killed, because Morgoth wanted to use him, to display him, to let his presence in Angband break Húrin watching on Thangorodrim. Beleg was left for dead, bound to a tree.

Beleg was not dead. He had extraordinary endurance. He recovered, freed himself, and went after the Orc-host that was carrying Túrin north. He tracked them across the wild, alone, through weather and terrain, following a column of Orcs carrying one prisoner. He was Beleg Cúthalion, the greatest ranger among the Elves of Doriath, and he did not stop.

He found the camp. He crept among the Orcs — sleeping, because they had reached a seeming safe distance from pursuit — and cut Túrin's bonds. Túrin was unconscious but uninjured; the Orcs had not been permitted to harm him. Beleg got him to the edge of the camp and tried to cut the bonds from his feet, but his knife — Angrist, no wait, that was another knife in another story; Beleg used the black blade Anglachel, which was difficult to control — slipped and cut Túrin's foot.

Túrin woke in darkness. He had been an Orc-prisoner. He felt someone cutting at him in the dark. He reacted with all the speed and violence of a warrior waking from nightmare: he seized Anglachel from Beleg's hand and killed him.

The death of Beleg Cúthalion is one of the most devastating moments in Tolkien's writing. There is a lightning flash — in the midst of the storm that had been following the Orc-host — and in the light of it Túrin saw what he had done. He saw Beleg's face. He recognised his best friend, and understood that he had killed him with Beleg's own sword.

He sat there through the rest of the night and did not move and did not speak. He could not move and could not speak. This was something beyond ordinary grief: it was the confrontation with a catastrophe entirely of his own making, a catastrophe that no one but himself could have caused, the death of the one person who had given up everything to save him.

A wood-elf named Gwindor found them — Gwindor had been a prisoner of Morgoth himself, escaped from Angband after long years, and he was wandering west when he came upon this scene: a dead Elf and a Man sitting silent beside him in the rain. He helped Túrin bury Beleg. He led Túrin away, and over months he slowly brought him back to himself.

## Part 4: Nargothrond — The Greatest of Follies

Gwindor led Túrin to Nargothrond. Gwindor had been of Nargothrond before his captivity — he had gone to the Nirnaeth Arnoediad in grief for his brother Gelmir, who had been killed by Morgoth, and had led a charge so reckless it broke the tactical plan and contributed to the disaster. He bore that guilt. He also bore the marks of Morgoth's dungeons on his body, and was no longer the lord he had been.

Nargothrond received them both, and Túrin's presence there changed everything.

He had carried Anglachel from the moment he picked it up from beside Beleg's body, and he never put it down. The sword was reforged at Nargothrond — it was given a new form, a new hilt, and a new name: Gurthang, "Iron of Death." It spoke, in some sense — Tolkien describes it as a sword that responded to its wielder, that could express its nature in the few words it was given to say at the very end of the story. Gurthang was a sword that wanted to kill. Its maker had made it with something too close to real malice for comfort, and it kept that quality through all its renamings.

In Nargothrond, Túrin met Finduilas, the daughter of Orodreth who had become king after Finrod's death. She was beautiful and gentle — an Elf of great kindness — and she fell in love with Túrin. This was a disaster waiting to happen, because Gwindor also loved her, and had loved her before his captivity, and his loss was compounded by seeing her fall for his companion. Gwindor recognised what Túrin was: a man who brought catastrophe to wherever he dwelt, not through malice but through the curse on his line and the pride of his nature. He told Finduilas this. She could not stop loving Túrin regardless.

Túrin did not love Finduilas the way she loved him. He did not love her with the full force of what love meant to him — we will see that force when he meets Nienor — but he was fond of her, protective of her, and her love was a comfort he did not wholly reject. He did nothing actively wrong in this situation, but his presence in it was a wound to Gwindor that complicated their friendship.

More dangerously: Túrin became the military leader of Nargothrond. He was the greatest warrior of his generation, and the people of Nargothrond recognised it, and they turned to him when Orodreth was uncertain. He changed their strategy — Finrod's policy of secrecy and concealment was, Túrin argued, a counsel of despair; the free peoples of Beleriand should be fighting openly, not hiding in caverns. He persuaded Nargothrond to build the Bridge of Nargothrond — a great stone bridge across the Narog, allowing armies to sortie from the city quickly — and to adopt an aggressive policy of open warfare.

This was wrong. It was the wrong strategy for Nargothrond's situation, and Gwindor told Túrin so in terms that were clear and specific: Nargothrond's safety rested entirely on its secrecy. Build a bridge, conduct open campaigns, and Morgoth would find them. Túrin's response was to accuse Gwindor of cowardice — which was genuinely unjust, because Gwindor was one of the bravest beings in Beleriand and had suffered more for his courage than Túrin yet had.

Túrin was right that concealment was no strategy for ultimate victory. He was wrong that open warfare from Nargothrond's position was workable. The bridge that was supposed to make the garrison mobile made the city accessible. Morgoth found Nargothrond.

He sent Glaurung.

## Part 5: Glaurung the Dragon — Father of Dragons

Glaurung, the first of all the great dragons, deserves his own section because he is one of the most completely realised villains in Tolkien's work — not just a monstrous animal but a personality, a will, a particular and horrible kind of intelligence.

He was the first dragon — whether Morgoth bred him from something else or created him by some other means is not specified, but he was in Angband during the early First Age and was sent out prematurely in what Tolkien describes as his first foray. In that early appearance he was still growing, and the Elves drove him back with arrows. After that, he was kept in Angband while he grew to full size, and when he emerged in the Dagor Bragollach, he was enormous — a fire-drake, a cold-drake, both kinds in one, armoured in scales that no ordinary weapon could pierce, capable of breathing fire that could melt stone, and possessed of the power of dragon-spell.

Dragon-spell was Glaurung's most terrible weapon. It was not merely hypnosis — it was something more than that, a direct assault on the will and the memory through the eyes. To meet Glaurung's gaze was to be paralysed, stripped of will, unable to act. And when he chose, he could use his spell to implant false memories, to erase true ones, to convince a person of things that were not true and to make them unable to access things that were. He was not merely a monster. He was a destroyer of minds.

He was also, in some sense, a tool of Morgoth's curse on Húrin's family. Morgoth had laid a doom on Húrin that all his children would be destroyed, and Glaurung was one of the instruments of that doom. But Glaurung was not a mindless instrument — he chose how to apply his power, he savoured the cruelty of it, he was a participant in the destruction rather than merely a weapon. This is important because it means the tragedy of Túrin and Nienor is not purely Morgoth's doing; it has an additional layer of evil chosen by Glaurung for its own.

His physical description: vast, scaled in green-black armour, moving on four great legs (unlike the winged dragons that would come later, Glaurung was wingless — a ground-crawling beast of enormous mass), capable of speeds that were shocking for a creature of his size, leaving a trail of burned and poisoned earth wherever he passed. The land around Nargothrond after his visit was called Talath Dirnen, the Guarded Plain, no more — it became a waste.

## Part 6: The Fall of Nargothrond

Glaurung crossed the Guarded Plain with an enormous Orc host — Morgoth had assembled the force, waited for Glaurung to lead it, sent the whole thing south toward Nargothrond after his spies confirmed the location. The Bridge of Nargothrond, built at Túrin's insistence, let them come straight to the gates.

Orodreth led the sortie from Nargothrond — the army went out to meet them in the field, because the Bridge made this possible and the strategy Túrin had urged made this necessary. The Noldor of Nargothrond were brave and skilled, but they were not enough. Glaurung's fire and the weight of the Orc-host broke the Noldorin army on the Guarded Plain. Orodreth was killed.

Túrin was there, fighting. He was almost supernaturally effective in the battle — he always was, the curse making him a being of terrible power who could not use that power to save anything. He cut his way through the battle, but the battle was lost, and at the end he stood before the Bridge.

Glaurung was crossing the Bridge.

Tolkien stages this confrontation with great care: Túrin, who has been the most powerful warrior in Beleriand for years, standing before a dragon. He had his dragon-helm. He had Gurthang. He stood his ground when other men would have run. He might even have prevailed — he was approaching Glaurung when the dragon turned his eyes on him.

Dragon-spell. Túrin stood paralysed, unable to move or speak, while Glaurung spoke to him. And what Glaurung said was calculated with the specific cruelty of something that has access to the full story and chooses the most devastating angle: he told Túrin that his mother Morwen and his sister Nienor were enslaved in Dor-lómin because of him, because he had abandoned them. He told Túrin that everything that had happened — Beleg's death, Nargothrond's fall, all of it — was Túrin's fault, and that Túrin's proper place was not here, fighting dragons, but there, in Dor-lómin, helping his family who needed him.

This was a lie, but it was a lie with enough truth in it to be devastating. Morwen and Nienor were in danger. Túrin had not been able to protect them. His pride had been part of the chain of decisions that led here.

Túrin ran. Not because he was a coward — he was among the least cowardly beings in Middle-earth — but because Glaurung's spell had convinced him that running was the only right thing left to do. He left Nargothrond behind. He left Finduilas behind.

Finduilas was taken captive with the survivors of Nargothrond, and when Túrin recovered himself — when the dragon-spell lifted — he pursued. He tracked the Orc column that was carrying the captives north, trying to catch them before they crossed the Teiglin. He did not catch them. The Men of Brethil caught them instead — led by Dorlas, who attacked the Orc column at the Crossings of Teiglin — but in the fight Finduilas was killed, not rescued: speared by an Orc, dying on the pike, crying out Túrin's name.

She had known he was coming. In her last moments, she called for him. He arrived after.

She was buried at the Crossings of Teiglin, and the mound was called Haudh-en-Elleth, the Mound of the Elf-maid, and it was said afterward that Orcs would not approach it, that something of Finduilas's spirit protected the crossing. Túrin stood over the mound and heard what she had said in her dying, and could not weep. He was past weeping. The tears would come later, and they would not be his alone.

## Part 7: Brethil — The Forest Kingdom and a New Identity

The Men of Brethil — the House of Haleth's descendants, the forest-warriors who had held their territory through the whole of the wars — offered Túrin refuge. He took it. He took also a new identity: he called himself Turambar, "Master of Fate," a name that expressed both a real aspiration and a terrible irony, because fate was the one thing he could not master, and the name he chose for freedom became the name associated with the final catastrophe.

He lived among the Men of Brethil for a time in deliberate anonymity. He did not reveal who he was. He kept himself apart from the fighting, refusing the role of leader and warrior that seemed to follow him everywhere. He had tried to be a leader and had destroyed Nargothrond. He tried now to be a simple man of the forest, doing his portion and no more.

There was peace for a short time. A relative peace — the world outside Brethil was increasingly dark, but within the forest, guarded by the Teiglin and the courage of its people, there was a kind of quietude. And into that quietude, Nienor came.

## Part 8: Morwen's Attempt — Nienor on Amon Ethir

Some time before Nargothrond's fall, Morwen had finally reached safety. She had left Dor-lómin at last, driven out by the Easterlings, and had come to Thingol's court in Doriath with Nienor. She had sent messengers to Nargothrond before its fall, and had received word of Túrin's presence there. She and Nienor set out for Nargothrond.

This was a mistake, but it was a comprehensible one. Morwen was a mother who had been separated from her son for most of his life, who had no idea whether he was alive, who had survived years of hardship on pride alone. When she had news of where he was, she went.

Mablung of Doriath accompanied them with a guard — Thingol sent him as escort, knowing the journey was dangerous, hoping his presence would make it less so. Nienor was twenty years old, a woman of extraordinary beauty — Tolkien describes her as the image of her mother, and Morwen was Elfsheen. She had never met Túrin; she was a child when he left, and she knew him only as the brother who had gone away.

They came to Amon Ethir, the hill of spies, which overlooked the site of Nargothrond from a safe distance. They could see that Nargothrond was taken — the smoke of its destruction, the presence of Glaurung by the river. Mablung tried to bring them back. Morwen refused. She needed to know about Túrin.

Glaurung came.

He came from Nargothrond and swam the Narog and climbed to where they stood, and he turned his eyes on Nienor specifically. The guard scattered in dragon-terror. Morwen vanished into the chaos — she was never found, and what became of her is one of the story's many unanswered griefs; she simply disappears from the narrative at this point and is not confirmed dead for a long time. Mablung survived but was thrown from his horse. Nienor stood before Glaurung's gaze.

He took everything from her.

With his spell, Glaurung erased Nienor's memory entirely — not just clouded it, not just distorted it, but took it all. She lost her name, her family, her language, her self. She became a blank — a person without a history, without knowledge of who she was or where she had come from. She ran from the hill and into the forest and ran for days, unable to stop, naked (she had shed her clothes in the running, or they had been torn away), mute, stripped of everything.

This is the depth of Glaurung's cruelty: he did not kill her. He took her self and left the body, and placed the self-less body in the path of the one person whose finding her would cause the greatest possible harm.

## Part 9: Nienor Found — Niniel of the Tears

The Men of Brethil found a young woman wandering in the forest, naked and mute and terrified. They brought her to their village. She could not speak. She could not communicate anything of who she was. Over time — through gentleness and care — they reached her enough that she began to speak again, learning the language of Brethil as if she had never known any language. She was given a name: Niniel, "Maid of Tears," because she wept when she first learned to speak, and her first words were grief.

Túrin met her. And he fell in love with her with the full force of what love meant to him — not the fond protection he had felt for Finduilas, not anything partial, but everything. She was beautiful and gentle and brave in her damaged way, and she clearly needed him, and he had been without anyone to love or protect for a long time, and he loved her.

She loved him also. She called him Turambar, Master of Fate, because that was the only name she knew for him. He told her enough of himself — not his true name, not his full history — that she understood him as a man carrying great grief and great danger. She accepted this. They were married. For a short time they were happy, in a forest far from the main war, in a peace that was an eye in the storm.

But the storm moved.

Glaurung moved from Nargothrond northward, following whatever destruction he was pointed toward. He came toward Brethil and the Crossings of Teiglin. The Men of Brethil would have to fight him, or flee.

Túrin could not flee. The Master of Fate could not run from this — not from a dragon, not again, not with Niniel watching. He organised the defence. He went with a small group to intercept Glaurung at the cliffs above the Cabed-en-Aras — the Deer's Leap, a gorge where the Teiglin ran through a narrow ravine. The plan was to descend the cliff and strike at Glaurung from below, driving Gurthang into his belly where the scales were thinner.

Dorlas, who had been brave enough to fight Orcs, could not face the dragon. He abandoned the expedition at the cliff-face. Hunthor, one of the chiefs of Brethil, went instead. He was killed by a falling stone before they reached the bottom.

Túrin went alone.

He crossed the river in the dark, under the bank while the dragon lay above on the cliff-edge, and drove Gurthang upward into Glaurung's belly with all his strength. The sword went in to the hilt.

Glaurung screamed — a sound that was heard for miles. He thrashed across to the far side of the ravine and lay still.

Túrin went to him. He drew out Gurthang from the wound. And as he did, the dragon's blood — venomous, caustic — splashed on his hand and he fainted from the pain and the fumes.

---

## Part 10: Glaurung's Last Words

Niniel had followed. She had come to the place of battle because she could not stay behind, and she found Túrin collapsed beside the dying dragon, and she thought he was dead or dying. She knelt over him, weeping. 

And Glaurung, with his last strength, opened his eyes and spoke to her. He had been waiting for this. The dragon-spell had always been aimed at this moment — the full revelation, timed for maximum destruction.

He told her who she was. He told her her name was Nienor. He told her who Túrin was. He told her that the man she had married, the man she carried the child of, was her own brother.

Then Glaurung died, and the spell he had cast on her years ago broke. Her memory returned in full, all at once, everything that had been taken from her. She remembered Morwen, and Doriath, and the hill of Amon Ethir, and herself as she had been. She remembered her brother Túrin who had gone away when she was three years old and whom she had never met, and she looked at the unconscious man beside the dead dragon and understood.

She did not scream. She stood up and walked to the edge of the gorge and threw herself into the Teiglin.

She was pregnant with Túrin's child. She died. The child died.

Túrin woke and was told what had happened. He was told by the Men of Brethil who had come to the scene. He was told his wife had thrown herself into the Teiglin, and why.

He asked Gurthang — asked his sword, as the sword was a thing with something like a will — he asked it to kill him quickly and without flinching. Tolkien gives the sword's reply:

*"Yea, I will drink thy blood gladly, that so I may forget the blood of Beleg my master, and the blood of Brandir slain unjustly. I will slay thee swiftly."*

(Brandir was a chief of Brethil whom Túrin had also killed in his grief and rage upon hearing of Niniel's death, blaming him wrongly for not preventing it — the last and perhaps the least justified of Túrin's killings.)

Túrin set the hilt of Gurthang in the ground and fell upon the blade, and the sword killed him.

He was buried with Gurthang on the heights above the Teiglin, in a mound called Haudh-en-Ndengin. And on the stone was written in runes of Doriath: *Túrin Turambar Dagnir Glaurunga* — "Túrin Turambar, Slayer of Glaurung." And beneath it, after Nienor's death was known: *Nienor Níniel.*

It was the darkest ending Tolkien ever wrote for a protagonist.

---

## Part 11: Húrin Released — And What He Found

Many years after Túrin's death, Morgoth released Húrin from Thangorodrim. Twenty-eight years Húrin had sat on the stone seat, watching through Morgoth's malicious perspective everything that had happened to his wife and children. The release was not an act of mercy — Morgoth had no mercy. It was a final cruelty: send the broken man back into the world to see the ruins of everything his family had been, and see if he could make still more damage by his going.

Húrin walked out of the north alone, without possessions, without the great companions of his youth. He was old — not in the way Men usually age, but in the way that beings wear out when they have endured too much for too long. His hair was white. His eyes were changed. The fire was still there, but banked lower, under ash.

He found the ruins of Nargothrond — sacked long since, its treasures looted, Glaurung's hoard scattered. He found Mîm living there, the Petty-dwarf who had betrayed Túrin's outlaw band. He killed him. Then he took the Nauglamír — the necklace of Dwarven craft — from among the ruins, because it had been Finrod's and Húrin had reason to think of it as something that should be brought to the king who had loved Finrod's memory.

He went to Doriath. He brought the Nauglamír to Thingol and put it before him with words that were not entirely kind — the grief of a man who had watched everything from a distance for nearly three decades was not a cleanly directed grief. He then left Doriath. There is some indication he went looking for Morwen, and there is a tradition — in some versions of the story — that he found her at Túrin's grave, and they spoke, and she died there, and he buried her, and then he went to the sea. Whether he then died, or went west, or walked off the edge of the world, is not stated. He simply ends.

He is one of the most completely realised characters in Tolkien's work: a man of extraordinary gifts — physical, intellectual, moral — who was destroyed not by any weakness of character but by the deliberate and sustained malice of a power that was angry at what he represented. Húrin never broke. He endured Thangorodrim for twenty-eight years and did not tell Morgoth where Gondolin was, and he walked out of it still himself, however damaged. The darkness got his children. It did not get him.

---

## Part 12: The Theological Weight of Túrin's Story

Tolkien wrote about the Children of Húrin in multiple versions across his life, returning to it again and again in a way that suggests it occupied a special place in his imagination. He wrote it as the Lay of the Children of Húrin in alliterative verse, as the prose Narn i Hîn Húrin in Unfinished Tales, as the condensed Quenta Silmarillion version, and it was assembled by Christopher Tolkien into a standalone book published in 2007.

Why was this story so important to him? Partly because it was his version of a great Northern myth — the story of Sigurd and the dragon Fafnir, of the Norse hero doomed by fate, of tragedy as a form of nobility. Tolkien's deep engagement with Old Norse literature and with the concept of the Northern heroic spirit — facing fate without flinching even when the fate is certain destruction — runs through the Children of Húrin more directly than anywhere else.

But it is also Tolkien's most sustained exploration of the intersection between free will and external evil. Túrin is not simply a victim. He makes choices — bad ones, consistently. His pride is real and it drives real decisions. At the same time, Morgoth's curse is real, and the question of what would have happened to Túrin without the curse is genuinely open. Would he have been a great hero? He had all the gifts for it. Would he have been self-destructive? Possibly. The nature of Tolkien's tragedy here is that we cannot fully separate the curse from the character, because the curse works through the character's own tendencies.

This is philosophically interesting and theologically honest. Tolkien does not present Túrin as a puppet of fate — he makes clear that Túrin's choices are real choices, that he bears genuine responsibility for them. But he also does not pretend that Morgoth's curse is nothing — it is something, it bends probability in ways that are not random, it ensures that the worst possible interpretation is always available. The curse does not force Túrin to kill Beleg, but it ensures that the circumstances for that killing will arise; it does not force Túrin to stay at Nargothrond and overrule its counsels, but it ensures that the pride which led him to do so would be applied to exactly the wrong situation.

Evil in Tolkien is a distorting lens, not a puppet-master. It takes what is there and twists it. Which means that the responsibility for what happens is shared between the one who twists and the one who provides the material for twisting — a moral framework that is considerably more nuanced than either pure determinism or pure free-will individualism.

There is also the matter of Nienor, who is the most purely victimised figure in the story. She did not have Túrin's pride. She was not marked by anything that Morgoth could have used against her in the way he used Túrin's nature. She was collateral damage — devastated because she happened to be the sister of the man Morgoth most wanted to destroy, because Glaurung found it convenient to use her as an instrument. Her death is the cleanest possible illustration of how evil in Tolkien works: it does not punish the wicked. It radiates outward, destroying the good in order to destroy the thing it is aimed at. Nienor was innocent and she was killed. This is true. Tolkien does not apologise for it.

---

## Part 13: The Significance of Gurthang

A word on the sword, because it is not merely a weapon. Gurthang — reforged from Anglachel, made by the Dark Elf Eöl from an iron meteorite — is one of the few inanimate objects in Tolkien's world that has something like a voice, something like a will. It spoke when Túrin asked it to kill him. It answered. This is more than metaphor.

The closest analogy in the wider mythology is the One Ring, which also has a kind of will — a will to return to its maker, to seek its master, to manipulate events toward its own ends. Gurthang is not nearly as powerful as the Ring, and its malice is not toward domination but toward death specifically. Eöl made it with too much of himself in it — too much of his own bitter, violent, isolated spirit — and the sword retained that spirit.

Tolkien in a note on the making of the Nauglamír and related matters discusses the way that great acts of craft can impress some of the maker's own nature into the made object. This is the theory behind the One Ring's power; it is also behind Gurthang's will. The difference is intentionality: Sauron deliberately put his will and his power into the Ring, as an act of purpose. Eöl's impressing of himself into Anglachel was perhaps less deliberate, more the inevitable result of the kind of person he was.

The sword's last words — agreeing to kill Túrin, saying it would drink his blood gladly so it could forget the blood of Beleg and of Brandir — are a remarkable piece of characterisation for an object. The sword does not want to remember what it has done. It is, in some small way, as cursed as its wielder.

---

## Part 14: What the Children of Húrin Leaves Behind

Túrin killed Glaurung. This is not nothing. Glaurung was the father of dragons, the most terrible dragon Morgoth had yet deployed, the instrument of Nargothrond's fall and of untold suffering across Beleriand. His death at Túrin's hand was a genuine blow against Morgoth's power. Later dragons — Ancalagon the Black, the winged dragons of the War of Wrath — would be more terrible, but Glaurung was the prototype, the one from whom all others derived their lineage. His killing mattered.

It also did not save anything. This is the tragedy's particular shape: Túrin accomplished something real and significant, and it cost everything and changed nothing at the strategic level. Beleriand was still falling. The free peoples were still losing. The death of Glaurung bought the Men of Brethil a respite, not a victory.

The house of Hador — Húrin's house — was effectively ended. There were cousins, distant relatives, people who carried some portion of that bloodline, but the direct line of the greatest of the Men of Beleriand was broken. Turgon of Gondolin carried on the hope of the Noldor. Círdan at the Havens held the sea. But Beleriand had been burning for generations, and its burning was nearly complete.

What would come next — the Fall of Gondolin, the voyage of Eärendil, the War of Wrath — is the subject of Part Four of this series. But we cannot leave the Children of Húrin without noting one final thing: in Tolkien's mythology, Túrin will come again. 

There is a prophecy — fragmentary, found in the early versions of the mythology, referenced but not fully elaborated in the published Silmarillion — that at the End of Days, when Morgoth returns from the Void and the final battle is fought, Túrin Turambar will be there. He will be the one to kill Morgoth — to drive Gurthang, that sword which drank the blood of the man who wielded it, into the throat of the Black Enemy himself. The doom-sayer, the cursed one, the man who could not save anything in the First Age, will be the instrument of Morgoth's final death.

Whether this is canon in Tolkien's final understanding of his mythology is uncertain — Christopher Tolkien indicates it belongs to an early phase that was later superseded. But it has the ring of something Tolkien deeply meant, even if he later doubted its fit in the larger structure. The idea that the most tragic figure of the First Age becomes the final hero of the world's history is the kind of eucatastrophe — the sudden turn, the unexpected redemption — that Tolkien built his entire theory of mythology around. Defeat is not the end of the story. The story is longer than any one life, longer than any one age.

Túrin Turambar. Master of Doom, by Doom mastered. His story is not over.

## Part 15: A Note on Sources and Variants

The Children of Húrin exists in more versions than perhaps any other single story in Tolkien's legendarium, and understanding the variants is important for anyone who reads deeply into the mythology.

The earliest version is the Lay of the Children of Húrin — an unfinished alliterative poem in Old English style, written in the early 1920s, which covers the story through Beleg's death and some of Gwindor's journey. It is published in The Lays of Beleriand (History of Middle-earth Vol. 3). It is the most Northern in spirit of all the versions, closest to the saga tradition Tolkien was working from.

The prose Narn i Hîn Húrin — written in the 1950s — is the fullest version, much longer than the Silmarillion account, containing episodes that the Silmarillion condenses or omits entirely. It is published in Unfinished Tales. It includes, notably, much more of Morwen's story, the extended account of Mîm and the Petty-dwarves, and a more detailed rendering of the fall of Nargothrond.

The Silmarillion version, prepared by Christopher Tolkien from his father's papers, is a condensation and synthesis intended to fit the Children of Húrin into the larger narrative framework. It is accurate in all major particulars but necessarily abbreviates.

The standalone Children of Húrin (2007), also edited by Christopher Tolkien, is the fullest assembly of the story from all available sources, with Alan Lee's illustrations. It is the most complete single-volume account. Where Tolkien's different versions conflict in detail — the exact sequence of events at Nargothrond, the precise nature of Glaurung's initial encounter with Nienor — Christopher's editorial choices in the 2007 volume generally represent his best judgment of his father's final intentions.

There is one significant theological problem that Tolkien himself flagged and never resolved: if Glaurung's spell on Nienor was so complete that it erased her memory entirely, how could she be held morally responsible for anything she did while under it? The answer, in terms of the story's dramatic logic, is that she cannot — the tragedy is entirely Glaurung's doing. But the story does not make Nienor's death the result of guilt; it makes it the result of grief. She throws herself into the Teiglin not because she feels she has sinned but because the world, in that moment, is too terrible to remain in. Her death is an act of horror, not of justice. Tolkien understood the difference.

---

## Conclusion: The Dark Before the Dawn

The Children of Húrin is the First Age at its most relentless. It shows what Morgoth's malice could do when focused on a single target across decades — the patient, sustained destruction of a family through the twisting of fate rather than direct assault. And it shows what courage looks like when it has no horizon: Húrin on Thangorodrim, Morwen in Dor-lómin, Túrin against the dragon he could not have defeated without dying. Courage without hope is the hardest kind. It is also, in Tolkien's moral framework, the most real kind.

The First Age was ending. Gondolin still stood, but its fall was coming. The sons of Fëanor were still alive and still bound by the Oath. The Silmaril that Beren had recovered was with Elwing at the Mouths of Sirion. And in Gondolin, Tuor and Idril's son Eärendil was growing up, half-Elf and half-Man, carrying in his bloodline the concentrated heritage of the Eldar and the Edain both.

He would be the one to sail to Valinor. He would be the one to plead for the world. But first, Gondolin had to fall, and the Third Kinslaying had to happen, and Eärendil had to find the way to the Hidden Land. That is the subject of Part Four.
