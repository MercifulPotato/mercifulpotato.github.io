For four posts we have built foundations. We know what a budget is, when it arrives and why, who wrote this one, and how a speech becomes a law and, sometimes, a result. Now we climb onto that scaffolding and begin the real work of analysis, and we begin where the budget itself begins: with the number that towers over everything else.

The total budget for fiscal year 2083/84 is **NPR 2,124.34 billion** — about **USD 14.16 billion** at our assumed rate of 150 rupees to the dollar. The government calls it the largest budget in Nepal's history, and a great deal of the press repeats the phrase. In this post we are going to take that single number and turn it over in our hands until we understand what it does and does not mean. We will learn to read a giant number in the units Nepalis actually use. We will learn why "largest in history" is, in the way it is usually meant, very nearly meaningless. We will learn the difference between a number that has grown and a number that has merely been inflated. And we will learn the handful of denominators — inflation, last year's true figures, the population, the size of the economy — that turn an incomprehensible total into something a citizen can actually judge. This is the post where you stop being impressed by bigness and start asking the questions that make bigness mean something.

## Learning to read a giant number

Before we can judge the number, we have to be able to *read* it, and here we meet a genuine obstacle that trips up foreign observers and even many Nepalis when they move between local and international reporting: Nepal does not count large numbers the way the Western world does.

The international convention groups digits in threes and names them thousand, million, billion, trillion. South Asia — Nepal, India, and their neighbours — uses a different system, grouping differently and using its own names, and Nepali budget documents are written in it. The key units, from small to large, are these. A **lakh** is one hundred thousand. A **crore** (in Nepali, *karod*) is ten million — that is, one hundred lakh. An **arab** (sometimes written *arba*) is one billion — one hundred crore. And a **kharab** is one hundred billion — one hundred arab. So the ladder runs: lakh, crore, arab, kharab, each step a leap of a hundred from the one before once you are past the lakh.

This matters practically, because when you read the budget in its original form, or in Nepali coverage, the total is not written as "2,124.34 billion." It is written in arab and kharab. Our headline figure of NPR 2,124.34 billion is the same as **2,124.34 arab**, which is the same as about **21 kharab 24 arab** — and, if you prefer crore, about **212,434 crore**. None of these is a different amount of money; they are the same sum spoken in different languages of counting. Throughout this series we will use billions, because that is what most international readers think in and what lets us translate cleanly to dollars, but you should be able to recognise the same number when you meet it dressed in arab and kharab, because that is how the government itself presents it, and a great deal of confusion comes simply from people comparing a figure in one system to a figure in the other without realising they have changed units. When in doubt, convert everything to one system before you compare. The unit is not the quantity; mixing units is one of the easiest ways to make two numbers look more different, or more similar, than they really are.

With that obstacle cleared, we can read the headline. NPR 2,124.34 billion is roughly USD 14.16 billion. It is a very large amount of money in the context of a small, low-income economy. The question is not whether it is large — of course it is large — but what its largeness actually tells us. And the first thing to understand is that, in the sense the headline intends, its largeness tells us almost nothing.

## Nominal versus real: the difference between growing and merely inflating

Here is the single most important idea for judging whether any sum of money has truly grown over time, and it is an idea that the word "record" is specifically designed to make you forget.

Money loses value over time, because prices rise over time. This rising of prices is called **inflation**, and it is one of the most fundamental forces in any economy. When there is inflation, a rupee tomorrow buys less than a rupee today. A hundred rupees that bought a certain basket of goods ten years ago buys a noticeably smaller basket now, because the prices of the things in the basket have climbed. Inflation is, in effect, the slow shrinking of what each unit of money can actually do.

This forces a crucial distinction between two ways of measuring any amount of money. A **nominal** figure is the amount in the money of the day, just as it is written, with no adjustment. A **real** figure is the amount after stripping out the effect of inflation, so that you are comparing the actual quantity of goods, services, and resources the money commands, rather than just the size of the number printed on it. The difference is not academic. It is the difference between a number that has genuinely grown — that commands more real stuff than before — and a number that has merely been inflated, that is bigger only because the money it is counted in is worth less.

Consider why "the largest budget in history" is, in nominal terms, almost a tautology — a statement that is true by definition and therefore tells you nothing. Because of inflation, the nominal size of nearly everything tends to climb year after year: prices, wages, the cost of a bus ticket, and yes, the nominal size of the government's budget. In an economy with steady inflation, almost every year's budget will be nominally larger than the last, for the same boring reason that almost every year sets a new record for the highest average prices in history. Announcing that this year's budget is the largest ever, measured in plain rupees, is a bit like announcing that this year you weigh more in grams than you did when you were measured in kilograms — the bigger number reflects the smaller unit, not a bigger you. To know whether the budget has *really* grown, you have to ask what it would be after adjusting for inflation, and compare real to real.

Let us do exactly that, roughly, with the figures we have. This budget of NPR 2,124.34 billion is, against last year's *original* budget of about NPR 1,964 billion, larger by roughly 8 percent in nominal terms. But Nepal has had inflation in recent years running somewhere in the region of 5 to 7 percent — indeed this very budget sets a target of holding inflation to no more than 6 percent. If prices are rising at, say, 5 to 6 percent, then a budget that is 8 percent larger in nominal terms is only about 2 to 3 percent larger in *real* terms — in terms of the actual quantity of resources it commands. The "record" budget, once you strip out the shrinking of the rupee, represents only a modest real increase over last year's original plan. That is a completely different impression from "the largest in history," and it is the truer one. We are not saying a 2 to 3 percent real increase is nothing; for a poor country it is a real expansion of state capacity. We are saying that the honest size of the increase is small and modest, not the dramatic record the headline implies, and that the gap between those two impressions is created entirely by ignoring inflation. (The exact real figure depends on the exact inflation rate, which should be taken from the official price data; the point is the method and the order of magnitude, not a precise decimal.)

## "Largest in history": the full anatomy of a hollow phrase

We can now see all the reasons the phrase "largest budget in history" is so much weaker than it sounds, and assembling them in one place gives you a complete defence against it for the rest of your life.

The first reason we have just covered: *inflation*. Nominal records are cheap because the unit keeps shrinking. A meaningful comparison is in real terms, and in real terms this budget is only modestly larger than last year's.

The second reason we covered back in Part 2: the *base-year switch*. The advertised increase of 25.2 percent is measured against last year's *revised* estimate — the deflated, mid-year figure the previous government settled on after admitting it could not collect or spend its original plan. Measured against last year's *original* budget of about NPR 1,964 billion, the increase is only about 8 percent. The dramatic-sounding 25.2 percent and the modest-sounding 8 percent describe the very same new budget; the difference is entirely in the choice of what to compare against. A careful reader always asks which base is being used and looks up the other.

The third reason is the deepest, and it is the one we will spend much of the rest of the series demonstrating: *a budget is a plan, not an outcome*. The headline total is the amount the government has decided to *try* to spend. It is not the amount it *will* spend. As we noted in the foundations and will document in detail in the post on capital spending, Nepal has a long and remarkably consistent record of failing to spend large portions of its budget — especially the capital portion, the money meant for building things. A budget can set a nominal record and then go substantially unspent, in which case the record exists only on paper and in the speech. Celebrating the size of a budget before it has been executed is like celebrating the size of a meal you have ordered but not yet been served, in a restaurant with a known habit of running out of food.

Put the three together and the phrase dissolves. "The largest budget in history" means, on inspection: a budget that is nominally the biggest only because money keeps shrinking; whose advertised growth depends on a flattering choice of comparison; and whose real largeness will not be known until we see how much of it is actually spent, which history suggests will be considerably less than the whole. The phrase is not a lie. It is a true statement engineered to create a false impression of significance. Your defence is to deflate it — literally, by adjusting for inflation, and figuratively, by remembering that bigness announced is not bigness delivered.

## What the number does not tell you: the execution gap

We have twice now gestured at the gap between budgeted and spent, and it deserves a clear statement here because it is the single biggest reason a headline total can mislead, even when every figure in it is honest.

Each year a government budgets a certain amount and then, over the following twelve months, actually spends some fraction of it. In a well-functioning system that fraction is high — close to the full amount, because the plan was realistic and the machinery worked. In Nepal, the fraction has chronically been lower than it should be, and the shortfall is concentrated in the capital budget, the money for construction and durable assets. It is common, year after year, for a substantial share of the capital budget to go unspent — for roads, bridges, and buildings that were funded on paper to remain unbuilt because the money never actually flowed, for the reasons we explored in the posts on the calendar and on how budgets become action: late fund release, slow procurement, land disputes, the monsoon, and the year-end scramble to spend whatever can be spent before the authority lapses.

This means that the most important number about any Nepali budget is one that does not appear in the budget at all, because it cannot be known in advance: the *execution rate* — the share of the budgeted money that will actually be spent. The headline total of NPR 2,124.34 billion is the numerator's promise. The real story will be written months later in the Financial Comptroller General Office's records of what was truly spent, and in the Auditor General's examination of how well it was spent. A reader who has internalised this will instinctively translate "the government has announced a budget of NPR 2.12 trillion" into the more accurate "the government has announced its *intention* to spend up to NPR 2.12 trillion, of which, on past form, a meaningful chunk — especially of the building money — may never actually be spent." That translation is not cynicism. It is simply replacing the plan with the realistic expectation, which is what an analyst does and an audience does not.

## Making the number mean something: per person, and against the economy

So far we have been deflating the headline. But a citizen still wants to know: is NPR 2.12 trillion a lot or a little? The honest answer is that bigness is meaningless in isolation; a number becomes judgeable only when you divide it by something that gives it scale. Two denominators do most of the work.

The first is *population*. Divide the whole budget by the roughly thirty million people of Nepal, and you get a per-person figure of about **NPR 70,000 per Nepali (around USD 470)** for the year. This is one of the most useful figures in the entire series, because it converts an incomprehensible national total into a human-sized one: this is, very roughly, the amount the government plans to collect-and-spend on behalf of each man, woman, and child over twelve months — to provide their share of every road, school, clinic, soldier, civil servant, subsidy, and interest payment in the country. Seventy thousand rupees per person per year, for everything the federal state does. Stated that way, the number stops being a boast and becomes a budget — a finite, human amount that must somehow cover an enormous list of needs. And it immediately raises the right questions: is seventy thousand rupees per person enough to do what the budget promises? When the budget pledges, say, sweeping health coverage and new hospitals and dramatic infrastructure and large social allowances all at once, the per-person figure is the reality check — because all of those promises, for every person, have to fit inside that one modest per-person sum, alongside salaries and interest and everything else.

The second denominator is the *size of the economy* — the Gross Domestic Product, or GDP, that we met in Part 1, the total value of everything the country produces in a year. This is the denominator economists care about most, because it measures the budget against the economy's actual capacity to bear it. A budget that is a small slice of a large economy is easily afforded; a budget that is a large slice of a small economy is straining the country's means. Nepal's GDP is on the order of NPR 6 trillion (very roughly USD 40 to 46 billion — the exact figure should be taken from the official Economic Survey, and the dollar value depends on the exchange rate). Set the budget of NPR 2,124.34 billion against an economy of that order, and the budget is on the rough order of a third of the entire economy. That is a large share — it means the federal government alone is planning to collect-and-spend an amount equivalent to roughly a third of everything the country produces — and it tells you that the state is a very major actor in Nepali economic life, that its choices ripple widely, and that there is not vast untapped room to simply spend more without either taxing more or borrowing more, both of which have costs we will examine. The share-of-GDP lens also lets you compare Nepal sensibly to other countries and to its own past, in a way that raw rupee totals never can. (We give the share as "roughly a third" rather than a precise percentage deliberately, because the precise figure depends on the exact GDP and should be computed from the official data; the point is the order of magnitude and the method.)

With these two denominators, the headline finally means something. NPR 2.12 trillion is about NPR 70,000 per person and roughly a third of the economy: a modest amount per citizen, a large claim on a small economy, and only slightly larger in real terms than last year's plan. That is a real, judgeable description of the budget's size — and it bears almost no emotional resemblance to "the largest in history."

## A skeptic's checklist for any headline budget number

Let us distil this post into a portable tool. Whenever you encounter a headline budget figure — in Nepal or anywhere, this year or in twenty years — run it through these questions before you let it impress or alarm you.

First, *what units, and converted to a common system?* Make sure you are not comparing arab to billions or crore to millions. Convert everything to one system first. Second, *nominal or real?* If a figure is described as larger or a record, ask what it looks like after adjusting for inflation, because nominal records are nearly automatic and tell you little. Third, *compared to which base?* If there is a percentage change, find out exactly which earlier figure it is measured against — original estimate, revised estimate, or actual — and look up the others, because the base does much of the deceiving. Fourth, *plan or outcome?* Remember that a budget total is an intention, not a result, and that the meaningful figure — the execution rate — will only be known after the year is over. Fifth, *divided by what?* Put the number against population and against the size of the economy, because bigness is meaningless until it is scaled by the people it must serve and the economy that must bear it. Run any giant national number through those five questions and you will understand it far better than the person who announced it wanted you to.

## A worked example: last year's three faces

To make the plan-versus-outcome point concrete, let us trace a single budget through its three faces — announced, revised, actual — using the shape of last year's numbers, which is exactly the comparison this year's headline rests on.

Last year, the government *announced* a budget of about NPR 1,964 billion (around USD 13.1 billion). That was the original estimate: the ambitious plan, the figure in the speech, the number that was itself, at the time, described as a record. Then the year unfolded. Revenue came in below hopes; ministries, especially on the capital side, proved unable to spend what they had been given. So partway through the year the government produced a *revised* estimate, cutting the figure to something in the region of NPR 1,700 billion (around USD 11.3 billion) — a figure to confirm against the official documents, but clearly well below the original. And the *actual* spending, the money that truly went out the door by year's end, would have been lower still in the places where execution failed, particularly in capital spending, where a large share of even the revised allocation may have gone unspent.

Now watch what this does to this year's headline. This year's budget of NPR 2,124.34 billion is advertised as 25.2 percent larger than last year — but larger than last year's *revised* NPR 1,700 billion, the deflated figure. Against last year's *original* NPR 1,964 billion, it is only about 8 percent larger. And against what last year actually *spent* — which, because of the execution gap, was likely lower than even the revised estimate — this year's *plan* would look larger still, but that comparison is unfair in the opposite direction, because it sets this year's optimistic plan against last year's disappointing reality. The honest picture is this: the new budget is modestly larger than last year's original plan, more substantially larger than last year's deflated revision, and its own true size will not be known until it too passes through the grinder of execution and emerges, months from now, as an actual figure that history suggests will fall short of the promise. Three faces, three numbers, one budget — and the headline quietly chose the comparison that flattered it most.

## For the technically minded: deflating and reconciling

For readers who work with data, two short exercises capture the analytical content of this post.

First, the real-terms adjustment. To compare two nominal figures from different years honestly, you deflate the later one by the cumulative inflation between them, or equivalently express both in the prices of a single year. As plain pseudo-logic:

```text
# Compare this year's budget to last year's ORIGINAL budget, in real terms.
nominal_this_year   = 2124.34   # NPR billion
nominal_last_year   = 1964.00   # NPR billion (last year's ORIGINAL estimate)
inflation_rate      = 0.055     # ~5.5% assumed; use the official figure

nominal_growth = nominal_this_year / nominal_last_year - 1      # ~0.082  -> ~8.2%
real_growth    = (1 + nominal_growth) / (1 + inflation_rate) - 1 # ~0.026 -> ~2.6%

# Interpretation: an ~8% nominal rise is only an ~2-3% REAL rise once
# inflation is removed. "Largest in history" shrinks to "slightly larger in real terms."
```

Second, the reconciliation discipline we introduced in Part 1, now applied to the headline. A budget's pieces must add up, and forcing them to do so is how you catch what a speech glosses over:

```text
recurrent + capital + financing  ==  total_outlay
1270.58   + 431.10  + 422.64     ==  2124.32   # ~= 2124.34 (rounding in sources)

revenue + grants + foreign_loans + domestic_borrowing  >=  total_outlay
1405.31 + 61.74   + 247.28       + 410.00               =  2124.33   # the plan balances on paper

deficit_gap  ==  total_outlay - revenue - grants
657.29       ==  2124.34 - 1405.31 - 61.74              # = 657.29   # this is what must be borrowed
```

When you total a budget rather than reading it line by line, two things happen. You confirm that the announced pieces actually sum to the announced whole — and small discrepancies, like the rounding here, tell you where to be precise and which official figure to trust. And you make visible the quantities the speech presents gently, above all the deficit: the NPR 657.29 billion (about USD 4.38 billion) gap between what the government plans to spend and what it expects to raise in revenue and grants, which can only be filled by borrowing. That deficit is the subject of two whole posts later in the series. For now, simply note that it falls out of the arithmetic the moment you insist the budget add up — which is precisely why insisting it add up is the beginning of reading it honestly.

## Where we go from here

We have dissected the big number. You can now read it in the arab and kharab of its native counting and in the billions of international comparison; you know the difference between a nominal record and a real increase, and that this budget's real growth over last year's original plan is modest, not dramatic; you know the three reasons "largest in history" is a hollow phrase — inflation, the base-year switch, and the gap between plan and outcome; and you know the two denominators, population and the size of the economy, that turn the total into something judgeable: about NPR 70,000 per person, roughly a third of the economy. You have a five-question checklist for any headline number, and you have seen the deficit fall out of the arithmetic, waiting for us in a later post.

In Part 6 we make the single most important cut inside the budget: the division between *recurrent* spending and *capital* spending — between the money that runs the state and the money that builds it. We will see why Nepal spends roughly three rupees running itself for every one rupee it spends building itself, why economists find that ratio worrying, how the new government's large civil-service pay rise tightens the squeeze, and how to spot the most common trick in all of public spending: consumption dressed up as investment. The headline is dissected. Now we look at where the money actually goes.

## A Note on Numbers and Assumptions

All US dollar figures use our single assumed rate of **1 US dollar = 150 Nepalese rupees**, an approximation close to the Nepal Rastra Bank reference rate in mid-2026; the rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee at 1.60 and drifts daily, so treat dollar figures as intuition, not accounting. All rupee figures are nominal unless explicitly adjusted; where this post computes a real-terms figure, it does so to illustrate the method, using an assumed inflation rate of around 5.5 percent that should be replaced with the official figure for any precise statement.

Several specific numbers here are reported or implied rather than independently confirmed and should be checked against the official Ministry of Finance documents: last year's revised estimate (cited as roughly NPR 1,700 billion, the figure implied by the official 25.2 percent comparison) and the exact size of GDP (cited as on the order of NPR 6 trillion). The budget's share of GDP is given as "roughly a third" deliberately, because the precise percentage depends on the exact GDP figure and should be computed from the official data rather than asserted. The per-person figure of about NPR 70,000 uses a round population of thirty million.

And, as ever: a budget total is an announced intention, not an enacted certainty, and still less an implemented result. The largest number in this budget is the one that does not appear in it — the share that will actually be spent — and that number will be written, months from now, by the machinery of execution and the pen of the auditor, not by the speech.
