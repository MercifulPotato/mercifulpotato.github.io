## Part 1: The Investment Process as the Core of the Institution

Everything else the law does — establishing the fund, setting up the board, appointing the CEO, authorizing financial instruments, establishing governance rules — exists to enable one thing: good investment decisions. Money must flow from the fund into infrastructure projects that generate economic returns, create jobs, advance development, and repay the investment.

The quality of the investment process is therefore the most operationally important element of the entire institution. An institution with excellent governance but a poor investment process will either fail to invest (unable to find or structure viable projects) or invest badly (putting money into projects that fail, losing capital, and damaging the institution's credibility and financial health). Sections 22 through 27 of the Act set out the investment framework.

## Part 2: What the Fund Can Do When It Invests

Section 22 establishes the range of investment instruments the fund can use. When the fund determines that a project is suitable for investment, it can apply any or all of the following approaches:

**Share purchase (equity investment):** Buying shares in the project company, becoming a co-owner of the project. As discussed in the financial instruments article, this gives the fund upside participation but also means it bears losses in a bad scenario.

**Debt (lending):** Providing a loan to the project company, which must be repaid with interest over an agreed schedule. Loans are typically secured by collateral and carry less risk than equity, but also have lower upside.

**Combined equity and debt:** Investing in a project through both a loan and an equity stake — a structure sometimes called a "mezzanine" investment or a "quasi-equity" structure. This combination allows the fund to participate in project upside (through the equity) while maintaining some debt-like protection (the loan is senior to the equity in a default scenario).

**Special guarantees:** Rather than directly investing cash, the fund can provide a guarantee — committing to cover the project's debt obligations up to a specified amount if the project defaults. This allows the fund to catalyze more investment without deploying its own cash.

**Other financial instruments as prescribed:** The catch-all provision allows the fund to use any additional instruments authorized in the subordinate regulations.

The Act specifies that loans made by the fund will be subject to supervision and monitoring by Nepal Rastra Bank (the central bank), which can issue directives as needed, and that the fund is obligated to follow such directives. This provision places the fund within the central bank's oversight framework for financial sector lending — an appropriate safeguard ensuring that the fund's lending activities are conducted in accordance with prudent financial practices.

## Part 3: Project Analysis Before Investment

Section 23 establishes the due diligence requirements before any investment can be made. The fund must study any proposed project across the following dimensions:

**Overall economic and financial strength of the project:** Is the project's business case sound? Does it make economic sense? Are the revenue projections realistic? Are the cost estimates credible? This is the basic financial feasibility analysis.

**Feasibility of high returns:** Can this project actually generate returns above the fund's hurdle rate — the minimum return the fund requires to justify an investment? Projects that look attractive but have optimistic revenue assumptions may fail this test under more conservative analysis.

**Capacity to attract more private investment:** One of the fund's key objectives is "additionality" — using public investment to leverage larger amounts of private investment. The analysis must assess whether the fund's investment in this project will encourage private investors to co-invest who would not otherwise invest. If a project would attract the same private investment without the fund's involvement, the fund's participation adds less value.

**Contribution to sustainable development and overall economic and social development:** Infrastructure projects are justified not just by their direct financial returns but by their broader development impact — jobs created, communities served, economic linkages enabled, social services improved. The analysis must assess these broader effects.

**Capacity to expand employment opportunities:** Job creation is a specific and explicit criterion in the Act. The fund is not purely a commercial investor; it is a development finance institution with an employment mandate. Projects that generate significant employment — construction jobs, operational jobs, induced employment in linked sectors — score better on this criterion.

**Use of state-of-the-art technology:** The Act specifically requires assessment of whether the project uses the most advanced available technology. This criterion reflects an awareness that infrastructure built with outdated technology will have shorter useful lives and lower operational efficiency. Nepal should not be building infrastructure that will be obsolete within a decade.

### Independent Risk Assessment

In addition to its own analysis, the fund must also arrange for a separate, independent risk assessment of each project through a different structure, institution, or team. This two-track analysis — internal assessment plus independent risk assessment — is a standard safeguard in serious project finance practice.

The logic is straightforward: those doing the internal analysis may develop a bias toward projects they have spent time evaluating (the sunk cost fallacy — "we've spent six months on this, we can't say no now"). An independent assessor who has not been involved in developing the investment thesis is better positioned to identify risks that internal analysts may have minimized or missed.

If both the internal analysis and the independent risk assessment support investment, the fund can proceed to approve investment in high-return, long-term projects with minimum risk.

## Part 4: Collateral Requirements

Section 23, Sub-section 4 addresses collateral — the assets pledged as security for a loan. When the fund provides a loan, it must take adequate collateral. If the available collateral is insufficient to fully secure the loan, the fund must take a security interest in the entire project as collateral.

This provision has significant practical implications. Project finance often involves limited recourse financing — the only security for the loan is the project itself (its assets and revenues). If the project fails, lenders can seize and sell the project assets but have no claim on the owners' other assets. This is different from corporate loans, where the borrower's entire balance sheet is typically available as collateral.

For infrastructure projects in Nepal — a road, a power plant, a cable car — the "collateral" is the project's physical assets (the infrastructure itself) and its revenue streams (tolls, electricity sales contracts, lease payments). These assets are often illiquid — you cannot sell a hydropower dam on the open market to recover a defaulted loan — and their value depends heavily on continued operation and the regulatory environment.

The requirement to take adequate collateral, and to take a lien on the entire project if specific assets are insufficient, is a prudent lending discipline. But it must be implemented thoughtfully, as overly rigid collateral requirements could prevent genuinely viable projects from accessing financing simply because their assets do not fit standard collateral structures.

## Part 5: Pre-Investment Conditions — What Projects Must Commit To

Section 24 specifies conditions that a project must fulfill before it can begin receiving investment from the fund. These are pre-investment conditions (conditions precedent, in legal terminology) — the project must demonstrate compliance with these conditions before the money flows.

**Environmental standards compliance:** The project must commit to compliance with applicable environmental standards and to using environmentally friendly construction materials and technology. Nepal has an Environmental Protection Act and associated regulations that require environmental impact assessments for significant projects. Investment from the fund is explicitly conditioned on these environmental requirements being met.

**Institutional governance:** The project must establish good corporate governance — appropriate management structures, accountability mechanisms, and reporting systems. An infrastructure company that does not have adequate governance is a higher risk for the fund.

**Risk analysis, market study, cost-benefit analysis, and evaluation mechanisms:** The project must have established systems for analyzing its risks, understanding its market, assessing costs against benefits, and evaluating performance over time.

**Revenue projection and evaluation system:** The project must have credible mechanisms for projecting and tracking the revenues it will generate.

Once investment begins, the project must maintain certain ongoing conditions under Section 24, Sub-section 2:

**Periodic technical, commercial, and environmental assessment:** Regular (typically annual or more frequent) evaluation of the project's technical performance, commercial performance, and environmental compliance. The fund receives these reports and can use them to monitor the project's health.

**Risk mitigation provisions:** The project must maintain adequate risk management systems and insurance coverage.

**Coordination with public, private, and local community sectors:** Infrastructure projects affect many stakeholders. The project must coordinate with relevant government bodies, the private sector, and the communities in which it operates. Community consultation and benefit-sharing are increasingly recognized as essential for infrastructure project sustainability, particularly in Nepal where many hydropower projects have faced community opposition when local communities felt excluded from the benefits.

**Other conditions as prescribed:** A catch-all provision for additional conditions specified in subordinate regulations.

These pre- and post-investment conditions are standard in project finance globally. The discipline they impose — requiring environmental assessments, governance structures, risk management systems, and ongoing reporting — significantly improves the quality and sustainability of financed projects compared to informal or poorly documented financing arrangements.

## Part 6: The Guarantee Process

Section 25 specifies the guarantee provisions in detail. This is one of the most operationally complex parts of the investment framework.

When a project needs a guarantee to secure financing (from a bank, from the bond market, from international investors), the implementing institution can request a guarantee from the fund. The fund can then either:

- Provide the guarantee itself; or
- Recommend through the Finance Ministry that the Nepal government or an international financial institution provide the guarantee

Before providing or recommending a guarantee, the fund must verify:

- The fund's own analysis supports the guarantee (the project's returns and risk profile justify it)
- The relevant sector ministry has recommended the project
- The project is in the National Project Bank
- If a PPP project, the Investment Board Nepal has recommended it

If the Nepal government or an international financial institution agrees to provide a guarantee based on the fund's recommendation, the fund can then use alternative development finance instruments to channel investment to the project.

When a guarantee is granted, the fund must notify:
- The Public Debt Management Office (for government guarantees)
- The relevant financial institution (for institutional guarantees)

The Act explicitly states that nothing in this section limits the government's existing legal authority to provide guarantees — it is additive, not restrictive.

The detailed record-keeping, notification, and settlement procedures for guarantees are to be specified in subordinate regulations.

### Why Guarantees Are Both Powerful and Risky

Guarantees are among the most powerful tools in development finance — and among the most dangerous if misused.

Powerful because: a small guarantee amount can unlock much larger investment. If the fund provides a guarantee of Rs. 500 million on a Rs. 5 billion project, it is covering 10% of the risk but enabling 100% of the investment. Without the guarantee, commercial lenders might not participate at all.

Dangerous because: guarantees are contingent liabilities — they do not appear as a cash outflow on the fund's balance sheet until they are called. This makes it easy to accumulate guarantee obligations without feeling the immediate financial discipline of cash deployment. If many guarantees are called simultaneously — in an economic downturn or a wave of project failures — the fund (and the government) could face sudden, large payment obligations that were not fully anticipated in their risk models.

The history of guarantee programs in developing countries includes many cases where guarantees were extended too liberally, were called in unexpectedly large numbers during economic downturns, and created fiscal crises. Nepal's government has had experience with infrastructure project guarantees going wrong — power purchase agreements with guaranteed minimums that proved unaffordable when the electricity market changed.

The Act's requirement that guarantees be based on rigorous analysis and prior approval from government or international institutions is a prudent safeguard. But the implementation of these safeguards — the quality of the analysis, the rigor of the approval process, the conservatism of the assumptions — will ultimately determine whether the guarantee program is well-managed or becomes a source of fiscal risk.

## Part 7: Recovery — Getting Money Back When Projects Fail

Section 26 addresses what happens when a project fails to meet its investment obligations. This is an uncomfortable topic — it implies failure — but a sound investment framework must plan for failures, because some investments will inevitably fail.

The fund can demand immediate repayment of invested capital (and suspend further disbursements) in the following circumstances:

- The project fails to comply with investment conditions (those specified under Section 24)
- Investment proceeds are used for purposes other than the project — diverted to other uses, siphoned off, or misappropriated
- False documentation or misrepresentation was used to obtain the investment
- Without reasonable cause, the project fails to maintain investment timelines or implement the project as planned
- Another project with fund loans outstanding has failed to make installment payments as scheduled

When repayment is demanded, all outstanding loan principal, interest, service charges, and fees become immediately due. If the borrower does not repay, the fund follows prescribed procedures, takes control of the project, and can transfer it to another implementing entity to ensure continuity.

This recovery framework is essential. Without it, borrowers who face financial difficulty might simply choose not to repay, knowing the fund has no teeth. With it, the fund has legal authority and procedural mechanisms to enforce repayment and, if necessary, to take over a project and continue operating or transferring it.

The practical effectiveness of these recovery provisions depends heavily on Nepal's legal system. Contract enforcement — the ability to actually exercise a lien on project assets, to seize and operate a project in default — requires functional courts, clear property rights, and processes that do not drag on for years through appeals and procedural delays. Nepal's legal system has historically had challenges in this area, particularly for complex commercial disputes.

## Part 8: Collateral Priority — The Fund Always Gets Paid First

Section 27 establishes the fund's priority rights on collateral. This is important both for the fund's financial security and for the fund's relationships with other lenders.

**The fund has first priority on collateral.** When a project borrows from the fund and pledges assets as collateral, the fund has first priority over those assets — it gets paid from the collateral assets before any other creditor. No agreement, contract, commitment, or arrangement that gives another party equal or prior rights over the collateral is valid unless the fund explicitly consents to it.

**Any arrangement purporting to give another party prior rights is automatically void.** This is strong language — it means that even if a project company tries to agree with another lender that the other lender will have first priority, that agreement is legally meaningless as far as the fund is concerned.

**Exception for consortium finance:** When multiple lenders are financing a project together (consortium or syndicated finance), with the fund taking a lead or participating role, all lenders have equal priority on the collateral. This is sensible — it makes consortium arrangements viable by ensuring that co-lenders do not face being subordinated to the fund in every case.

First priority on collateral is standard for senior secured lenders in commercial project finance. But establishing it explicitly in the statute — rather than relying on contract law — provides additional legal certainty, which is particularly important in a developing legal system where contract enforcement can be uncertain.

## Part 9: The Investment Process in Practice — What This Means

Putting all of these provisions together, the investment process for a typical project would look something like this:

**Step 1: Project identification.** The fund's investment team identifies a potential project — perhaps from the National Project Bank, perhaps proposed by a project developer, perhaps through the fund's own sector analysis.

**Step 2: Initial screening.** The investment team does a quick preliminary assessment to determine whether the project falls within the eligible sectors, meets the minimum cost threshold, and has basic feasibility.

**Step 3: Due diligence.** For projects that pass initial screening, a detailed due diligence process begins. The fund's analysts examine financials, technical specifications, environmental and social impacts, regulatory status, land acquisition, counterparty creditworthiness, and other factors. An independent risk assessment is commissioned.

**Step 4: Investment Sub-Committee review.** The Investment Sub-Committee of the board reviews the due diligence findings and the investment team's recommendation. The sub-committee asks hard questions, requires additional information if needed, and produces its own recommendation.

**Step 5: Board approval.** The full board reviews the Investment Sub-Committee's recommendation and votes on whether to approve the investment. The CEO presents the proposal. The board can approve as proposed, approve with conditions, or reject.

**Step 6: Condition satisfaction.** If approved, the project must satisfy all pre-investment conditions (environmental compliance, governance structure, risk management systems, etc.) before money is disbursed.

**Step 7: Legal documentation.** The fund's lawyers prepare the investment agreement — the loan agreement, the equity subscription agreement, the guarantee documentation, as relevant. These documents specify all terms, conditions, obligations, and remedies.

**Step 8: Disbursement.** Money is disbursed according to the agreed schedule, typically in tranches linked to project milestones.

**Step 9: Ongoing monitoring.** The fund monitors the project's technical, financial, and environmental performance. It receives periodic reports, conducts site visits, and can intervene if performance deteriorates.

**Step 10: Repayment and exit.** Loan principal and interest is repaid according to the agreed schedule. Equity investments may eventually be sold (an "exit") if the fund decides to realize its gains and redeploy capital.

This process, if implemented rigorously, creates a disciplined investment machine that can consistently identify, analyze, approve, and monitor infrastructure investments. If implemented poorly — with shortcuts, political pressure, inadequate analysis, or weak monitoring — it becomes a formality that masks bad investments.
