There is a phrase that Nepali politicians, engineers, and development economists have been repeating for at least four decades: Nepal is sitting on white gold. The white gold is water — specifically, water falling from the Himalayas through the steep gorges of dozens of major river systems, dropping thousands of meters in elevation over relatively short distances and generating, in that fall, the potential for electricity on a scale that would be transformative not just for Nepal but for the entire Indian subcontinent. The phrase has been repeated so often, and the transformation has arrived so slowly, that it has become something of a national sorrow. The gold is there. The country remains poor. Understanding why that gap exists, and whether the Alternative Development Finance Fund can help close it, requires understanding hydropower from first principles — the physics, the economics, the politics, and the long, complicated history of a sector that has been perpetually almost ready to change everything.

**The Physics of Falling Water**

Hydropower is, at its core, the conversion of gravitational potential energy into electrical energy. Water at a high elevation contains energy by virtue of its height. When that water is allowed to fall — whether through a natural waterfall or a controlled tunnel bored through rock — it accelerates. The accelerating water spins a turbine. The turbine drives a generator. The generator produces electricity. The amount of electricity produced depends on two variables: how much water is flowing, and how far it falls. Engineers call the distance of the fall "head," and the formula for power output is essentially head multiplied by flow multiplied by a set of efficiency constants. Nepal has extraordinary head. The Himalayas, the world's highest mountain range, rise from the Terai plains at roughly 100 meters above sea level to peaks above 8,000 meters within a horizontal distance of less than 200 kilometers. No other river system on Earth concentrates so much elevation change over such a short distance. The result is that Nepali rivers carry a disproportionate amount of gravitational potential energy relative to their flow volumes. A river that might produce modest power in, say, Germany can produce enormous power in Nepal simply because it is falling so much farther.

The rivers themselves originate in one of two ways. Some are glacier-fed, drawing their water from the permanent ice fields of the high Himalayas. These rivers run year-round, with relatively stable if seasonal flows, and they are less vulnerable to drought. Others are monsoon-fed, drawing primarily from the intense rainfall that drenches Nepal's hills from June through September. These rivers swell dramatically during the monsoon season and can shrink to a fraction of their peak flow during the dry winter months. The distinction matters enormously for power project design. A glacier-fed river with stable dry-season flow is a better candidate for a run-of-river project — one that simply diverts water through a turbine and releases it downstream without significant storage. A monsoon-fed river with dramatic seasonal variation often requires a storage reservoir to capture wet-season excess and release it during the dry season, producing power more evenly across the year. Storage projects are significantly more valuable commercially because they can produce power when it is most needed, but they are also vastly more expensive to build and environmentally more complicated, because they flood valleys.

The government's own technical assessments have consistently estimated Nepal's hydropower potential at somewhere between 40,000 and 83,000 megawatts of technically exploitable capacity, depending on the assumptions used. Even the lower figure would represent roughly forty times Nepal's current installed capacity. For context, the entire electricity generation capacity of the United Kingdom is approximately 100,000 megawatts, serving a population of 67 million. Nepal, with a population of roughly 30 million, has potential capacity of nearly that magnitude sitting largely undeveloped in its rivers. The comparison is imperfect — electricity demand, transmission infrastructure, and geography all differ — but it conveys the scale of the opportunity that has, for generations, been visible but mostly out of reach.

**What Has Been Built So Far**

Nepal's current installed hydropower capacity, as of the mid-2020s, stands at roughly 2,800 to 3,000 megawatts. This is a significant improvement over just a decade earlier, when the country was managing barely 700 megawatts and enduring daily load-shedding of up to eighteen hours in Kathmandu. The decade from 2014 to 2024 represented the most productive period of hydropower development in Nepali history, driven by a combination of government priority, private sector investment, and the completion of several large projects that had been under development for years.

The anchor of the system is the Upper Tamakoshi Hydroelectric Project, a 456-megawatt run-of-river plant on the Tamakoshi River in Dolakha district. Upper Tamakoshi was a landmark for multiple reasons. It was developed primarily with Nepali capital — a departure from the pattern of large projects requiring foreign financing — through a consortium led by the Nepal Electricity Authority and supported by domestic employee provident funds, meaning that ordinary Nepali workers who contributed to their pension funds were literally owning shares in the country's largest power plant. The project took far longer to complete than planned, suffering from the 2015 earthquake, difficult terrain, and the inevitable complications of building major civil infrastructure in remote Himalayan geography, but when it finally commissioned in 2021 it made a material difference to the national grid. The Upper Tamakoshi story is significant not just technically but symbolically: it demonstrated that Nepal could finance, build, and operate large hydropower infrastructure without depending entirely on foreign governments or multilateral development banks.

Other significant existing projects include the Kulekhani storage facility — two reservoirs and two powerhouses totaling about 106 megawatts — which remains strategically important because it is a storage project capable of providing peak power during dry months. Kulekhani was built with World Bank and Japanese financing in the 1980s and has been operated by the Nepal Electricity Authority since commissioning. Its importance is disproportionate to its nameplate capacity because it can be dispatched on demand rather than being dependent on river flow. The Middle Marsyangdi, the Kaligandaki A, and a series of smaller projects round out the existing portfolio, along with an increasingly significant contribution from a new wave of small and medium private sector plants that came online during the 2014–2024 development surge.

Nepal crossed a remarkable threshold in 2022 and 2023: for the first time in the country's history, domestic generation capacity exceeded peak domestic demand. Nepal was no longer a country perpetually short of electricity. It was, for certain seasons and hours, producing surplus power. This surplus was immediately recognized as both an opportunity and a problem. The opportunity was export — selling power to India, which has enormous electricity demand and runs chronic shortfalls particularly in northern states bordering Nepal. The problem was infrastructure — the transmission interconnections with India were limited, the commercial and regulatory frameworks for cross-border power trading were still being negotiated and refined, and the Nepal Electricity Authority, which operates the national grid, was simultaneously managing rapid demand growth as electrification of cooking and transportation accelerated and more Nepali households connected to the grid.

**The Pipeline: What Could Be Built**

The projects currently under development or in advanced planning represent a staggering volume of potential new generation. The 900-megawatt Arun III project, developed by India's SJVN Limited under a build-own-operate-transfer arrangement, is the single largest project currently under active construction. Arun III is located in the Sankhuwasabha district in eastern Nepal on the Arun River, one of Nepal's great rivers that originates in Tibet. When completed, it will roughly double Nepal's installed capacity in a single project. The involvement of an Indian state-owned enterprise is not coincidental — India has been the dominant foreign investor in Nepali hydropower for geographic, political, and commercial reasons that will be discussed further below.

Beyond Arun III, the pipeline includes projects of varying scale and development status. The 1,200-megawatt Budhigandaki storage project has been debated, studied, and partially prepared for decades. It would involve a major reservoir in Gorkha and Dhading districts, flooding significant valley areas and requiring large-scale displacement and resettlement. Its development has been repeatedly delayed by disputes over the appropriate model — should it be developed by Nepal's government alone, by a joint venture, through international bidding? — and the costs of resettlement have made private investors hesitant. Budhigandaki represents the category of large storage projects that Nepal most needs for year-round power security but that are most difficult to finance and develop given their social and environmental footprint.

The Tamakoshi V, Dudhkoshi storage project, Upper Arun, Nalgad, and a long list of smaller projects fill out a development pipeline that, if fully realized over the coming fifteen to twenty years, would take Nepal's installed capacity to somewhere between 15,000 and 20,000 megawatts — enough not just to satisfy all domestic demand including ambitious electrification goals, but to become a substantial net exporter to South Asia.

The gap between this pipeline and what actually gets built comes down, almost entirely, to financing. The physical potential is real. The engineering knowledge to develop it exists. The market for the power — particularly in India and the emerging interconnected South Asian electricity market — is genuine. What stands between Nepal and the realization of its hydropower potential is the extremely large amount of capital required, the long construction timelines before any revenue is generated, the sovereign risk that makes international private capital cautious, and the institutional capacity required to develop, finance, and manage projects of this complexity. This is precisely where the Alternative Development Finance Fund is designed to intervene.

**The Economics of Hydropower Financing**

Building a large hydropower project is one of the more capital-intensive activities in the infrastructure world. A large run-of-river project in Nepal costs somewhere between 2,000 and 3,500 US dollars per installed kilowatt of capacity, depending on size, location, and site conditions. Storage projects with dams can cost considerably more. A 500-megawatt project — a substantial but not enormous plant by international standards — thus requires a capital investment of roughly one to two billion US dollars. That is, to put it in Nepali terms, somewhere between 100 and 200 billion Nepali rupees, at a time when Nepal's total annual government budget is in the range of 1,600 to 1,800 billion rupees. A single large hydropower project can represent between six and twelve percent of the entire national budget.

This scale of capital requirement has several consequences. First, it means that any individual project requires financing from multiple sources — equity from project sponsors, long-term debt from commercial banks, and often concessional loans from development finance institutions that can provide the long tenors that commercial banks are reluctant to offer. A typical hydropower project finance structure involves equity making up perhaps 20 to 30 percent of the project cost, with the remainder coming from debt. The equity investors take the development risk and earn returns from the project's power sales. The debt providers take a more secure position, receiving interest payments before equity investors take any profits, but they require certainty about the project's cash flows — which means they require power purchase agreements before they will lend.

Power purchase agreements — contracts in which a utility or another buyer commits in advance to purchase the project's output at a specified price for a specified period — are the financial backbone of hydropower project development. Without a power purchase agreement, no lender will finance the project, because without one there is no certainty about revenue. With a good power purchase agreement, the project's revenue stream becomes predictable enough that lenders can model their debt service coverage ratios and decide whether to lend. The Nepal Electricity Authority has traditionally been the domestic power purchaser, signing agreements for projects targeting the domestic market. For export projects, the power purchase agreement must involve a buyer in the importing country — which in Nepal's case primarily means Indian utilities or the Indian national grid. The India connection is thus not merely political but structural to the entire financing chain.

**India: The Essential Partner and the Complicated Neighbor**

India's relationship with Nepal's hydropower sector is one of the defining dynamics of the entire industry. India is simultaneously Nepal's largest neighbor, a country that shares both Himalayan rivers and cultural and linguistic heritage with Nepal, a dominant economic partner that handles roughly two-thirds of Nepal's trade, and an investor in and buyer of Nepali electricity whose involvement is essentially indispensable for large-scale hydropower development at the moment.

From India's perspective, Nepal represents an attractive source of clean, reliable hydropower for the northern Indian states — Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal, and others — that face chronic electricity shortages and where the alternative is building more thermal power capacity, which is both expensive and politically complicated given India's climate commitments. The proximity of the generating stations to demand centers in northern India keeps transmission losses manageable and the economics of cross-border power trade relatively favorable. Indian state-owned enterprises like SJVN and NHPC have been active developers of Nepali hydropower through investment agreements that give them build-own-operate-transfer rights, meaning they develop and finance the project, operate it for an agreed period, and then transfer ownership to Nepal. SJVN's development of Arun III is the largest current example.

From Nepal's perspective, Indian investment in hydropower development is welcomed but also viewed with some ambivalence. The concern, long present in Nepali political discourse, is that arrangements that give Indian companies long operating periods and favorable power purchase terms may not represent the best deal for Nepal's long-term interests. The BOOT model means that while Nepal will eventually own a major plant, it will not receive full control of that asset for decades, and the terms of the power purchase agreement — what price India pays for the power — are negotiated in a context where India has significant leverage given Nepal's dependence on it as a market and a financing source. A more financially independent Nepal, with its own development finance institution capable of co-investing or providing equity support, would be in a considerably stronger negotiating position. This is one of the strategic rationales for the Alternative Development Finance Fund that is rarely stated explicitly in the text of the legislation but is understood by anyone who follows the sector.

The bilateral India-Nepal power trade framework has evolved considerably, particularly after a 2014 power trade agreement and its subsequent amendments, and most recently through negotiations around the Long-Term Power Trade Agreement that has been under discussion for several years. Nepal has been pushing to increase cross-border transmission capacity, to be able to sell power to Indian private buyers and not only to government utilities, and to receive prices that reflect the competitive value of hydropower in the Indian market rather than prices set in bilateral negotiations that may not fully capture that value. Progress has been made but the negotiations are ongoing, and the outcome will shape the economics of an entire generation of Nepali hydropower projects.

**China: The Other Presence**

China's role in Nepali hydropower is less dominant than India's but increasingly visible. Chinese construction companies have been involved in several major projects as engineering, procurement, and construction contractors, offering competitive pricing and financing packages that sometimes include Chinese policy bank loans. The Nepal-China relationship in infrastructure more broadly is evolving under the Belt and Road Initiative framework, with which Nepal formally associated itself in 2017 through the signing of a memorandum of understanding, though the actual implementation of specific BRI projects in Nepal has been much slower than the headline signing suggested.

In hydropower specifically, Chinese companies have been involved in projects including the Upper Marsyangdi A, where Sino Hydro served as the primary contractor, and several other mid-size projects. The attraction of Chinese involvement is primarily the competitive pricing of Chinese construction contractors, whose cost structures reflect both state backing and massive scale from decades of domestic dam-building in China. The concern from Nepal's perspective — and from India's perspective, given the geopolitical sensitivities of having a Chinese footprint on projects that generate power sold into India — is a complex set of considerations around technology transfer, debt terms, and the strategic implications of Chinese companies holding significant infrastructure positions in a country that sits between China and India.

Nepal has, under various governments, attempted to manage the India-China dynamic in hydropower by being careful about which projects are assigned to which investors, maintaining relationships with both neighbors, and using the competition between them to some extent as leverage in negotiations. The Alternative Development Finance Fund, by providing Nepal with its own capital and financing capacity, modestly improves Nepal's position in this diplomatic-commercial balancing act — a Nepal that can contribute its own capital to project development is less dependent on any single external partner.

**How the ADF Fund Fits In**

The Alternative Development Finance Fund is not a hydropower-specific institution. Its investment mandate covers energy, roads, airports, railways, and urban infrastructure broadly. But in practice, hydropower is likely to be the single most significant sector for the fund's investments, for the simple reason that hydropower represents Nepal's largest infrastructure opportunity and its greatest financing gap.

The fund's authorized capital of 100 billion rupees and initial paid-up capital of 25 billion rupees is a meaningful but not transformative sum in the context of Nepal's hydropower pipeline. A single large storage project can cost two to four times the fund's initial capital. The fund's role, realistically, is therefore not to be the primary financier of any single very large project, but rather to play targeted roles — providing equity co-investment alongside other investors, offering long-term concessional debt to projects that commercial banks will not finance over the required timelines, providing guarantee instruments that allow projects to access commercial financing at better terms, and blending with multilateral development bank capital to make projects viable that would otherwise not attract private investment. In project finance language, the fund is designed to be a critical but partial contributor to each deal's financing structure, not the sole source of capital.

The fund's governance structure, discussed in earlier installments of this series, gives it the institutional credibility and independence needed to be a trusted co-investor alongside multilateral development banks, commercial lenders, and private equity investors. This is not trivial. International development finance institutions are cautious about their co-investors and will not partner with entities whose governance they consider suspect. A fund chaired by the Finance Ministry secretary, with a CEO selected through the Civil Service Commission, with annual Auditor General oversight and triennial international independent evaluation, is a credibly governed institution in the eyes of the development finance community. That credibility is what allows the fund to unlock co-financing that Nepal would otherwise not have access to.

The specific financial instruments authorized under the legislation — equity investment, bonds, guarantees, blended finance structures, remittance bonds, and asset monetization — correspond precisely to the financing needs of the hydropower sector. Equity investment allows the fund to take ownership stakes in projects. Guarantee instruments allow the fund to backstop project obligations, making commercial lenders more willing to provide debt. Blended finance structures allow the fund to use its concessional capital to subsidize certain project costs, bringing down the overall cost of capital to the point where projects become commercially viable. The remittance fund mechanism — mobilizing capital from the Nepali diaspora abroad — represents a potentially large and underutilized source of patient capital that, if effectively structured, could provide the fund with resources well beyond its initial government contribution.
