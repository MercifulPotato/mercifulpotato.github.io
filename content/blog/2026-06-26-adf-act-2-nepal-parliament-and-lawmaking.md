## Part 1: The Country as a Political System

In the previous article, we described Nepal primarily as a geographic and economic entity. Now we need to describe it as a political and legal entity — because the Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 is, above all else, a piece of legislation. It is law. And to understand what that means — what it can do, what it cannot do, who is bound by it, and how it relates to all the other laws and institutions of the state — we need to understand how Nepal governs itself.

Let us start from the very beginning.

### What a State Is

A state, in the political science sense, is an organized human community that exercises authority over a defined territory. That authority includes the power to make and enforce laws, to collect taxes, to deploy armed forces, to conduct foreign relations, and to maintain order. A state is distinct from a nation (a cultural or ethnic community), a government (the people currently in charge of running the state), or a country (a geographic term). When we say "Nepal is a state," we mean that Nepal has a recognized government, a defined territory, a legal system, and the capacity — however imperfect — to govern itself.

Nepal is a sovereign state, meaning its government is the ultimate authority within its borders and is not formally subordinate to any other government. It is a member of the United Nations, recognized by all major countries, and maintains embassies and diplomatic relations around the world.

### What Kind of State Nepal Is: A Federal Democratic Republic

Nepal's constitution, adopted in 2015 after years of political transition following the end of the civil war, defines the country as a "Federal Democratic Republic." Each of these three words matters.

**Federal** means the government is divided between a central (or federal) government and sub-national (or subnational) governments that have their own authority. Before 2015, Nepal was a unitary state — all governmental power resided in Kathmandu. The 2015 constitution created a federal structure with three tiers of government: the federal government, seven provincial governments, and approximately 753 local governments (municipalities and rural municipalities). Each tier has its own elected bodies and its own defined areas of responsibility. Infrastructure development of national significance is primarily a federal responsibility. Local service delivery — schools, health posts, local roads — is primarily a local government responsibility. Provinces have responsibilities somewhere in between.

This federal structure is relevant to the Alternative Development Finance Act because the fund it creates operates at the federal level, but the infrastructure it will finance — roads, electricity, water systems — affects all three levels of government and many communities across the country. When we ask questions like "who gets to decide which projects get funded?" and "how does the fund interact with provincial and local governments?", the federal structure of the state is the necessary background.

**Democratic** means the government derives its authority from elections, operates under a constitution that limits its powers, and is accountable to the people through regular elections, freedom of speech and press, and an independent judiciary. Nepal's democracy is imperfect — there is significant corruption, the media environment has pressures, and institutions are still developing — but the basic democratic structure is in place. Governments are elected. Elections are held. Governments lose elections and leave power. Courts operate with some degree of independence. These are not small things.

**Republic** means there is no monarch. Nepal had a monarchy — often described as one of the world's only Hindu kingdoms — for centuries. The last king, Gyanendra Shah, was removed from power in 2006 after the People's Movement (Jana Andolan) forced him to relinquish executive authority, and the monarchy was formally abolished by the Constituent Assembly in 2008. Nepal is now governed by elected officials, with a ceremonial President as the constitutional head of state.

### The Three Branches of Government

Like most modern democracies, Nepal's constitution establishes three branches of government: the legislature (which makes laws), the executive (which implements laws and runs the government), and the judiciary (which interprets laws and resolves disputes).

**The Legislature: The Federal Parliament**

The Federal Parliament of Nepal is bicameral — it has two chambers. This is a common structure in federal systems, where one chamber represents the population directly and the other chamber represents the constituent units (provinces or states).

The lower house is called the **House of Representatives** (Pratinidhi Sabha). It has 275 members. Of these, 165 are directly elected from single-member constituencies using a first-past-the-post system (the candidate with the most votes in a defined geographic constituency wins). The remaining 110 are elected through a proportional representation system, where voters choose parties and seats are allocated in proportion to vote shares, with reserved seats for women, Dalits, indigenous nationalities, Madhesi communities, Tharu communities, Muslims, backward regions, and minorities. The House of Representatives is the more powerful of the two chambers — it is where most legislation is debated and passed, and the government (the Council of Ministers) is accountable to it. If the House passes a vote of no confidence in the government, the government must resign.

The upper house is called the **National Assembly** (Rashtriya Sabha). It has 59 members. Of these, 56 are elected by provincial assemblies and local governments (eight from each province), and three are nominated by the President. The National Assembly has a lesser role in ordinary legislation but serves as a check on the House of Representatives and is particularly important for constitutional amendments and for legislation affecting the provinces.

**The Executive: The Cabinet and the Prime Minister**

The head of government in Nepal is the Prime Minister. The Prime Minister is the leader of the party or coalition that commands a majority in the House of Representatives. The Prime Minister appoints a Council of Ministers (cabinet), which is responsible for running the government day-to-day.

The government — the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers — is the entity that proposes most legislation to the parliament. A "government bill" (sarkari vidheyak) is a bill introduced to parliament by the government. The Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 is a government bill, introduced by the Finance Ministry under Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister Vishnu Prasad Paudel. This means it carries the official policy commitment of the government. It was not an idea from a backbench member of parliament; it was a deliberate, planned piece of financial architecture designed by the executive branch and submitted to parliament for approval.

The Council of Ministers meets as a Cabinet. The notes at the beginning of the Act state that the Cabinet approved the submission of this bill to the Federal Parliament on the 25th day of the 12th month of BS 2081. Cabinet approval is a formal, minuted decision of the highest executive body in the state. It means the government as a whole — not just the Finance Ministry — endorsed this bill and committed to advocate for its passage.

**The Judiciary**

The judiciary is not directly involved in making legislation. Its role becomes relevant when laws are challenged as unconstitutional (the Supreme Court has the power of judicial review), when disputes arise about the interpretation of laws, and when the enforcement mechanisms in laws — like the criminal penalties in the Alternative Development Finance Act — are exercised. We will discuss the judicial dimensions of this law in later articles in this series.

## Part 2: How a Bill Becomes a Law in Nepal

The legislative process in Nepal follows a path that will be familiar to anyone who has studied parliamentary democracies. Let us trace the journey of a government bill from conception to enactment.

### Conception and Drafting

The process begins inside the relevant ministry. In this case, the Finance Ministry would have identified the need for a law on alternative development finance, potentially drawing on:
- Studies and recommendations by the National Planning Commission
- Advice from multilateral institutions like the World Bank or Asian Development Bank
- Research by ministry economists and legal experts
- Consultations with the private sector, financial institutions, and other stakeholders
- International precedents from countries that have established similar institutions

A drafting team — typically lawyers and economists within the ministry — would produce initial drafts of the bill. These drafts go through multiple rounds of internal review. Other relevant ministries are consulted — in this case, the Ministry of Infrastructure, the Ministry of Energy, the Ministry of Industry, and others would all have views on a law about development finance for infrastructure. Nepal Rastra Bank (the central bank) would also be consulted, since the fund will operate in the financial sector under central bank oversight.

Once the draft is mature, it is submitted to the Ministry of Law, Justice, and Parliamentary Affairs for legal vetting. The law ministry checks that the bill is consistent with the constitution and with other laws. It checks the drafting for errors, inconsistencies, and ambiguities. It may suggest revisions.

The revised draft then goes to the Cabinet — the full Council of Ministers — for approval. Cabinet approval is the decisive executive step. When the Cabinet approves a bill, it authorizes the Finance Minister to introduce it formally in parliament and commits the government to advocate for its passage.

### Introduction in Parliament

A government bill is typically introduced first in the House of Representatives, the lower and more powerful chamber. The Finance Minister formally introduces the bill in the House, presenting its objectives and major provisions in a speech.

The bill is then distributed to all members of parliament. It is published in the Nepal Gazette (the official government publication where laws and regulations are formally notified).

### Committee Stage

The House then refers the bill to a relevant committee for detailed examination. In the case of a finance bill, this would be the Finance Committee of the House of Representatives (though the specific committee may vary depending on the House's structure at any given time). The committee:

- Studies the bill in detail, section by section
- Invites testimony from experts, stakeholders, and affected parties
- Members ask questions of government ministers and officials
- The committee may recommend amendments to specific provisions

This is the stage where the technical details of the bill get most scrutiny. Committee members who have expertise in finance, economics, or the relevant sectors can probe weaknesses in the drafting, ask about implementation plans, and push for stronger governance safeguards. Committee deliberations are generally recorded, and committee reports are public documents.

### Debate and Vote in the House of Representatives

After the committee reports, the bill returns to the full House for debate. All members can speak. The government defends the bill; opposition members critique it. Amendments can be proposed and voted on. If a majority of the House votes in favor of the bill (as amended), it passes the House.

### Senate (National Assembly) Stage

From the House, the bill goes to the National Assembly. The National Assembly reviews it, can suggest further amendments, and must approve it. If the National Assembly proposes amendments, the bill goes back to the House for reconciliation.

### Authentication and Enactment

Once both chambers have approved the same text, the bill is authenticated by the President of Nepal. Once authenticated, it is a law — an Act (Ain). It is published in the Nepal Gazette, and its provisions take effect according to their own terms.

The Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 specifies, in its very first section, that it will come into force 101 days after authentication. This 101-day delay is deliberately built in to allow the government time to prepare: to begin establishing the institution the law creates, to draft the subordinate regulations (rules and guidelines), and to make the administrative preparations necessary for the law to be implemented effectively from day one.

### Why This Process Matters

This process matters for several reasons. First, it provides legitimacy — a law that has been debated, amended, and approved by an elected parliament has a degree of democratic legitimacy that executive orders or regulations do not. Second, it provides stability — laws are harder to change than regulations or policies, providing some reassurance to investors that the framework will not be upended at the next cabinet meeting. Third, it provides accountability — the parliamentary record shows who said what, who voted for what, and what commitments the government made in defending the bill.

But this process also has limitations. Parliamentary scrutiny in Nepal, as in many democracies, is often shallow. Members of parliament may not have the technical expertise to interrogate a complex financial law. Party discipline means members often vote along party lines regardless of the merits of specific provisions. And the gap between a law's stated objectives and its actual implementation is often wide in Nepal, where institutional capacity and political will to enforce laws consistently can be limited.

## Part 3: The Finance Ministry and Its Role

The Finance Ministry (Artha Mantralaya) is the most powerful and resource-controlling ministry in the government. It prepares the national budget, manages government revenues and expenditures, oversees the financial sector (banks, insurance companies, capital markets), manages government debt, and is responsible for economic policy. It also oversees Nepal Rastra Bank, the country's central bank, although the Rastra Bank has formal statutory independence.

The Alternative Development Finance Act places the new fund squarely within the Finance Ministry's orbit. The Fund's board is chaired by the Secretary of the Finance Ministry. Contact between the Fund and the government is formally conducted through the Finance Ministry. The Ministry has the power to approve the Fund's organizational structure, to agree on the CEO's remuneration, and to issue directives to the Fund. In a practical sense, the Finance Ministry is the "parent" of this new institution.

This creates an important tension that we will return to repeatedly in this series: the Fund is supposed to be autonomous — to operate at arm's length from day-to-day political pressures, to make investment decisions on commercial and technical criteria rather than political ones. But it is also formally under the Finance Ministry's supervision and guidance. The balance between autonomy and accountability — between letting the Fund operate like a sophisticated financial institution and ensuring it remains answerable to elected government — is one of the central design challenges of the entire law.

## Part 4: What Kind of Law This Is — and What It Is Not

The Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 is what lawyers call an "enabling law" or "framework law." It does not, by itself, build a single road or generate a single watt of electricity. It creates the legal machinery — the institution, the instruments, the governance structures, the accountability mechanisms — that will then be used to mobilize investment in infrastructure. The law is the scaffolding within which actual development activity will (hopefully) happen.

This is an important distinction. The quality of an enabling law is not measured by what it promises but by whether the machinery it creates actually works. A law that promises blended finance for renewable energy is meaningless unless the institution it creates can attract investors, identify viable projects, structure deals competently, manage risks prudently, and account for its activities honestly. All of that depends on the design of the institution, the quality of the people running it, the political environment in which it operates, and the broader investment climate of the country.

An enabling law is also not self-implementing. After the act comes into force, the government must draft and pass subordinate regulations — typically called Rules (Niyamawali in Nepali) — that specify the operational details. The Act frequently delegates specific decisions to these rules: how exactly will guarantee processes work? What are the detailed eligibility criteria for projects? How is the CEO performance evaluation structured? All of these details must be resolved in the regulations, and the regulations take time to draft, consult, and finalize.

This means there is always a significant gap — often measured in years — between the passage of an enabling law and its full operational implementation. This is not necessarily a sign of failure; it is simply how complex institutional law works. But it is important context for evaluating the Act's impact. The law was passed in 2081 BS. The institution will not be fully operational immediately. The first investments will not happen immediately. The full impact of the law — if it is positive — may not be visible for several years.

## Part 5: The Context of Previous Attempts

This is not Nepal's first attempt to create a development finance institution or to mobilize alternative financing for infrastructure. The country has a history of establishing such institutions, and that history is instructive.

Nepal has had infrastructure development banks, investment companies, and special purpose vehicles before. Some have worked reasonably well; others have been captured by political interests, suffered from governance failures, or simply failed to attract the investment and execute the projects they were set up for.

This history is not a reason to be pessimistic about the new law, but it is a reason to examine its design critically. Has it learned from previous failures? Does it have stronger governance safeguards? Does it have better insulation from political interference? Does it have realistic mechanisms for attracting genuine private investment rather than connected-insider investment? These are questions we will return to as we work through the law's provisions in detail.

The political economy of Nepal also shapes the context. Nepal has struggled with what development economists sometimes call "elite capture" — the tendency of institutions created in the public interest to be taken over by politically connected interests who use them for private benefit. The question of whether the Alternative Development Finance Fund is genuinely structured to resist such capture, or whether it creates new opportunities for it, is one of the most important questions this series will try to answer.
