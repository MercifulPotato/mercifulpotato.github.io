# Before the World Was Made: The Music of the Ainur, the Creation of Arda, and the First War of the Powers

*This is Part One of a comprehensive, in-universe chronological history of Middle-earth, drawing upon The Silmarillion, The Hobbit, The Lord of the Rings, Unfinished Tales, The History of Middle-earth, and all related texts left by J.R.R. Tolkien. Where Tolkien revised his own mythology — sometimes radically — both the earlier and later versions are discussed, because understanding how the story changed is as important as understanding the story itself. We write here as though these events are history, because in the world Tolkien built, they are.*

---

## Part 1: Eru Ilúvatar and the Timeless Halls

In the beginning, before there was a world, before there was time, before there was anything that could be seen or touched or measured, there was only Eru — whom the Elves would later call Ilúvatar, the Father of All. He existed in a place or state of existence that Tolkien's texts call the Timeless Halls: not a location within the universe we would recognise, but a realm outside and before creation itself, a place of pure thought and potential.

Eru Ilúvatar was, by the accounting of Tolkien's mythology, the one supreme deity — the monotheistic foundation beneath what on its surface looks like a polytheistic world of gods and demigods. He was not the god of one people or one faith; he was the maker of existence itself, the intelligence from which all things proceeded. He had no origin that any being within his creation could comprehend. He simply was. And from his thought, everything else would come.

The first act of Ilúvatar was not to make a world. It was to make beings who could think and feel and create, beings who were themselves expressions of his own thought. These were the Ainur — the Holy Ones — and they were the first children of Ilúvatar's mind. They were not material creatures in any sense we would understand. They were vast intelligences, spirits of enormous power and depth, each one arising from some particular aspect of Ilúvatar's own nature. They came into being knowing only Ilúvatar and one another. There was no world for them to dwell in. There was only the Timeless Halls, which were not halls in any architectural sense but simply the space of Ilúvatar's presence, the boundary of what was.

The Ainur were many. Their number is not given precisely in any of Tolkien's texts — the Silmarillion tells us only that they were great and many in number. Among them, there were those of surpassing power. These mightiest of the Ainur would later enter the world and be known by the Elves as the Valar — the Powers of Arda. But before any of that, while the world still did not exist, the Ainur lived in Ilúvatar's presence, and he spoke to them and they understood his nature and his intentions in ways that no other being could. They were as thoughts made real, each one a reflection of some facet of the infinite mind that had made them.

Ilúvatar taught the Ainur music. He told them of themes and variations, and they sang. But their singing was not yet the Great Music that would make the world — it was preparation, education, a deepening of their understanding of what Ilúvatar was. Each Ainur understood the music as it related to their own nature. None of them comprehended it whole. None of them could. Only Ilúvatar held the complete picture.

## Part 2: The Ainulindalë — The Music of the Ainur

Then came the moment that Tolkien's mythology treats as the literal beginning of everything: the Ainulindalë, which means in Quenya "The Music of the Ainur." This was the act of creation itself, performed not by Ilúvatar alone but by Ilúvatar conducting a cosmic symphony that all the Ainur participated in.

Ilúvatar proposed a theme of great beauty and complexity, and he told the Ainur that he wished them to make a Great Music from it — an elaboration, an interpretation, a performance of the theme in all its possible richness. He told them that they might adorn the theme as they wished, for he had given them each their own thoughts and their own powers. And so the Ainur began to sing.

The music that arose was of such magnificence that it passed beyond description. The Timeless Halls were filled with sound and with the harmonies of countless minds weaving together the strands of the theme Ilúvatar had given them. The music deepened and widened, and as it grew, it ceased to be merely music — it became, in some sense, a vision of what might be. It became a kind of prophecy, a blueprint, a revelation of things that were possible.

But then something went wrong.

Among the Ainur was one of supreme power — one who had been given greater gifts than any other, who understood more of Ilúvatar's mind than any of his siblings. This was Melkor. And Melkor had a desire that was unlike the desires of the other Ainur: he wanted to make things of his own devising that were not in the theme of Ilúvatar. He wanted to create not as an expression of Ilúvatar's thought but as an independent act — to introduce into the music something that Ilúvatar had not conceived, something that was purely his own.

This desire was not malicious in its origin. Tolkien is careful to establish that Melkor's fall was rooted not in pure malice but in pride — in the conviction that his own power and intelligence were sufficient to conceive of things that the maker of all things had not already encompassed. He went out into the Void, seeking the Imperishable Flame — the fire of creation that Ilúvatar alone controlled — hoping to use it to make things of his own. He did not find it, because it was with Ilúvatar alone. But the searching changed him. Loneliness bred resentment. The desire to create independently curdled into possessiveness. And when he returned to the Great Music, the notes he sang were no longer those of the original theme.

Melkor began to interweave into the music thoughts and desires that were his alone. He sang themes of power and dominion, of themes that conflicted with the harmonies others were producing. And because of his enormous power, his music was compelling. Other Ainur near him began to attune to his theme rather than Ilúvatar's. Discord spread.

Ilúvatar responded.

Three times in the account of the Ainulindalë, Ilúvatar intervened in the music. Three times he introduced a new theme, and each time, the theme he introduced was such that even Melkor's discordant counterpoint became, in the wider pattern, part of something greater. Tolkien's text makes this explicit: Melkor's themes were taken up and turned. They became not the victory of chaos over order but an element within the story that could not now be told without them. The sorrows that Melkor's music introduced — the suffering, the loss, the struggle against darkness — became inseparable from the beauty of the whole.

This is one of the most theologically complex ideas in all of Tolkien's work. Ilúvatar does not prevent evil; he does not simply override Melkor's discord. Instead he demonstrates that his own creative intelligence is so far beyond Melkor's that even Melkor's rebellion cannot escape the pattern — it can only add to it, change its texture, make it richer and more complex and in ways more beautiful for the anguish it carries. The things Melkor was proudest of in his counter-theme were shown to be, in the final accounting, not his at all but part of a vast design that was always larger than he could conceive.

At the conclusion of the music, Ilúvatar stopped, and there was silence. He spoke to the Ainur of what had happened, and he showed them a vision — a Vision of all that the music had been and all that it signified. And in the vision, the Ainur saw the world: the Earth that was to be, with its seas and mountains, its living things, its ages and its peoples. They saw the Children of Ilúvatar — the Elves and Men who would come into the world — walking and working and living and dying. They saw beauty of such force that many wept, and they saw sorrow, because Melkor's discord had woven sorrow into the fabric of what would be.

Then Ilúvatar spoke words that carry enormous weight in the later narrative: "Eä! Let these things Be!" And with that word, the vision became real. The world was made. The Timeless Halls were no longer the only thing that existed. Eä — the universe, the material creation, all of space and time — came into being.

## Part 3: The Question of Free Will and the Nature of Evil

Before we proceed to the physical shaping of Arda, we must pause to examine what the Ainulindalë means in terms of Tolkien's understanding of evil and free will, because these themes will echo through every single subsequent event in the history of Middle-earth.

Tolkien was a devout Roman Catholic, and his mythology is in many ways an extended meditation on theological questions that occupied him throughout his life. The problem of evil — how can evil exist in a world made by a good and all-powerful god? — is directly addressed in the Ainulindalë. Tolkien's answer, as expressed through Ilúvatar's relationship with Melkor, is essentially this: evil is not a force opposed to goodness on equal terms. Evil is a distortion, a perversion, a parasite on good. Melkor could not create the Imperishable Flame because the creative fire belonged to Ilúvatar alone. Everything Melkor ever made or did throughout the entire history of Arda was a corruption of something Ilúvatar had first conceived. He could distort; he could not originate.

This principle has profound consequences. It means that the darkness in Tolkien's world is never simply darkness — it always carries within it the memory of what it has been perverted from. The Orcs were Elves. The Trolls were something before they were Trolls. The land of Mordor was not always a wasteland. Melkor's greatest weapons — his corrupted Maiar, his distorted creatures, his poisoned minds — are greatest at the moment of their perversion and become progressively less capable, less original, less powerful as they move further from their source. This is why Tolkien's darkness always diminishes. It cannot sustain itself.

The question of free will is equally important. The Ainur were given genuine freedom. Melkor's rebellion was real, not predetermined. The other Ainur who, to varying degrees, were drawn toward his music or later toward his service were not puppets. And the Children of Ilúvatar — Elves and Men — would be given a freedom even more radical, because they would come into the world outside the Music entirely. They were not in the vision the Ainur sang. They were a surprise — Ilúvatar's own secret, something he had kept from even his mightiest servants. The consequence is that no matter how much Melkor — or later Sauron — might try to control the fate of the Children, they always contained within them something that was not in the original design and that could not, therefore, be predicted or fully controlled.

Tolkien also grappled with what he called the "Mannish question" — the particular nature of Men as opposed to Elves. Men were given what is sometimes called the Gift of Ilúvatar, or the Gift of Men, which is death. They are not bound to the world as the Elves are. Their fate after death is unknown even to the Valar — they pass beyond the circles of the world and are taken to Ilúvatar himself, or so the mythology suggests. This is, in Tolkien's framework, not a punishment but a gift: a freedom from the diminishment and weariness that eventually comes to even the Elves, who are bound to the world until its ending. Men were made for something beyond Arda, and their mortality is the expression of that destiny.

Melkor taught Men to fear death and to call it a curse. This was one of his greatest and most enduring lies. The corruption of the Númenóreans — which would eventually produce the catastrophe of the Downfall of Númenor — was rooted precisely in this fear, in the belief that mortality was an insult rather than a promise.

## Part 4: The Descent into Arda — The Ainur Become the Valar

With the world now made — existing in space and time, but as yet unformed, a place of potential rather than actuality — many of the Ainur desired to enter it, to give physical form to the things they had seen in the vision. They wished to make the music real in matter as they had made it real in sound. And Ilúvatar told them they could go, if they wished. Those who entered the world, however, would be bound to it until its ending. They would be part of its fate. They were not prisoners — they could leave, in principle — but they would be part of Arda in a way that they could not simply undo.

The Ainur who chose to descend are called the Valar. There were fifteen who were of chief importance among all who entered, and of these fifteen, one — Melkor — would become the enemy of all. The other fourteen are the ones properly called the Valar in most of Tolkien's later texts, though the term technically includes Melkor. But convention has excluded him, for obvious reasons.

We should meet them, because they are the powers that govern the world of Middle-earth, and understanding who they are is understanding the architecture of everything that follows.

**Manwë Súlimo** was the eldest and most honoured of the Valar, and he was the King of Arda — not by conquest but by Ilúvatar's choice and by the consent of all the Valar. He was the closest in thought to Ilúvatar among all the Ainur who entered the world. His domain was the sky and the air and the wind. The eagles were his servants. His sight was far, and his wisdom was great, but his goodness was sometimes insufficient preparation for the depths of evil he faced, because he found it genuinely difficult to comprehend corruption. This limitation — the inability of the greatest good to fully understand the depths of evil — would have real consequences in the history of Arda.

**Varda Elentári**, called Elbereth by the Elves, was the spouse of Manwë and the Queen of the Stars. She was beloved beyond all others by the Elves, and her name was a ward against evil — Melkor hated and feared her more than any of the other Valar. She had filled the heavens with stars before the Elves awoke, knowing that light would be their first sight, and this act of love bound her to them in a way that transcended all later history. When Frodo cries out "O Elbereth Gilthoniel!" in the chapter "Fog on the Barrow-Downs" and later when Sam speaks her name against Shelob, it is Varda they are invoking — a being of power so vast that her light still burns across the heavens in the stars she made.

**Ulmo** was the Lord of Waters. Unlike the other Valar, he never truly dwelt in Valinor — he moved always through the waters of the world, the oceans and the rivers and the underground streams and the rain. He was not estranged from the other Valar, but he was apart from them, always closer to the world of Elves and Men than any of the others. He loved them, and he worked for their benefit even when the other Valar had despaired of them or had chosen not to act. It is Ulmo who sends visions to Tuor in Unfinished Tales, who speaks to Finrod Felagund, who moves Círdan of the Havens to give Gandalf the ring Narya. He is the Vala who refuses to abandon the Children even when the others have retreated behind the walls of Valinor.

**Aulë** the Smith was the maker, the craftsman, the builder. His domain was the substance of the world — stone and metal, the deep places under the earth, the craft of making things with hands and mind. He was not as mighty as Manwë or Ulmo in terms of raw power, but his skills were extraordinary, and his love for craft was genuine and deep. He made the Seven Fathers of the Dwarves in an act of creation that exceeded his authority, and Ilúvatar — rather than simply destroying them — gave them life, because Aulë's desire was not possessive but genuinely loving. He wanted beings to whom he could teach his craft. This single act, the making of the Dwarves, had consequences that echoed through all of Middle-earth's subsequent history. Sauron was one of Aulë's Maiar, which explains much about the nature of the One Ring — the perversion of craft into control.

**Yavanna Kementári** was the spouse of Aulë and the giver of fruits. She loved all growing things — all the plants and trees and living vegetation of the world. She was the maker of the Two Trees of Valinor, the greatest single act of creation by any Vala, and the loss of those Trees was the single greatest catastrophe of the First Age. Her grief at that loss drove her to request the Ents — the tree-herders — as guardians for the forests. She is one of the most present of the Valar in the actual lived history of Middle-earth, though often invisibly: every tree, every field of grain, every garden is in some sense her domain.

**Mandos**, whose proper name was Námo, was the Keeper of the Dead. His halls were in the west of Valinor, and to those halls came the spirits of the dead Elves — their Houseless Ones, their fëar without their hröar — to await their rehousing or the end of the world. He knew the fate of all things within Arda, because he had perceived the music more completely than almost any of the others. He seldom spoke, but when he did, his words were of great weight — they were what Tolkien calls "dooms" in the archaic sense, pronouncements of fate. The Doom of the Noldor was spoken by Mandos. The conditions under which Beren and Lúthien were restored to life were set by Mandos. He is the most implacable of the Valar, not because he is cruel but because he sees the pattern of things most clearly and understands that bending it — even with compassion — carries costs.

**Vairë the Weaver** was the spouse of Mandos, and she wove the events of time into tapestries that lined the halls of her husband. All that had ever happened in Arda was preserved in her weaving. This is one of Tolkien's most evocative details: history is not simply past, not simply gone — it is woven into a permanent record that exists, unchangeable, in the keeping of a being whose craft is exactly that: the preservation of what was.

**Lórien** — whose proper name was Irmo — was the master of visions and dreams. His gardens in Valinor, also called Lórien, were among the most beautiful places in the world, a place of rest and dream and healing. He must be distinguished from the land of Lórien in Middle-earth, which is named after him (the full name is Lothlórien, though the Elvish name is more complex). The Elves of Middle-earth found in their forest a kind of shadow of Irmo's gardens — a place where time moved differently, where weariness was eased, where one might see clearly what the mind needed to perceive.

**Estë the Gentle** was the spouse of Lórien and the healer. She rested during the day and walked only in twilight, and her touch brought rest to those who were weary and sick.

**Nienna** was grief. Not despair — grief, which is not the same thing. She mourned for the hurt of the world and for the marring of Arda, and those who listened to her learned pity and patience. Among those who sat at her feet and learned from her was Gandalf — Olórin as he was in Valinor — and this is crucial. Gandalf's patience with hobbits and Men, his refusal to despair even when all seems lost, his capacity to see potential goodness in the most unlikely people — all of this comes from his education with Nienna. He is Nienna's student as much as he is Manwë's servant.

**Tulkas Astaldo**, the Champion, was the last of the Valar to enter Arda — he came specifically to aid in the battle against Melkor. He was the greatest of the Valar in physical strength and courage, the one who wrestled Melkor down and chained him at the end of the First Battle. He loved laughter and wrestling and games, and he was completely without guile. His weakness was the same as his strength: he was impatient, and in matters requiring cunning or long patience, he was less effective.

**Nessa** was the spouse of Tulkas and the Lady of Deer. She loved to run, and the deer ran alongside her, unable to match her speed.

**Oromë the Hunter** was the great rider, the Lord of Forests, whose horn was heard across the world. It was Oromë who first found the Elves when they awoke at the mere of Cuiviénen. He alone of the Valar still rode often into Middle-earth during the long years before the Elves came to Valinor, and it was his presence that gave the Elves their first knowledge of the world beyond their birthplace. His hunting of the great beasts and monsters of Melkor kept the darkened world slightly safer than it might otherwise have been.

**Vána the Ever-young** was the younger sister of Yavanna and the spouse of Oromë. Flowers bloomed at her passing and birds sang at her bidding.

And then there was **Melkor**, who would later be called Morgoth — the Black Enemy — a name given to him by Fëanor, the greatest of the Elves, when Melkor's crimes had become undeniable. Melkor was the mightiest of all the Ainur. All the gifts that Ilúvatar had distributed among the others, Melkor had some portion of. He understood heat and cold, light and darkness, sound and silence, creation and destruction. His power was extraordinary. But it was power turned inward, power bent toward possession, toward the desire not to share in creation but to own it. He was Tolkien's embodiment of the will-to-power in its most terrible form.

## Part 5: The Maiar — The Lesser Ainur

Below the Valar in power, but still extraordinary by any standard that would apply to mortal beings, were the Maiar — the lesser Ainur who also entered the world. They were far more numerous than the Valar. Most of them served specific Valar, acting as their assistants and representatives. Many are never named in any of Tolkien's published texts. But several of the Maiar are central figures in the history of Middle-earth, and we should know them.

**Olórin** is the most important Maia in all of Middle-earth's history, and he is known to virtually every reader of Tolkien by another name: Gandalf. He was one of Manwë's Maiar, but he also spent time with Nienna, and from her he learned wisdom and compassion. He was thought to be the least of the Maiar in some respects — he was gentle, he worked quietly, he helped by inspiring and encouraging rather than by direct action. But he was deeply wise, and his understanding of mortal hearts was extraordinary. We will return to him many times.

**Eönwë** was the Herald of Manwë and the mightiest of Maiar in martial might. It was Eönwë who led the Hosts of Valinor in the War of Wrath at the end of the First Age, who defeated Morgoth's armies, and who captured Morgoth himself and dragged him before the Valar in chains. He was also the one who summoned Sauron to face judgment after the War of Wrath, giving Sauron the chance — which Sauron refused — to repent.

**Ossë** and **Uinen** were Maiar of Ulmo, associated with the seas and coastlines. Ossë loved the storm and the turbulence of the ocean, and he was occasionally swayed toward Melkor's service — but was brought back by Uinen, his spouse, who loved the deep calm and the taming of seas. Ossë taught the Teleri their love of ships and the sea, and Uinen is called upon by mariners in distress. Between them they represented the dual nature of the ocean: its wildness and its mercy.

**Melian** was a Maia of unusual importance. She alone of the Maiar chose to incarnate fully and permanently in Middle-earth during the First Age, taking bodily form and marrying an Elf-king — Thingol Greycloak of the Sindar. Their daughter Lúthien was the most beautiful and most gifted being who ever lived, half-Maia and half-Elf, and Lúthien's deeds would permanently alter the fate of the world. Melian surrounded the forest of Doriath with the Girdle of Melian, a ward of such power that no enemy could enter Doriath against the will of its makers. When Melian departed Middle-earth — following Thingol's murder — the Girdle failed, and Doriath fell.

**Sauron** was the greatest and most terrible of the Maiar who served Melkor. He had been a Maia of Aulë — a craftsman, a maker — and his nature was always ordered toward skill and craft and the control of things according to design. When he was corrupted by Melkor, that orderly nature became tyranny: the desire not simply to make but to control all making, to impose one pattern upon everything. The One Ring is the most complete expression of his nature — a device that was simultaneously the greatest feat of craftsmanship in the history of Arda and an instrument of absolute domination. We will speak of Sauron at enormous length as this history proceeds, but his origin matters: he was a craftsman first. His evil was the evil of talent corrupted by pride and the desire for control.

**Gothmog**, the Lord of Balrogs, was a Maia who had entered Melkor's service early in the history of Arda and became the greatest of the Balrogs — a being of fire and shadow who commanded Melkor's armies in the field. He killed Fëanor, the greatest of the Elves. He killed Fingon, the High King of the Noldor. He was in turn killed by Ecthelion of the Fountain during the Fall of Gondolin. The Balrogs as a group were Maiar who had chosen Melkor's service; they were not separate kinds of creatures but fallen Ainur, and their fire was real power, not mere illusion.

**The five Wizards** — the Istari — are Maiar who will not enter the story until the Third Age, but we should note their existence here: Gandalf (Olórin), Saruman (Curunír), Radagast (Aiwendil), and the two Blue Wizards (Alatar and Pallando, or in Tolkien's later names Morinehtar and Rómestámo) were Maiar sent by the Valar to Middle-earth in diminished form to assist the peoples of Middle-earth against Sauron. They were not permitted to match Sauron's power directly. They were counsellors, not warriors — or were supposed to be. The corruptions of Saruman and the failures of the Blue Wizards are among the saddest episodes in the history of the Maiar.

## Part 6: The Shaping of Arda — The First Labours of the Valar

When the Valar entered Arda, the world was formless. This is not metaphor — it was literally without shape. The physical matter of the world existed, but it had not been organised into continents and seas and mountains. The work of the Valar was to shape this material into the world the Great Music had described.

But Melkor was there too. He had entered the world earlier than the other Valar, or alongside them, and he had begun his own work of unmaking. Whatever the Valar built, Melkor tried to destroy. Whatever mountain they raised, he threw down. Whatever sea they made fair, he troubled with storm and cold. This was the First War, and it is important to understand that the world we know — or rather, the world as it was in the First Age — was already a compromise, already marked by destruction and loss. The Spring of Arda that the Valar created was the world at its best, before any of its great catastrophes, but it was already not the world that had been shown in the Music, because Melkor had been at work.

The Valar made two great Lamps to light the world — Illuin in the north and Ormal in the south, set upon great pillars of stone. These Lamps flooded all of Arda with light, and under their radiance the world grew in beauty. The Valar established their dwelling at Almaren, in the middle of the world. And for a time — a time called the Spring of Arda — the world was lit and warm and growing.

Melkor had retreated, seemingly defeated. But he had built a fortress in the uttermost north — Utumno — delving deep into the earth beyond the sight and reach of the Valar. And while they built and tended and rejoiced in the Spring of Arda, he was working in Utumno, breeding monsters in the deep pits, corrupting the living things of the young world, making servants of darkness and terror. He moved with great cunning, because his experience in the Timeless Halls had taught him that direct confrontation with the combined power of the Valar could not succeed.

When the time was right, he struck. He attacked the great pillars of the Lamps and threw them down. The destruction was catastrophic. The Lamps fell and burned, and their fires spilled across the world, reshaping land and sea. The isle of Almaren, where the Valar dwelt, was destroyed. The great symmetry of the world before the Lamps fell was broken — the world as we come to know it in the stories, with its irregular coastlines and its scattered seas, was already the result of this catastrophe.

Melkor retreated to Utumno. The Valar, grieving, left Middle-earth entirely — they withdrew to the far west and raised the mountain range called the Pelóri, the Mountains of Defence, and built Valinor beyond them: a land of light and beauty that was forever separated from Middle-earth. They made the Two Trees of Valinor to give themselves light, since the Lamps were lost. And Middle-earth was left in darkness, lit only by the stars that Varda had made.

This decision — to leave Middle-earth and build Valinor — haunts the rest of the history. The Valar did not abandon the world out of cowardice; they withdrew because they could not build and protect and fight all at once, and Valinor was meant to be a staging ground from which they would eventually act to free the world. But their withdrawal meant that Middle-earth spent an immense age in darkness, and it was in that darkness that the Elves awoke.

## Part 7: The Ages Before Light — Yénë and the Deepening of Melkor's Corruption

The period between the fall of the Lamps and the awakening of the Elves is one of the most important and least discussed eras in Tolkien's mythology. It is a period measured not in years as mortals count them but in Valian Years, a different unit of time entirely — each Valian Year being equivalent to approximately 9.58 solar years, though Tolkien's numbers shifted across different versions of the mythology.

During this period, Melkor was not idle. He deepened Utumno and built Angband as a secondary fortress, a great underground complex under the mountains of the north. He bred and multiplied his servants. He corrupted the living things of Middle-earth — animals, trees, the very substance of the ground in some places — and created monsters: things of fire, of shadow, of distorted bone and scale and fang. The great dragons of later ages did not yet exist, but their precursors — fire-drakes of lesser power — were already being bred in the pits.

It is during this period that the Balrogs came into their full terrible being. They had been Maiar, corrupted by Melkor, and in their corruption they had become beings of fire and shadow — instruments of terror that carried real Ainu power. They would serve as Melkor's shock troops, his officers, the beings he trusted most after Sauron.

Sauron himself was already at this stage Melkor's greatest lieutenant. He had been an Aulëan Maia — a craftsman, an orderer — and Melkor had recognised his value and corrupted him early. Sauron's corruption was different from that of the Balrogs. The Balrogs became beasts of destruction, weapons. Sauron retained his intelligence, his craft, his ability to plan and to deceive. He was Melkor's administrator, his general, his spy. He could take fair forms when needed. He could persuade and seduce and corrupt. He was, in many ways, more dangerous than any Balrog.

Also during this period, there was a great division in the understanding of the Valar concerning Middle-earth and the fate of the Children of Ilúvatar. The Valar knew from the Music that the Elves and Men would come — they had seen them in the Vision. But they did not know when. Yavanna mourned the darkness of Middle-earth, the slow corruption of her growing things. Ulmo urged patience and continued presence in Middle-earth's waters. Manwë listened and waited for Ilúvatar's signs.

And then the stars came out over a world that had been dark, and by the light of those stars that Varda had made, the Elves awoke at the mere of Cuiviénen.

---

## Part 8: The Awakening of the Elves — The First Sunrise of the Firstborn

Cuiviénen was a bay of an inland sea in the far east of Middle-earth, deep in a forested valley where the stars could be seen reflected in still water. It was by those waters, in starlight, that the first Elves opened their eyes.

The Elves were the Firstborn Children of Ilúvatar — not made by any Vala or Maia, not a product of craft or music as understood by anyone else. They were Ilúvatar's own creation, his secret, conceived and placed in the world in his own time and fashion. No one else knew when they would awaken. Even the most prescient of the Valar could not have named the moment. Their awakening was one of the most joyful events in the history of Arda.

They awoke in pairs — husband and wife together, or groups of three, the texts vary — and the first thing they saw was the stars. This is why the Elves above all other beings love the stars, and why Varda is the most beloved of the Valar among them. Stars were their first sight and the light of their first home.

The first words the Elves spoke were made up — they invented language spontaneously, because language was part of their nature as Children of Ilúvatar. This language was the earliest form of what would eventually become Quenya. It was not taught to them; it arose from them, because they were beings for whom language was as natural as breathing.

The sixty who first awoke — the tradition preserved in Tolkien's texts — were: Imin and Iminyë, Tata and Tatië, Enel and Enelyë, and their companions. These names represent the first three pairs and three groups of eighteen, but the details of the early genealogy of the Elves are extremely complex and changed considerably across Tolkien's various drafts. The essential point is that three founding pairs gave rise to the three great kindreds of the Elves: the Vanyar, the Noldor, and the Teleri.

The Elves multiplied, and they lived by the shores of Cuiviénen for what would be, by later reckoning, enormous spans of time. They had no knowledge of Valinor, no knowledge of the Valar, no history beyond their own short experience. They hunted and sang and walked under the stars and marvelled at everything. The world was new to them, and they were new to it.

But Melkor knew they had come. And he sent hunters into the east.

---

## Part 9: The Corrupting of the Elves — The Origin of the Orcs

What happened at Cuiviénen before Oromë found the Elves is one of the darkest episodes in all of Tolkien's mythology, and it is a story told with varying degrees of directness in different texts.

Some Elves were lost — wandering away from the starlit shores, going out into the dark forests, and never returning. What became of them is not stated directly. The strong implication in the Silmarillion and in various notes of Tolkien's is that these lost Elves were captured by Melkor and brought to Utumno, where they were corrupted and twisted over long ages until they were no longer recognisable as Elves. They became the first Orcs.

This is a theory — Tolkien was careful, in some texts, to call it a rumour or a tradition rather than established fact. But it is the dominant theory within Tolkien's own writings, and the one with the most textual support. The Orcs were not made from nothing. Melkor could not create — he could only corrupt. And the corruption of captured Elves, tortured and broken and remade in the pits of Utumno, produced a being that was the mirror-image of an Elf: as Elves were beautiful, Orcs were hideous; as Elves loved starlight, Orcs hated it; as Elves were creative and loving, Orcs were destructive and cruel.

The tragedy of this origin is almost unbearable. The beings who would be Tolkien's main instrument of evil for most of the history of Middle-earth were not originally evil. They were Elves, taken before they had learned anything of the world, before any of the Valar had come to them, before they had names or history — and they were unmade and remade by the greatest evil in the world.

There is a theological problem here that Tolkien never fully resolved. If the Orcs were made from Elves, did they retain the souls — the fëar — of those Elves? Were they immortal as Elves are immortal? If so, what happened to them when they died? Did they go to the Halls of Mandos? Tolkien worried at this question in many notes, and in some of his later thinking he began to wonder whether the Orcs were not corrupted Elves at all but rather Maiar in corrupted form — which would solve the theological problem but creates different ones. In the end, he left the question open. The published Silmarillion, edited by Christopher Tolkien, retains the corrupted-Elves theory as the main text while acknowledging alternatives. This is one of those places where Tolkien's mythology is genuinely unresolved, and intellectually honest readers should hold the question open.

What is not in question is the effect: Melkor had servants who could operate in Middle-earth, who would breed and multiply, who would take the world's most beautiful and most beloved beings and turn them into instruments of destruction.

---

## Part 10: The Discovery of the Elves — Oromë Rides East

Oromë of the Valar was in the habit of riding through Middle-earth even during the long dark ages, hunting the monsters that Melkor had sent across the world. It was he who first found the Elves at Cuiviénen.

He heard their voices — singing, or calling to one another — and he went toward the sound, and in the starlight he saw them: these astonishing beings, new to the world, unlike anything else he had encountered. He was overjoyed. He called them by a name in their own language: Eldar, "People of the Stars." He remained among them for a time, because he recognised what they were — the Children of Ilúvatar — and he wanted them to know that not all powers were malevolent, not all great beings were to be feared.

But there was fear. Melkor had been spreading rumours and terrors among the Elves from the very beginning — sending shadows of terror through the forest, creating fell shapes that hunted the edges of the firelight, whispering to those Elves who were bold enough to wander that the great Rider of the Valar was himself a hunter of Elves. And so when Oromë first appeared, some Elves ran in terror. Others stood and did not flee. Those who stood, who looked at him and felt the truth of his nature — that he was not a hunter of Elves, that his light and his joy were genuine — became the nucleus of the Elves who would eventually journey to Valinor.

Oromë returned to Valinor with news of the Elves, and the Valar were troubled and then decided. The time had come to act. The long retreat to Valinor had not been permanent — it had been a strategic withdrawal, and now the reason to act had appeared. They would make war on Melkor.

---

## Part 11: The First War Against Melkor — The Assault on Utumno

The Valar went to war in full force — the War for the Sake of the Elves, as it is sometimes called. Melkor had built his power over long ages and had two great fortresses: Utumno in the north and Angband in the northwest, commanded by Sauron. But he had not expected the Valar to move so decisively, and he was not prepared.

The War was devastating to the world. The Valar were not fragile beings, and when beings of their power fought, the physical world suffered. The seas rose and fell, mountains crashed, the north of Middle-earth was devastated. The Elves by their mere of Cuiviénen, far to the east, were told by Oromë to remain there and not to travel north — the battle was too terrible.

Melkor was defeated. Utumno was cast down, its pits broken open, its servants scattered or taken. Melkor himself was taken by Tulkas — wrestling him down with his bare hands, the great champion of the Valar — and brought back in chains. The chain was called Angainor, and in it Melkor was bound.

But Angband was not fully destroyed. This is one of the crucial failures of the War, though it may not have been fully recognised as such at the time. Sauron, Melkor's greatest lieutenant, escaped. The depths of Angband — the deeper pits — were not fully searched. And in the pits of Utumno that were broken open, many of Melkor's creatures escaped into the wider world, to breed and multiply in the dark places of Middle-earth.

Melkor was brought before the Valar in Valinor, and he was judged, and he was sentenced to imprisonment in the Halls of Mandos for three ages of the world. Three Valian Ages — which represents an enormous span of time. He was to dwell there in thought, presumably hoping that thought would lead to repentance. He passed out of the history for a long time — but not forever.

---

## Part 12: The Debate Concerning the Elves — Come to Valinor, or Stay in Middle-earth?

The victory over Melkor raised an immediate question: what should be done about the Elves? They were now, at least technically, safe — Melkor was imprisoned. But the world was not entirely safe. Other servants of Melkor, and other dangers, remained. And the Valar had a powerful desire to bring the Elves to Valinor — to have them near, to teach them, to share with them the light of the Two Trees.

The debate among the Valar was real. Not all of them agreed. Ulmo in particular was troubled by the summons to Valinor. He argued that the Elves should be allowed to develop in Middle-earth — that bringing them to Valinor, however well-intentioned, was a kind of removal of the Children from their birthright. The world had been made for them as much as for the Valar. If the Elves were brought to Valinor, they would grow up in a kind of captivity — however beautiful the captivity — rather than in the world that was theirs.

This objection was overruled. The summons went out to the Elves at Cuiviénen.

Three ambassadors were sent: one from each of the three great kindreds. They were brought to Valinor and shown its wonders, and they returned to their people to report. What they reported convinced most of the Elves to go.

But not all.

The Elves who chose to come to Valinor and made the Great Journey were called the Eldar. The Elves who refused to come — who stayed in Middle-earth — were called the Avari, "the Unwilling." The Avari became the Elves of the deep wild, the Elves who never went to Valinor and who therefore never received the fulness of what Valinor offered. They are present in the background of later history — Tolkien mentions them occasionally — but they are not the primary focus of the great narratives. They lived and multiplied and wandered through the wide world, and some of their descendants eventually merged with other Elvish peoples.

The Eldar who set out on the Great Journey were divided into three main groups: the Vanyar (led by Ingwë), the Noldor (led by Finwë), and the Teleri (led in different branches by Elwë and Olwë). The journey from Cuiviénen to the shores of the Great Sea, and eventually to Valinor, was one of the great epics of the First Age — though it predates what most people think of as the "First Age" proper, which begins with the rising of the Moon and Sun. This period is sometimes called the Elder Days of the Eldar, or more simply the time of the Trees.

---

## Part 13: The Great Journey — From Cuiviénen to the Sea

The journey of the Eldar from Cuiviénen to the shores of Belegaer, the Great Sea, took an enormous span of time. The paths led westward across the wide, dark, forested continent of Middle-earth — through lands that the Elves had never seen, past mountains and rivers and plains that no map had yet described.

Oromë led the march, riding ahead and to the flanks, clearing the path of monsters, guiding the great host. The Vanyar were fewest in number and most devoted to Oromë's guidance — they moved quickest. The Noldor were many and curious, pausing to examine everything, to observe and to learn. The Teleri were most numerous and least hurried — they moved slowly, always looking back the way they had come.

It was during the Great Journey that the first great division of the Teleri occurred. Elwë Singollo — later called Thingol Greycloak — was one of the two leaders of the Teleri, and he was among the mightiest of all the Elves. Tall and silver-haired, of a beauty that matched any Elf in Valinor. During the journey he wandered into the forest of Nan Elmoth to investigate, and there he came upon Melian — the Maia of Lórien who walked in Middle-earth — and their eyes met.

What happened to Elwë and Melian in Nan Elmoth is described in the Silmarillion as a kind of enchantment, but it was not an unwilling one. They stood in the forest, hand in hand, for what felt to them like moments but was in fact a very long time. The rest of the Teleri searched for their lord and could not find him. Eventually, many of them refused to go further without him — they were the Sindar, the Grey Elves, who settled in Beleriand and became the people of Thingol and Melian. Others, led by Olwë, eventually continued westward to the sea and then to Valinor. They became the Teleri of Valinor, the greatest of all mariners.

The Nandor were another group who turned aside during the journey. Led by one Lenwë, they refused to cross the Misty Mountains — a significant natural barrier — and turned south along the eastern banks of the Anduin. The Nandor eventually became two peoples: the Laiquendi who crossed the Blue Mountains into Beleriand, and those who remained east of the mountains and became the Wood-elves. The Silvan Elves of Mirkwood and of Lórien in the Third Age are their distant descendants.

The Vanyar and the Noldor — and eventually the remaining Teleri under Olwë — reached the shores of the Great Sea. And here they found another problem: the Sea itself.

The Elves had never seen the ocean. The vast expanse of Belegaer was unlike anything in their experience, and many were afraid of it. They camped on the shore for long ages, and during this time Ossë taught the Teleri the art of building ships, and the Teleri's love of the sea — their devotion to ships and sailing, their characteristic skill that would make them the greatest mariners in the world — was born here, on the western shore of Middle-earth, waiting for transport to Valinor.

The Vanyar and Noldor were brought to Valinor on the island of Tol Eressëa, which the Valar moved across the sea like a ferry. The Teleri remained for a long time on the shore, not yet ready to leave, not yet able to build ships large enough for the crossing. Eventually, in the fullness of time, they too came to Valinor, and Tol Eressëa was anchored in the Bay of Eldamar, off the shores of Valinor itself.

---

## Part 14: Valinor and the Years of the Trees — The Golden Age of the Eldar

The period during which the Eldar dwelt in Valinor and the Two Trees gave their light to the world is called the Years of the Trees, and it is the closest the world ever came to Paradise — at least in the northwest where Valinor lay. During this time, the Vanyar and Noldor and Teleri grew in wisdom and art and skill to degrees that have never been matched since.

The Two Trees of Valinor were Telperion and Laurelin — Telperion the silver-leafed, Laurelin the gold-leafed. They did not simply provide light the way the Sun and Moon later would. They were living things of extraordinary power, made by Yavanna with the full force of her creativity, and their light was qualitatively different from the later lights of sun and moon. It was the light of the unmarred world, the light that Valinor held in trust for a world that had been darkened by Melkor.

Telperion bore a silver light that waxed and waned in a cycle of about seven hours, and Laurelin bore a golden light in a complementary cycle, so that for a period each day both trees gave their light together in what the Elves called the mingling of the lights — the most beautiful time, when gold and silver light blended into something both. Later, when the Trees were destroyed, the last flower of Telperion was made into the Moon and the last fruit of Laurelin was made into the Sun. These are lesser things than the Trees themselves — copies, echoes, but not originals.

In Valinor, the Noldor in particular grew to extraordinary heights of achievement. They learned the making of gemstones from Aulë and the nature of light from Varda, and in the earliest time they produced things of beauty that have never been equalled. Fëanor, son of Finwë, king of the Noldor, created the Tengwar — the script later used to write both Quenya and Sindarin — and performed feats of craftsmanship that surpassed even Aulë's expectations. And then he created the Silmarils.

The Silmarils are the central objects of the First Age, and their creation is simultaneously the most magnificent and most catastrophic act in the history of the Eldar. They were three great gems in which Fëanor captured the light of Telperion and Laurelin — the light of the Two Trees, crystallised and preserved. They shone with the light of the Trees but also with something more — something of Fëanor's own spirit that he had poured into them. They were holy objects: no evil thing could touch them without being burned. And they contained the only remaining light of the Trees, which would prove desperately important.

But here we are approaching the great catastrophe. And before we reach it, we must understand Melkor's return.

---

## Part 15: The Release of Melkor — Repentance or Cunning?

After three ages of imprisonment in the Halls of Mandos, Melkor was brought before the Valar and he pleaded for pardon. He submitted. He acknowledged the error of his ways, or appeared to. He humbled himself before the Valar, and he promised to use his great gifts in service of Arda's healing, to repair the hurts he had done.

The Valar debated. Manwë was inclined toward mercy, because he believed repentance was possible even for the mightiest. Others were more cautious. But in the end, Melkor was released and given his freedom in Valinor, though he was not yet permitted to wander beyond Valinor's borders.

Was his submission genuine? This is a question the texts leave deliberately open. Tolkien wrote in notes that he was uncertain whether Melkor's desire for repentance was ever fully real, or whether even in his contrition there was the cunning of one planning for the long term. The most that can be said is that if any part of Melkor had ever wished to return to the goodness of his original nature, it was extinguished by what happened next. By the time he was acting openly again, whatever genuine impulse toward good he might have retained was gone.

Melkor moved through Valinor, apparently helpful and cooperative, offering counsel and assistance to the Valar, beginning to rebuild his reputation. He was clever and persuasive, and he knew exactly where each of the Valar's blind spots lay. He had spent ages in the Halls of Mandos thinking, and what he had thought about, in addition to everything else, was the psychology of his enemies.

He found his best opportunities among the Noldor.

The Noldor were brilliant and proud and curious — characteristics that were virtues, genuinely, but that Melkor could twist. He moved among them sharing knowledge, true knowledge, things that were genuinely useful and true, mingled with subtle falsehoods and provocations. He told them that the Valar had brought them to Valinor not out of love but out of a desire to keep them from their birthright in Middle-earth. He whispered of the power of the Silmarils and suggested that Fëanor was undervalued, that the Valar regarded the Noldor's gifts with jealousy rather than appreciation.

He was particularly successful in driving a wedge between Fëanor and his half-brothers, Fingolfin and Finarfin. Fëanor was the eldest son of Finwë by his first wife Míriel; Fingolfin and Finarfin were the sons of Finwë by his second wife Indis. The situation was already complex — Míriel had died in giving birth to Fëanor, exhausted by the effort of making a spirit so powerful, and this tragedy had shaped Fëanor's pride and his possessiveness. Melkor found the fracture and widened it.

When it was discovered that Melkor had been spreading these whispers, he fled Valinor. He went first to Avathar, a dark region south of the Pelóri, where he found Ungoliant.

---

## Part 16: Ungoliant — The Great Spider and the Darkness

Ungoliant is one of the most mysterious and terrible beings in all of Tolkien's mythology. Her origin is not stated with certainty in any text — she was not an Ainur, or if she was, Tolkien does not say so. She dwelt in Avathar in a form of absolute darkness, drinking light wherever she found it, turning it into more darkness and into the silken webs of her making. She was hunger given form — insatiable, consuming, becoming larger and more terrible with everything she devoured.

Melkor promised her a great feast if she would help him. He promised her the light of the Two Trees themselves. This was an offer she could not refuse.

Together they climbed the Pelóri, Ungoliant spreading a darkness so complete that even the Ainur could not perceive them. They came down into Valinor on the day of a great festival, when all the Valar and all the Eldar and all the folk of Valinor had gathered in celebration on the hill of Ezellohar, where the Two Trees grew.

Melkor had a black spear. He pierced each of the Two Trees three times, and Ungoliant drank the sap and the light that poured from the wounds. She drank until the Trees were drained of everything, and she drank even the drought that came in to fill the void the sap had left. She was enormous with what she had consumed, swollen to a monstrous size, and still she drank.

The Two Trees died.

This was the greatest single catastrophe of the First Age. The light of the Trees was gone. Valinor was dark. Middle-earth, which had never had the Trees' light anyway, was unchanged, but the heart of the world's beauty was extinguished. The light that the Elves had loved above all things — the light they had left Middle-earth to find — was gone.

There was one hope. Yavanna said that if the Silmarils were given to her, she might be able to use their light — which was the light of the Trees, preserved — to revive the Trees or at least to use their essence in a new making. But Fëanor, when the question was put to him, refused.

He had not been asked yet — the Valar were still debating whether to ask him — when news came that changed everything.

Melkor had not only attacked the Trees. Before leaving for Avathar, he had gone to Formenos — the fortress in the north of Valinor where Fëanor had been banished for a time following a confrontation with his half-brother — and there he had killed Finwë, the King of the Noldor and Fëanor's father. He had taken the Silmarils.

---

## Part 17: The Aftermath — Fëanor's Oath and the March into Exile

Fëanor stood before the assembled Valar and the Eldar in the dark of Valinor and heard the two pieces of news simultaneously: the Trees were dying and could not be saved without the Silmarils; the Silmarils were taken; and his father was dead. He stood in the darkness and he refused to give the Silmarils even if he still had them. His words were the most important and most terrible words spoken by any Elf in Valinor: he cursed Melkor with a name that has endured through the whole of the subsequent history. He called him Morgoth — the Black Enemy of the World — and from that moment, that is how Tolkien's own authorial voice refers to him.

Then Fëanor, in his grief and rage, did the thing that the Valar could not foresee and could not prevent. He gathered the Noldor together, and he swore the Oath.

The Oath of Fëanor was not a simple vow. It was the most binding oath conceivable in the context of Tolkien's world — sworn by the name of Ilúvatar himself, invoking the Everlasting Darkness as the consequence of breaking it. And its terms were absolute: Fëanor and his sons — Maedhros, Maglor, Celegorm, Curufin, Caranthir, Amrod, and Amras, all seven of them — swore to pursue any person, any being, any power who possessed the Silmarils and who would not give them up, forever, across all time and all death, to the ends of the world. No exception. No qualification. Any creature who held a Silmaril and did not yield it would be their enemy.

The consequences of this Oath are devastating and far-reaching. It is not simply an oath of vengeance — it is an oath that makes reconciliation impossible, that makes the Noldor permanently incapable of accepting the help of anyone who held a Silmaril legitimately, that sets them against gods and Elves and Men and even against common sense and compassion whenever a Silmaril was in play. The Oath of Fëanor is the machine that drives much of the tragedy of the First Age, because however brave and skilled and righteous the Noldorin warriors might be as individuals, their sworn commitment to recover the Silmarils at any cost means they will always, ultimately, act against their own best interests when a Silmaril is involved.

Fëanor's speech roused the Noldor to a fever. He demanded that they leave Valinor — abandon the Blessed Realm that had imprisoned them (so he framed it) — and return to Middle-earth, where Morgoth had fled with the Silmarils, to take them back by force. He argued for the independence and the greatness of the Noldor, for their right to rule themselves in their own lands. It was powerful oratory, and it moved the hearts of many.

The Valar sent Mandos to speak. He delivered the Doom of the Noldor — the most famous and most terrible prophecy in all of Middle-earth:

*"Tears unnumbered ye shall shed; and the Valar will fence Valinor against you, and shut you out, so that not even the echo of your lamentation shall pass over the mountains. On the House of Fëanor the wrath of the Valar lieth from the West unto the uttermost East, and upon all that will follow them it shall be laid also. Their Oath shall drive them, and yet betray them, and ever snatch away the very treasures that they have sworn to pursue. To evil end shall all things turn that they begin well; and by treason of kin unto kin, and the fear of treason, shall this come to pass."*

Tolkien's version has variant translations in different texts, but the substance is always the same: the Noldor who followed Fëanor into exile were condemned. Not because the Valar would actively pursue them, but because the nature of what they were doing — the Oath, the pride, the kinslaying that was about to follow — would inevitably bring ruin. The Doom was not a punishment imposed from outside; it was a description of what the Oath would do to them from within.

Most of the Noldor heard the Doom and still chose to march. Some — Finarfin, the wisest and most patient of Finwë's sons, and some of his people — turned back at the Doom and returned to Valinor and were forgiven. But his children, including Finrod and Galadriel, continued with the march. The tragedy of the Noldorin exile is that many of those who went to Middle-earth were good and great and did not deserve what the Oath would eventually bring upon them — but they were caught up in something larger than themselves, set in motion by Fëanor's pride and grief and the Oath he swore.

And then came the Kinslaying.

---

## Part 18: The First Kinslaying — Alqualondë

The Noldor needed ships to cross the Great Sea. The Teleri of Valinor had ships — the greatest ships in the world, built during their long waiting on the shores of Middle-earth and perfected in Valinor. Fëanor asked the Teleri to give or lend their ships. The Teleri refused — they would not be party to an exodus that the Valar had condemned, and the ships were their most beloved possessions.

Fëanor tried to take the ships by force. The Teleri defended their ships. And the Noldor, in the heat of battle and desperation, killed Teleri Elves — kinslaying, the killing of one Elf by another, the most abominable act in Elvish tradition.

The First Kinslaying at Alqualondë, the Swan-haven, was the moment from which there was no return. It stained the Noldorin exile in blood and made the Doom of the Noldor almost self-fulfilling — how could beings capable of this act expect anything but grief? Some of the Noldor who had not yet heard the Doom heard of the battle and understood it as a sign of what following Fëanor would mean. Some turned back. Others, already committed, continued.

The Noldor took the ships and sailed north toward the coldest part of the world — the only path to Middle-earth if they could not use the Helcaraxë, the Grinding Ice, the terrible ice-bridge in the far north where the seas met.

---

## Part 19: The Burning of the Ships and the Crossing of the Helcaraxë

The fleet of stolen ships could not carry all of the Noldor in a single crossing. Fëanor took the first group — his sons and their followers — across in the ships. They landed in Middle-earth, at the bay of Losgar in Drengist. And then, in the most decisive act of treachery among Elves in the history of Arda, he burned the ships.

He left Fingolfin's host — Fingolfin was his half-brother and leader of the largest portion of the Noldor — stranded on the shores of Middle-earth's north, with no way across the sea except the Helcaraxë.

The Helcaraxë was a grinding, deadly passage of broken ice between the world's northernmost land and the shores of Aman. It was not merely cold — it was a passage where the ice moved and ground together, where Elvish feet might break through into the freezing sea, where the cold was so terrible that it could kill even the Eldar. Fingolfin led his people across it anyway, in darkness and grief, with Morgoth's star Carcharoth howling in the distance and the northern lights playing overhead. Many were lost. Those who survived emerged into Middle-earth as a people who had been tested to the uttermost limit of their endurance.

Fingolfin himself arrived in Middle-earth with his people, and the first thing they saw was the light of the Sun — because while they crossed the Helcaraxë, Morgoth had killed Finwë and taken the Silmarils, the Trees had been destroyed, and the Valar had taken the last fruit and flower of Laurelin and Telperion and set them sailing through the sky: the Moon and the Sun, whose rising marked the beginning of the First Age in the reckoning of most Elvish historians.

---

## Part 20: The Rising of the Moon and Sun — The Beginning of the First Age

The Moon was set sailing first. It was lit by Telperion's last flower, and its keeper was Tilion, a Maia who loved Telperion above all things and whose course was erratic — he sailed too close to the path of the Sun at times and had to be cautioned. The Moon gave a cold white light over Middle-earth.

Then came the Sun. Laurelin's last fruit was made into the vessel of the Sun, and its keeper was Arien, a Maia of great courage and incorruptible spirit — Morgoth himself feared her and could not corrupt her, though he tried. The Sun's first rising was seen by the Noldor under Fingolfin as they emerged from the Helcaraxë: a red dawn in the west, which they interpreted as a sign of fire, and then the great blaze of the Sun coming above the horizon.

Middle-earth had been in darkness or starlight for the entirety of its existence. The rising of the Sun changed everything. Trees and plants that had existed only in cold and dark began to grow as they never had before. Animals changed. The Elves who had lived in starlight — the Sindar of Beleriand, the scattered survivors of the ages of darkness — saw the Sun for the first time.

And Men awoke.

The Second Children of Ilúvatar awoke in the east of Middle-earth — the land called Hildórien — at the rising of the Sun. This is the key synchronicity of Tolkien's mythology: Men and the Sun are linked. Men are mortals who age and die; the Sun is the vessel that provides the rhythm of their lives, the measure of their time. The Elves are linked to stars and the deep past; Men are linked to the present, to the moving light of day.

The Men who awoke in Hildórien knew very little of the world. They were quick to learn, but also quick to be led astray. Morgoth found them before the Valar did. He was in the east while the great battles were being fought in the west, and he brought his influence to bear on the first Men — teaching them to fear death, to distrust the Valar, to see themselves as abandoned and small in a large hostile world. This early corruption of the Men of the east would have consequences that lasted through the Second and Third Ages and into the time of the War of the Ring itself.

But some Men — those who later became the Edain, the Men of the Three Houses who eventually found their way into Beleriand — escaped this early corruption, or escaped it substantially. They came west, following rumours of Elvish lands, drawn by the light of the stars and the stories they had heard of Oromë's presence in the world. And when they crossed the Blue Mountains into Beleriand, they found the Noldor and the Sindar in the midst of the great struggle of the First Age.

---

## Conclusion to Part One: The Stage Is Set

We have now traced the history of the world from before its making to the beginning of the First Age proper — from the Ainulindalë through the shaping of Arda, the awakening of the Elves, the time of the Trees, the catastrophe of Morgoth's theft and the death of the Trees, the Oath of Fëanor, the Kinslaying, the Burning of the Ships and the Crossing of the Helcaraxë, the rising of the Moon and Sun, and the awakening of Men.

The stage is now set for the vast tragedy and heroism of the First Age itself: the long wars of Beleriand, the stories of Beren and Lúthien and Túrin Turambar and Tuor and Eärendil, the eventual War of Wrath that ended Morgoth's power, and the reshaping of the world that preceded the Second Age.

The world of Middle-earth is already, by the time of the First Age's dawn, a world marked by loss and by the beauty that exists in the face of that loss. The Elves who came to Beleriand brought with them the memory of Valinor's light and the guilt of Alqualondë's blood. The Men who entered Beleriand brought with them the freshness and the vulnerability of a new people. And Morgoth sat in Angband with the Silmarils set in his Iron Crown, and planned.

- Tolkien, J.R.R. *Unfinished Tales of Númenor and Middle-earth*, edited by Christopher Tolkien. George Allen & Unwin, 1980. Contains crucial supplementary material on the Great Journey, Tuor, and other topics.
