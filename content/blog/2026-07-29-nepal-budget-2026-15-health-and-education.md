We have spent two posts on physical capital — the energy and the infrastructure that a country builds. Now we turn to something economists call *human capital*, an awkward phrase for a profound idea: that the health, knowledge, and capabilities of a country's people are themselves a form of wealth, as real and as important to its future as any road or power plant. A healthy, educated population is more productive, more prosperous, more capable of building a good society; an unhealthy, poorly-educated one is held back no matter how much physical infrastructure it has. So the budget's spending on health and education is, in the deepest sense, an investment in the country's future — perhaps the most important investment of all, because everything else depends on the capabilities of the people.

This budget allocates substantial sums to both: about **NPR 218.30 billion (around USD 1.46 billion)** to education and about **NPR 101.95 billion (around USD 0.68 billion)** to health, and it makes ambitious, humane promises — sweeping health-insurance coverage, hundreds of new hospitals, free cancer treatment for children, scholarships, new educational institutions. These are genuinely good aspirations, and parts of the agenda deserve real praise. But this post will insist on a distinction that is the key to judging all human-capital spending, and that the warm language of "investing in our people" is designed to blur: the difference between *inputs* — the rupees spent, the buildings built, the schemes announced — and *outcomes* — people actually healthier, actually better educated, actually better served. Counting the money is not the same as counting the results, and a budget can spend impressively on health and education while delivering disappointingly, if the spending does not translate into outcomes. By the end you will be able to read these allocations and these promises and ask the question that matters: not how much was spent, but what it actually achieved.

## A note on numbers: sector versus ministry

Before the analysis, a clarification that matters for honesty and that illustrates a small but real way budget figures can mislead. When we cite health spending, we must distinguish between the *ministry* allocation and the *sector* total. The Ministry of Health and Population has an allocation of about NPR 96.43 billion (around USD 0.64 billion). But the total spending on the health *sector* — which includes health-related spending that flows through other channels, such as the conditional grants for health that go to provincial and local governments, and other health spending outside the ministry's own budget line — is larger, on the order of NPR 101.95 billion (around USD 0.68 billion). These are different numbers measuring different things: the ministry figure is what the central health ministry directly controls; the sector figure is the broader total of health-related spending across the government. Neither is wrong, but they are not interchangeable, and a careless or motivated presenter can cite whichever serves their argument — the larger sector figure to claim generosity, the smaller ministry figure to claim stinginess. We will use the sector figure of about NPR 101.95 billion for health, and note that the precise definitions and the split between the ministry and the broader sector should be taken from the budget documents. The lesson generalises: when comparing spending figures, always check whether you are comparing like with like — ministry to ministry, or sector to sector — because mixing them is an easy way to make spending look larger or smaller than it is.

## Putting the allocations in perspective

How large are these allocations, really? We learned in Part 5 that bigness is meaningless until scaled, so let us scale them — by population and as a share of the budget — because the results are sobering and frame everything that follows.

Education's NPR 218.30 billion is about 10 percent of the total budget — a significant share, the second-largest after infrastructure, reflecting education's priority. Divided among Nepal's roughly thirty million people, it works out to about **NPR 7,280 per person (around USD 49)** for the year — the amount the government plans to spend on education for each Nepali. Health's NPR 101.95 billion is about 5 percent of the budget, and divided by the population works out to about **NPR 3,400 per person (around USD 23)** for the year — the amount of public spending on health per Nepali. Pause on those per-person figures, because they are the reality beneath the large-sounding totals. The government plans to spend, per person, about 49 dollars on education and about 23 dollars on health, for the entire year. These are small amounts — strikingly small to anyone accustomed to the health and education spending of wealthier countries — and they reflect the fundamental fact we established at the very beginning: Nepal is a poor country, with limited resources, trying to provide health and education to thirty million people on a per-person budget that is, by the standards of the rich world, tiny.

This perspective is essential for judging the budget's promises, and it cuts in two directions. On one hand, it should make us *sympathetic* to the difficulty of the task: providing decent health and education to thirty million people on about 23 and 49 dollars per person respectively is genuinely hard, and the government deserves recognition for the constraint it works within rather than blame for not achieving rich-country outcomes on poor-country budgets. On the other hand, it should make us *skeptical* of grand promises, because ambitious commitments — sweeping health coverage, hundreds of hospitals, transformative education — must somehow fit within these small per-person sums, alongside the salaries and operating costs that consume most of the spending. When a budget promises, on about 23 dollars per person, to dramatically expand health coverage and build hundreds of hospitals and provide free specialised treatments, the per-person reality is the check: can all of that genuinely be delivered, for everyone, within that small sum? The per-person figures do not prove the promises are undeliverable, but they are the reality against which the promises must be measured, and they counsel that the gap between ambitious promise and achievable outcome may be wide. Keep these figures — about 23 dollars per person for health, about 49 for education — in mind as we examine the promises, because they are the budget constraint that ambition must respect.

## The recurrent reality: most of it is salaries

Before examining the specific promises, one more crucial reality about health and education spending, connecting to the recurrent-versus-capital distinction from Part 6: *most* of these allocations is recurrent spending, and most of that is *salaries*. The bulk of the education budget goes to paying teachers; the bulk of the health budget goes to paying health workers and running facilities. This is not a criticism — teachers and health workers are exactly what education and health *require*, and paying them is the core of the service. But it means two things a citizen should understand. First, the room for *new* initiatives — new hospitals, new programmes, expanded coverage — is limited, because most of the money is already committed to the salaries and operations that keep the existing system running; the headline allocation is not a pot of free money for new promises but largely a continuation of the existing payroll. Second, it means that the *quality* of health and education depends heavily on the quality and deployment of these salaried workers — on whether teachers actually teach well and are present in their schools, whether health workers are skilled and posted where they are needed — rather than simply on the amount spent. A budget can increase the health and education allocations (mostly increasing salaries and the existing system's costs) without improving the outcomes, if the additional spending does not translate into better teaching, better care, or better-served people. This is the input-output gap in its starkest form: the inputs (rupees, salaries, the existing system) can grow while the outcomes (learning, health) stagnate, if the spending does not actually improve what the system delivers. Hold this as we examine the promises, because it underlies why spending more does not automatically mean achieving more.

## The health promises

This budget makes several ambitious and genuinely humane promises on health. Let us examine them, giving real credit where it is due and applying the inputs-versus-outcomes test throughout.

The headline promise is to expand **health insurance coverage to 90 percent of the population within three years**, backed by a substantial allocation (on the order of NPR 15 billion, around USD 100 million) for the health-insurance programme. Health insurance — a system in which people are covered for the cost of their healthcare, protecting them from the financial catastrophe that illness can bring to a poor family — is a genuinely important goal, and expanding it toward universal coverage is exactly the kind of aspiration a developing country should have. If achieved, near-universal health insurance would be transformative, sparing millions from the impossible choice between medical care and financial ruin. So the *aspiration* deserves real praise. But apply the inputs-outcomes test, and the skepticism is warranted, because health-insurance schemes have a particular and well-documented gap between enrolment and effectiveness. *Enrolling* people in an insurance scheme (an input — a number the government can announce: "X million enrolled," "90 percent covered") is not the same as those people *actually receiving good care when they need it* (the outcome). Insurance schemes can have high enrolment figures while suffering from low *utilisation* (people enrolled but not actually using the coverage, perhaps because they do not understand it, or because the covered services are not available near them), from *inadequate covered services* (the insurance covers too little, or the quality of covered care is poor), from *delayed or denied claims* (the scheme not actually paying for the care it promised), and from *sustainability problems* (the scheme running short of money, unable to pay providers, who then stop accepting it). So "90 percent covered" could be an impressive input figure masking a disappointing outcome reality, if the coverage is shallow, the care is poor, or the scheme cannot actually deliver. The promise of 90 percent coverage in three years is also, given the per-person health budget of about 23 dollars and the recurrent reality, genuinely ambitious — and ambitious coverage promises have a history, in Nepal and elsewhere, of slipping past their deadlines. The right stance is to welcome the aspiration while watching the outcome: not "how many were enrolled?" but "did enrolled people actually receive good care, were claims actually paid, and is the scheme actually sustainable?"

The budget also promises to build **336 basic hospitals** — a large number of new health facilities to expand access, particularly in underserved areas. Again, the aspiration is good: more health facilities, closer to more people, is a real need in a country where many live far from any hospital. But here the inputs-outcomes test, combined with the capital-absorption lesson from the infrastructure post, raises sharp questions. *Building* 336 hospitals (an input — and one subject to all the capital-absorption problems we examined: will they actually be built, on time, given the chronic under-spending of capital budgets?) is not the same as those hospitals *actually functioning and delivering care* (the outcome). A hospital building is not a hospital; a functioning hospital requires staff (doctors, nurses, technicians — who must be trained, recruited, and willing to work in remote areas, a notorious difficulty), equipment, supplies, medicines, and ongoing operating funds. Nepal's history includes health facilities built but then under-staffed, under-equipped, or under-utilised — buildings that exist but do not deliver the care they were meant to, because the recurrent funding and the staff to operate them did not follow the capital funding to build them. So "336 hospitals" could be an impressive construction target (if even achieved, given capital-absorption problems) that delivers far less in actual healthcare than the number implies, if the hospitals are built but not properly staffed, equipped, and funded to operate. The right question is not "were the hospitals built?" but "are they functioning, staffed, equipped, and actually delivering care?"

The budget makes the specific, touching promise of **free treatment for childhood cancer** in government hospitals. This is a genuinely good and humane commitment — sparing families the devastating cost of a child's cancer treatment is exactly the kind of thing a compassionate state should do, and it deserves unreserved praise as an aspiration. The questions, as with all such promises, are about delivery: is the free treatment actually available, at facilities families can reach, with the specialists and equipment that cancer treatment requires (which are concentrated in a few centres), and is it actually funded sufficiently to deliver on the promise rather than to be free in name but unavailable in practice? A promise of free specialised treatment is only as good as the actual availability of that treatment, and childhood cancer care requires specialised facilities that may not be widely available. So even this admirable promise must be judged by whether the care is actually delivered, not just by the announcement that it is free. There are also other health commitments — safe-motherhood programmes, and various other initiatives — each of which deserves the same treatment: credit for the aspiration, skepticism about delivery, and attention to outcomes rather than announcements.

## The education promises

On education, the budget likewise makes substantial commitments, and we apply the same lens. The large allocation (about NPR 218.30 billion) funds, predominantly, the running of the existing education system — the salaries of teachers, the operation of schools — which is the core of education provision and rightly the bulk of the spending. On top of this, the budget makes various specific commitments worth examining.

There is significant funding for **scholarships** (on the order of NPR 8.60 billion, around USD 57 million) — support to help students, particularly from disadvantaged backgrounds, access education. Scholarships are a genuinely good tool for expanding educational access and opportunity, and the allocation is meaningful. The outcome question is whether the scholarships actually reach the students who most need them and actually enable them to access and complete quality education — whether they translate into educational attainment, not just disbursement.

There are commitments around **new and expanded educational institutions** — including provisions for foreign universities to operate in Nepal (intended to expand higher-education options and stem the outflow of students abroad), an Open University (for distance and flexible learning), residential schools (for certain populations), and the development of health-sciences education such as medical (MBBS) programmes at institutions like the one at Geta. These are aimed at expanding and improving education, particularly higher education, which is a real need — Nepal loses many of its students to study abroad, often permanently, and expanding quality higher education at home could help. The aspirations are reasonable. But the inputs-outcomes test applies: establishing institutions (an input) is not the same as those institutions delivering quality education that improves students' lives and the country's human capital (the outcome), and the history of educational expansion includes institutions established but of poor quality, or under-resourced, or not delivering the outcomes hoped for. The deeper question for education, as for health, is not how much is spent or how many institutions are established, but whether students are actually *learning* — whether the quality of education is improving, whether students are gaining the knowledge and skills that education is supposed to provide. And on this deepest outcome, the evidence in many developing countries (and the concern in Nepal) is that increased spending and expanded access have not always translated into improved *learning* — that children may be in school (an input — enrolment) without actually learning much (the outcome — the "learning crisis" that researchers have documented in many countries, where schooling has expanded but learning has lagged). So the education budget, like the health budget, must be judged not by the rupees or the institutions but by whether Nepal's people are actually becoming better educated — actually learning more — which is the outcome that all the inputs are supposed to serve and which spending alone does not guarantee.

## The honest verdict: good aspirations, the outcome question

The honest assessment of the budget's health and education agenda, holding both hands as always, is this. The *aspirations* are genuinely good and humane — expanding health coverage, building health facilities, providing free care for sick children, supporting students, expanding education. A budget that prioritises health and education, and that aims to expand access and protect families from the costs of illness and the barriers to learning, is pursuing exactly the right goals, and the new government deserves real credit for the humanity and ambition of these commitments. There is nothing cynical to say about *wanting* near-universal health coverage and educated children; these are precisely what a good society pursues.

But the *delivery* is the question, and on delivery the appropriate stance is skeptical vigilance, for reasons this post has established. The promises must fit within a small per-person budget (about 23 dollars for health, 49 for education) most of which is already committed to salaries and the existing system. The construction promises (hospitals) are subject to the capital-absorption problems that have plagued all Nepali building. The coverage and service promises (insurance, free treatment) are subject to the gap between enrolment and actual care, between buildings and functioning services, between access and quality. And the deepest outcomes — people actually healthier, children actually learning — depend not on the spending but on whether the spending translates into better-delivered services, which it does not automatically do. So the verdict is: praise the aspirations, watch the outcomes, and refuse to mistake the inputs (rupees, buildings, enrolment, institutions) for the outcomes (health, learning, served people) that they are merely supposed to produce. A citizen who holds this stance honours the genuine goodness of the goals while insisting on the only thing that ultimately matters — whether the goals are actually achieved in the lives of the people they are meant to serve.

## A worked example: the insurance card that bought nothing

Make the inputs-outcomes gap concrete with a composite example drawn from the documented difficulties of health-insurance schemes, because it captures the whole lesson of this post.

Imagine a family in a rural district, enrolled — perhaps automatically, as part of the drive toward 90 percent coverage — in the national health-insurance scheme. They have a card; they are counted in the impressive enrolment figures; the government can cite them as part of the "90 percent covered." By the input measure, they are a success: another family insured, another step toward universal coverage. Now follow what happens when illness actually comes. A member of the family falls seriously ill and needs care. But the nearest facility that accepts the insurance and can provide the needed care is far away, difficult and expensive to reach from their remote village — so the coverage, though real on paper, is hard to actually use. When they do reach a facility, they find that the covered services are limited, that the medicines or treatments they need are not fully covered or not available, so they must pay out of pocket anyway for much of the care — the insurance covering less than the illness costs. Or they find that the facility, though it accepts the insurance in principle, is reluctant because the scheme has been slow to pay providers, who are owed money and wary of treating insured patients who may not bring prompt payment. Or the claim for their care is delayed or partially denied, the promised coverage not actually materialising when needed. In the end, the family — counted as "covered," part of the proud enrolment figures — receives far less actual protection than the coverage promised, perhaps facing much of the financial burden of the illness despite being "insured." Their insurance card, an impressive input, bought them little actual care, a disappointing outcome.

This example — and the difficulties it describes are documented realities of insurance schemes, not pessimistic invention — illustrates the entire lesson of this post. The *input* (enrolment, the card, the "90 percent covered" figure) was real and announceable; the *outcome* (actual financial protection and access to care when needed) fell far short, because of the gaps between enrolment and access, between coverage and actual payment, between the promise and the delivery. A government can truthfully announce impressive coverage figures while the actual protection delivered is disappointing, and the gap is invisible in the headline enrolment number — visible only in the experience of families like this one when illness actually comes. The lesson for a citizen is to refuse to be satisfied with the input figures — the enrolment, the coverage percentage, the hospitals built, the institutions established — and to ask always about the outcomes: when people actually need care, do they get it? When children are actually in school, are they actually learning? When the insurance is actually called upon, does it actually pay? Those outcome questions, not the impressive input figures, are the true measure of whether the budget's health and education spending is achieving its humane goals — and they are exactly the questions the warm language of "investing in our people" and the proud announcement of coverage figures are designed to keep from being asked.

## For the technically minded: the allocations as data, and the per-person reality

For readers who work with numbers, here are the health and education allocations in schematic form, with the per-person figures that provide the essential reality check:

| Sector / item | NPR | USD (≈) | Per person (≈) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Education (total) | 218.30 billion | 1.46 billion | NPR 7,280 (USD 49) | ~10% of budget; mostly teacher salaries |
| — Scholarships (within education) | 8.60 billion | 57 million | — | Access support |
| Health (sector total) | 101.95 billion | 0.68 billion | NPR 3,400 (USD 23) | ~5% of budget; mostly salaries/operations |
| — Health ministry (subset) | 96.43 billion | 0.64 billion | — | Direct ministry control |
| — Health insurance programme | ~15 billion | ~100 million | — | Toward 90% coverage in 3 years |

The decisive columns are the per-person figures: about 49 dollars per person for education and 23 for health, for the whole year. Now a sketch of the discipline that turns allocations into the reality check, and that distinguishes inputs from outcomes:

```text
# Always convert a big allocation to a per-person figure to feel its real size.
def per_person(allocation_npr_billion, population=30_000_000, fx=150):
    npr_per_person = allocation_npr_billion * 1_000_000_000 / population
    usd_per_person = npr_per_person / fx
    return npr_per_person, usd_per_person

per_person(101.95)   # health: ~NPR 3,400 (~USD 23) per person per year
per_person(218.30)   # education: ~NPR 7,280 (~USD 49) per person per year

# The input-output gap, made explicit. Do NOT confuse the left with the right:
INPUTS  = ["rupees allocated", "hospitals built", "people enrolled in insurance",
           "institutions established", "scholarships disbursed"]
OUTCOMES = ["people actually healthier", "hospitals actually delivering care",
            "insured people actually treated and claims actually paid",
            "students actually learning", "lives actually improved"]
# A budget reports the INPUTS. The truth is in the OUTCOMES, which inputs do not guarantee.
# Judge by OUTCOMES; treat INPUTS as promises to be tested, not achievements.
```

The code embodies the two disciplines of this post. The per-person function turns the large-sounding allocations into their sobering reality — about 23 and 49 dollars per person — which is the budget constraint that all the ambitious promises must respect. And the explicit separation of inputs from outcomes captures the central lesson: a budget reports inputs (rupees, buildings, enrolment, institutions), but the truth that matters is in the outcomes (health, functioning facilities, actual treatment, actual learning), which inputs do not automatically produce. A developer or analyst evaluating health and education spending would track not the inputs the budget announces but the outcomes — coverage that actually delivers care, schools where children actually learn — because those are what the spending is for. And a citizen, equipped with the same logic, knows to scale every allocation to its per-person reality and to ask always about outcomes rather than inputs, which is the only way to judge whether the budget's genuinely good human-capital aspirations are actually being achieved.

## How budgets deceive: input-counting

The technique of deception for this post is one of the most pervasive in all of social policy, because it exploits our natural tendency to credit visible effort and visible spending: **input-counting** — the presentation of inputs (money spent, things built, people enrolled, programmes launched) as though they were outcomes (people actually better served), and the celebration of the former in place of the latter.

Here is why the deception is so natural and so common. Inputs are *easy to measure, easy to announce, and easy to credit*: a government can state, precisely and impressively, how many rupees it allocated to health, how many hospitals it will build, how many people it enrolled in insurance, how many scholarships it disbursed, how many institutions it established. These input figures are concrete, announceable, and satisfying — they sound like achievement. Outcomes, by contrast, are *hard to measure, slow to materialise, and uncomfortable to confront*: whether people are actually healthier, whether children are actually learning, whether the insured actually got care, whether the hospitals actually function — these are difficult to measure, take years to show, and frequently disappoint. So there is a powerful temptation for a government to *celebrate the inputs* — the spending, the buildings, the enrolment, the programmes — as though they were the achievement, while the outcomes, which are what actually matter and which are far less flattering, go unmeasured and unmentioned. "We allocated a record amount to health"; "we will build 336 hospitals"; "we have covered 90 percent of the population" — these are all input claims, and they invite the public to feel that health has been achieved, when what has actually been achieved is spending and building and enrolment, which are merely supposed to produce health and may or may not actually do so. The deception is in presenting the input as though it were the outcome — in letting the impressive, measurable, announceable input stand in for the difficult, slow, often-disappointing outcome it is meant to produce.

The defence is to *insist on outcomes, and to treat inputs as promises rather than achievements*. Whenever a government claims an achievement in health, education, or any social sector, ask: is this an *input* (money spent, things built, people enrolled, programmes launched) or an *outcome* (people actually healthier, children actually learning, the served actually better off)? Almost always it is an input — and the right response is to treat it not as an achievement but as a *promise* whose fulfilment depends on whether it actually produces the outcome, and then to ask after the outcome itself: not "how much was spent on health?" but "did health improve?"; not "how many hospitals were built?" but "are they functioning and delivering care?"; not "how many were enrolled?" but "did they actually get treated?"; not "how many children are in school?" but "are they actually learning?" A citizen who insists on outcomes, who refuses to credit inputs as though they were results, and who asks always whether the spending and building and enrolling actually translated into better lives, cannot be deceived by input-counting — and applies the right test to this budget's genuinely good health and education aspirations: not whether the money was allocated and the promises made (it was, they were), but whether, over the coming years, Nepalis actually become healthier and better educated as a result. That outcome, measured in the actual health and learning of the people, not the inputs announced in the budget, is the only true measure of whether the investment in human capital succeeds. The deception thrives on crediting inputs; the defence is to credit only outcomes, and to treat every input as a promise still to be kept.
