We come now to the largest single ministry allocation in the entire budget, and to the problem that has frustrated Nepal's development more than any other. The Ministry of Physical Infrastructure and Transport receives about **NPR 302.83 billion (around USD 2.02 billion)** — more than any other ministry, reflecting the central place that roads, bridges, and physical connectivity hold in the country's development ambitions. And yet infrastructure, the area where Nepal allocates the most, is also the area where its deepest dysfunction is most visible: a chronic, decades-long inability to actually *spend* the money it sets aside for building, to spend it *on time*, to spend it *without massive cost overruns*, and to spend it on work of adequate *quality*. The story of Nepali infrastructure is, too often, the story of money allocated but not spent, projects announced but not completed, and roads that appear in budget after budget while remaining unbuilt.

This post examines that story. We will look at why infrastructure looms so large for Nepal, at the flagship projects the budget funds, and then — at length, because it is the single most important fact about Nepali capital spending — at the *capital-absorption problem*: the chronic failure to spend the capital budget. We will examine why projects stall, the difference between allocating money and building something, the curse of cost overruns and poor quality, and the role of foreign-funded versus domestically-funded projects. And we will expose the deception that this dysfunction breeds: *ribbon-cutting accounting* — the political art of repeatedly announcing the same unbuilt project, harvesting credit for an intention that is never fulfilled. By the end you will understand why, in Nepal, the problem of infrastructure has never been a shortage of money allocated, but a failure to turn that money into roads — and why this is the truest test of whether this ambitious government can do what its predecessors could not.

## Why infrastructure looms so large

To understand why infrastructure gets the largest ministry allocation, return to the picture of Nepal we drew at the very beginning: a small, vertical, landlocked country, where the geography that gives it hydropower also makes it brutally hard to connect. Infrastructure — above all roads, but also bridges, airports, and the physical scaffolding of a modern economy — is the answer to that geography, and the lack of it is one of the country's deepest constraints on development.

Consider what inadequate infrastructure does to Nepal. It *isolates regions*: communities cut off by the lack of a road, or by a road that washes out each monsoon, are isolated from markets, from healthcare, from education, from the rest of the national economy — their goods expensive to move, their access to services precarious. It *raises the cost of everything*: in a country where moving goods is slow and difficult, the cost of transport is built into the price of all goods, making everything more expensive and the economy less competitive. It *holds back industry*: businesses cannot thrive where they cannot reliably move inputs and products, where power and connectivity are poor. And it *deepens inequality between places*: the connected centres prosper while the unconnected peripheries are left behind, feeding exactly the regional grievances that federalism was meant to address. So infrastructure is not a luxury or an adornment for Nepal; it is, alongside electricity, one of the two physical foundations on which the country's development depends, and the largest allocation reflects a genuine and correct recognition of its importance. The need is enormous and the priority is justified. The problem, as we will see, has never been the recognition or the allocation. It has been the doing.

## The flagship projects

The infrastructure allocation funds a vast array of projects, from major national highways to local roads, bridges, airports, and more. The budget highlights certain *flagship* projects — the large, nationally significant works that anchor the country's infrastructure ambitions. Among those that recur in Nepal's infrastructure planning are the upgrading of the **East-West Highway** — the great horizontal artery, sometimes called the Mahendra Highway, that runs the length of the southern Terai and is the country's most important road, with ambitions to expand it to four lanes — and the **Pushpalal Mid-Hill Highway**, a major route through the middle hills intended to connect the hill districts along the country's spine. Beyond roads, the country's infrastructure ambitions extend to studies and plans for railways and urban mass transit (metros), and to airport development, including the newer regional international airports built to expand the country's air connectivity. (The specific projects emphasised in this budget, their allocations, and their target dates should be taken from the budget documents and the Ministry of Physical Infrastructure and Transport; the projects named here are among the well-known flagships of Nepal's infrastructure planning, and their details should be verified against the budget.)

These flagship projects are genuinely important — completing the East-West Highway's expansion, connecting the hills, building the rail and transit and air links the country needs would be transformative. And the budget's emphasis on them reflects, again, a correct understanding of what Nepal needs to build. But here is where the recurring caution of this series becomes the central theme rather than a footnote. Because the history of these very flagship projects — and of Nepali infrastructure projects generally — is a history of *delay, cost overrun, and chronic under-completion*. Many of Nepal's flagship infrastructure projects have been under construction or "planned" for years, even decades, appearing in budget after budget, their completion dates repeatedly pushed back, their costs repeatedly ballooning, their progress repeatedly disappointing. The announcement of a flagship project in a budget is, by now, something a Nepali citizen has learned to greet with weary skepticism, having seen the same projects announced before. So while the flagship projects matter and the allocation to them is justified, the question is not whether they are worthy — they are — but whether *this* budget and *this* government will actually build them, when so many budgets and governments before have allocated the money and failed to deliver the roads. And that question leads us directly to the heart of the matter.

## The capital-absorption problem

Here is the single most important fact about Nepali infrastructure and capital spending, the fact we have previewed repeatedly throughout this series and now examine in full: **Nepal chronically fails to spend its capital budget.** Year after year, after the budget allocates large sums for building infrastructure, the fiscal year ends with a substantial portion of that capital budget *unspent* — money set aside for roads and bridges and projects that was never actually disbursed, never turned into construction, returned at year's end as authority that lapsed unused. This is the **capital-absorption problem** — the failure to "absorb," to actually spend, the capital budget — and it is, more than any shortage of allocation, the binding constraint on Nepal's infrastructure development.

Let us be clear about what this means and why it is so damaging. It means that the problem is *not primarily a shortage of money allocated*. The budget allocates large sums to capital spending — the NPR 302.83 billion to infrastructure, part of the NPR 431.10 billion total capital budget. The problem is the *failure to spend what is allocated*. A government can allocate ever-larger sums to infrastructure, and trumpet the size of its "development budget," while actually building far less than the allocation implies, because a large share of the money never gets spent. This is why, as we noted in the post on recurrent versus capital spending, the *real, executed* capital budget is even smaller than the allocated one: not only is capital allocated only about a fifth of the total budget, but a meaningful portion of even that fifth goes unspent, so the amount actually turned into infrastructure is smaller still. The recurrent spending — salaries, operations, interest — gets spent in full, because it is easy to spend; the capital spending lags, because building is hard. The result is a country that consistently builds less infrastructure than it budgets for, year after year, with the gap between allocation and actual building being one of the most persistent features of its public finances.

And there is a particularly damaging pattern within this failure: the *year-end spending rush*. Because the capital budget goes largely unspent through the early and middle months of the fiscal year — as projects fail to get going, for reasons we will examine — there is often a frantic scramble in the *final months and weeks* of the fiscal year to spend the money before the authority lapses, before the unspent allocation is lost. This year-end rush is doubly damaging. First, it means much capital spending happens in a hurried, last-minute fashion that is conducive to *waste, poor quality, and corruption* — money spent in haste, on work done quickly to meet a deadline rather than carefully, with inadequate oversight in the rush, and with the pressure to disburse creating opportunities for misuse. Second, it distorts the whole rhythm of construction, cramming work that should have been spread across the year into a frantic finish. So the capital-absorption problem is not only that money goes unspent, but that what *is* spent is often spent badly, in a year-end rush that undermines its quality and its honesty. A citizen evaluating infrastructure should therefore watch not just whether the capital budget was spent, but *when* and *how* — because a high spending figure achieved through a chaotic year-end rush may have produced worse infrastructure than a lower figure spent steadily and carefully.

## Why projects stall

The capital-absorption problem is not random or mysterious; it has consistent, identifiable causes, and understanding them is essential both to judging whether this budget will overcome the problem and to seeing why simply allocating more money does not solve it. Let us examine the chain of obstacles that stalls infrastructure projects, because each link in the chain is a place where building gets stuck.

The first obstacle is *project readiness*. Many projects are allocated money in the budget before they are actually *ready* to be built — before the detailed designs are complete, before the feasibility studies are done, before the necessary preparations are in place. Money is allocated to an idea rather than to a shovel-ready project, and so the early months of the fiscal year are consumed not in building but in the preparatory work that should have been done *before* the money was allocated, delaying any actual construction. A budget that allocates money to projects that are not ready to build is, in effect, allocating money that cannot be spent productively in the year it is given.

The second obstacle is *procurement* — the process of advertising the work, receiving and evaluating bids from contractors, and awarding the contract. This process, governed by public-procurement rules meant to ensure fairness and value, is in Nepal frequently slow, cumbersome, and prone to delays, disputes, and legal challenges. A procurement process that drags on for months delays the start of construction by those months, eating into the building season. And procurement is also a notorious site of *corruption and collusion* — of bid-rigging, of contracts steered to favoured contractors, of the manipulation of the process — which both delays projects and degrades their quality and value.

The third obstacle is *land acquisition* — obtaining the land on which infrastructure must be built. This is one of the most intractable problems in Nepali (and not only Nepali) infrastructure, because acquiring land means dealing with its owners, with compensation disputes, with people who do not wish to sell or to be displaced, with legal challenges, and with the slow machinery of acquisition. A road or a project can be fully funded and fully designed and still be unable to proceed because the land it must be built on has not been acquired, tied up in disputes that can last years. Land acquisition delays are among the most common reasons Nepali infrastructure projects stall.

The fourth obstacle is *contractor and institutional capacity*. Building large infrastructure requires capable contractors with the equipment, expertise, and financial capacity to do the work, and capable government agencies to design, supervise, and manage the projects. Nepal's pool of such capacity is limited; contractors may be over-stretched across too many projects, may lack the capacity to deliver, or may abandon or delay projects; and the government agencies that must supervise may be under-resourced and overwhelmed. Limited capacity, on both the contractor and the government side, is a fundamental constraint on how much building can actually happen, regardless of how much money is allocated.

And the fifth obstacle, which compounds all the others, is the one we met in the post on the calendar: the *monsoon and the fiscal-year timing*. The fiscal year begins in mid-July, in the middle of the monsoon, when construction is hardest, and the early months — when projects should be getting going — coincide with the rains that make building difficult or impossible. So the obstacles of readiness, procurement, and land acquisition play out against a calendar that already compresses the usable building season, and the combination produces the slow start and the year-end rush.

Notice what this chain of obstacles reveals, and it is the crucial point for judging this budget: *the binding constraint on Nepali infrastructure is not money — it is the capacity to spend money well.* Project readiness, procurement, land acquisition, contractor and institutional capacity — these are problems of *capacity and process*, not of *funding*. Allocating more money to infrastructure does nothing to fix any of them; a project that cannot proceed because its land is not acquired or its design is not ready or its procurement is stalled will not proceed faster because more money was allocated to it. This is why simply increasing the infrastructure allocation — which budgets love to do, because the allocation is announceable — does not increase the infrastructure built, if the capacity constraints remain. And it is why the true test of whether this government can transform Nepal's infrastructure is not whether it allocated a large sum — every government does — but whether it can fix the *process and capacity* problems that have caused the chronic under-spending: making projects ready before funding them, reforming and speeding procurement, resolving land acquisition, building contractor and institutional capacity. A budget that allocated a large sum while doing nothing about these binding constraints would simply reproduce the chronic under-spending under new management. A citizen should therefore look past the impressive allocation to ask whether this budget and this government are actually tackling the capacity and process problems that determine whether the money becomes roads — because that, not the size of the allocation, is what has always determined the outcome.

## Cost overruns and the quality problem

Two further afflictions of Nepali infrastructure deserve attention, because they mean that even the money that *is* spent often delivers less than it should: cost overruns and poor quality.

A *cost overrun* is when a project ends up costing far more than originally estimated — and Nepali infrastructure projects are notorious for them, with final costs often ballooning to multiples of the original estimates as projects drag on, designs change, delays accumulate, and (sometimes) corruption inflates costs. Cost overruns are damaging in several ways: they mean the country gets less infrastructure per rupee than planned, they consume budget that could have funded other projects, and they often signal the delay and mismanagement that plague the project. A project whose cost has doubled or tripled from its original estimate is a project that has, in an important sense, failed even if it is eventually completed, because it consumed far more resources than it should have. When you read of a flagship project's allocation, it is worth asking how that allocation compares to the original estimate, and whether the project has a history of cost escalation — because a project that keeps costing more is a warning sign.

The *quality problem* is that infrastructure built in Nepal is too often of poor quality — roads that deteriorate rapidly, work that does not meet standards, construction that fails prematurely. This connects directly to the year-end spending rush (work done in haste to meet the deadline), to weak supervision (under-resourced government oversight), and to corruption (substandard work by contractors who cut corners, sometimes in collusion with officials). Poor quality means that even infrastructure that is built may not last or perform as it should, so that the country must repair or rebuild it sooner, wasting further resources. A road that washes out in the first monsoon after it is built, or that crumbles within a few years, has not delivered the durable asset that capital spending is supposed to produce. So the quality problem means that the *real* output of infrastructure spending — durable, functioning assets that last — is even less than the spending figures suggest, just as the absorption problem means the spending is less than the allocation. At every stage — from allocation to actual spending to quality of what is built — the real delivery falls short of the headline, and a citizen who understands this chain of shortfalls reads infrastructure claims with appropriate skepticism.

## Foreign-funded versus domestically-funded projects

A brief but important distinction: not all infrastructure is funded the same way, and the funding source affects the project's character. Some major infrastructure is funded by *foreign* money — loans and grants from development partners like the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank, and bilateral donors — while other infrastructure is funded *domestically*, from the government's own resources. This distinction matters for several reasons. Foreign-funded projects often come with the funder's own *procurement rules, oversight, and standards*, which can mean better-managed projects and higher quality (because the funder insists on it), but also their own delays and conditions. Domestically-funded projects are under the government's own control and rules, for better and worse. The mix of foreign and domestic funding in the infrastructure budget affects how much of the building is subject to external discipline versus domestic process, and it connects back to the debt discussion: foreign-funded infrastructure adds to the external debt (if loans) or depends on donor priorities (if grants), while domestically-funded infrastructure draws on the government's own resources and domestic borrowing. A citizen examining a major project may find it useful to know how it is funded, because that shapes who controls it, what standards apply, and how its costs fall on the country's finances. The general point is that "infrastructure spending" is not monolithic; it is a mix of differently-funded, differently-managed work, and the character of each project depends in part on where its money comes from.

## A worked example: the highway that was always coming

Make the capital-absorption and ribbon-cutting problems concrete with a composite example of a kind that is depressingly familiar in Nepal: a flagship road that is perpetually "coming."

Imagine a major highway project — a road that would connect important regions, that everyone agrees is needed, that has genuine economic value. It is announced in a budget, with an allocation and a target completion date a few years out. A citizen reading that budget might reasonably expect the road to be built by the stated date. But follow it across the years. In the first year, much of the allocated money goes unspent, because the detailed designs are not ready and the land has not been acquired; the project barely begins. The next budget *re-announces* the project, with a new allocation and a pushed-back completion date, and again most of the money goes unspent — now because procurement is mired in disputes, or because a contractor has stalled, or because land acquisition is tangled in litigation. The budget after that re-announces it again, the cost estimate now substantially higher than the original (a cost overrun), the completion date pushed back again. And so it continues: the same project, announced and re-announced across budget after budget, its allocation provided year after year but largely unspent each year, its completion date a perpetually receding horizon, its cost ever climbing, while the road itself remains unbuilt or half-built. Citizens who have followed it have heard it announced so many times that the announcement means nothing to them. The road is "always coming" and never arrives.

This pattern — and it is a real and common pattern in Nepal, not an exaggeration — illustrates everything this post has discussed. It shows the *capital-absorption problem*: money allocated year after year but not spent, because the project cannot proceed. It shows the *binding capacity constraints*: design readiness, procurement, land acquisition, contractor capacity — the process problems that stall the project regardless of the money allocated. It shows *cost overruns*: the estimate climbing across the years of delay. And it sets up the deception we turn to now: the political *re-announcement* of the same unbuilt project, harvesting fresh credit each time for an intention never fulfilled. The lesson for a citizen is to recognise the pattern — to ask, when a flagship project is announced, whether it has been announced before, how many times, how its current cost compares to its original estimate, and how much of its previous allocations were actually spent. A project being announced for the fifth time, its cost tripled, its previous allocations largely unspent, is not a new commitment to celebrate; it is the latest verse of a familiar song, and a citizen who knows the song is not moved by hearing it again.

## For the technically minded: flagship projects and a spend-down detector

For readers who work with data, the right way to track infrastructure is by *execution*, not allocation — and a flagship-project tracker should follow each project across years, watching the gap between what was allocated and what was spent, and the drift in cost and completion date. Here is the schematic structure (to be filled from the budget and the Financial Comptroller General Office's execution data over time):

| Pushpalal Mid-Hill Highway | (from budget) | (compare) | (track) | (the execution rate) |
| Rail / metro studies and works | (from budget) | (compare) | (track) | (the execution rate) |
| Airport development | (from budget) | (compare) | (track) | (the execution rate) |

The decisive column is the last one: the *execution rate*, the share of the allocation that was actually spent. A project with a low execution rate year after year is the capital-absorption problem in action, regardless of how impressive its allocation. Now a sketch of how one would detect the damaging year-end spending rush in the expenditure data:

```text
# The capital-absorption problem shows up as a LOW execution rate
# and a SPIKE of spending in the final weeks of the fiscal year.

def assess_capital_spending(monthly_spend, allocation):
    total_spent = sum(monthly_spend)
    execution_rate = total_spent / allocation            # how much was actually spent
    last_month_share = monthly_spend[-1] / total_spent   # share crammed into the final month

    flags = []
    if execution_rate < 0.80:
        flags.append("LOW ABSORPTION: much of the capital budget went unspent")
    if last_month_share > 0.30:
        flags.append("YEAR-END RUSH: spending crammed into the final month -> quality/corruption risk")
    return execution_rate, flags

# A healthy capital budget: high execution rate, spending spread STEADILY across the year.
# The Nepali pattern: low execution rate AND a year-end spike -> money unspent, and what
# was spent was spent in haste. Watch BOTH numbers, not just the allocation.
```

The detector embodies the post's central lesson: infrastructure must be judged by *execution*, not allocation, and by the *pattern* of execution, not just the total. A low execution rate reveals the absorption problem; a year-end spike reveals the rushed, low-quality, corruption-prone spending that the absorption problem breeds. A developer or auditor tracking infrastructure would watch these numbers — the execution rate and the year-end concentration — across projects and years, because they reveal the truth that the allocation conceals: how much was actually built, and how well. And a citizen, equipped with the same logic even without the data, knows to ask not "how much was allocated?" but "how much was actually spent, when, and on work of what quality?" — the questions that separate real infrastructure progress from the announcement of it.

## How budgets deceive: ribbon-cutting accounting

The technique of deception for this post is the one the capital-absorption problem breeds, and it is among the most politically powerful in all of governance: **ribbon-cutting accounting** — the harvesting of political credit from the *announcement* and *inauguration* of infrastructure, repeatedly and prematurely, regardless of whether the infrastructure is actually completed and functioning.

The deception has two related forms. The first is *re-announcement*: as our worked example showed, a government can announce the same project — the same road, the same flagship — in budget after budget, harvesting fresh credit and attention each time, even though the project is not progressing and the previous announcements came to little. Because each announcement is reported as news and the *non-completion* is rarely reported, a government can accumulate credit for "committing to" a project many times over, while never actually delivering it — the announcement, not the road, becomes the recurring political product. The second form is *premature or hollow inauguration*: the literal ribbon-cutting of a project that is incomplete, or that does not function, or that will not last — the ceremony, the photographs, the claim of an achievement, attached to infrastructure that is not actually delivering the value it is supposed to. A road can be inaugurated with great fanfare and then prove to be incomplete, or to wash out, or to be of such poor quality that it fails within years — but the ribbon was cut, the credit was claimed, and the failure unfolds later and more quietly. In both forms, the deception exploits the gap between the *vivid, announceable, ceremonial moment* and the *diffuse, slow, often-disappointing reality* of whether the infrastructure actually got built, on time, on budget, and to a standard that lasts.

The defence is to *credit completed, functioning, durable infrastructure — and nothing else*. When a government claims an infrastructure achievement, ask: Is this project actually *complete and functioning*, or merely announced, begun, or inaugurated-but-incomplete? Has it been *announced before* — how many times — and what happened to those previous announcements and allocations? How does its actual cost compare to its original estimate? How much of its allocated money was actually *spent*, and how much built? And — the test that defeats the hollow inauguration — will it actually *last and function*, or is it the kind of rushed, poor-quality work that fails within years? A citizen who credits only genuinely completed, functioning, durable infrastructure — who treats announcements, re-announcements, and premature inaugurations as the empty gestures they often are — cannot be deceived by ribbon-cutting accounting. And such a citizen applies the right test to this budget's flagship projects: not "were they announced?" (they were) but "will they actually be built, this time, when so many announcements before came to little?" — a question answered not by the budget speech but by the execution data over the coming years, by whether the binding capacity constraints are actually tackled, and by whether the roads actually, finally, get built. The deception thrives on crediting announcements and ceremonies; the defence is to credit only roads that actually exist, function, and endure.
