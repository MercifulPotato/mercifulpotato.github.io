# The Voting Rights Act and the Path Forward: Why Congress Must Mandate a Single Statewide Multi-Member District for Every State in America

*Published June 25, 2026*

---

> *"Today's decision renders Section 2 all but a dead letter."*
> — Justice Elena Kagan, dissenting in *Louisiana v. Callais*, April 29, 2026

---

It is Wednesday morning, April 29, 2026. It is the sort of morning that will be remembered. Not for a hurricane or a market crash or the fall of a foreign government, but for six words written in an opinion by Justice Samuel Alito that arrived, like most acts of judicial demolition, in the polished language of technical legal analysis: that Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act of 1965 "is not designed to punish for the past."

With those words, and the 6–3 party-line vote that accompanied them, the Roberts Court completed a project that its chief architect — John Roberts himself — had begun in a Department of Justice conference room in 1981. The Voting Rights Act, the crown jewel of the Second Reconstruction, the document for which Amelia Boynton Robinson was beaten unconscious on the Edmund Pettus Bridge, the law that Lyndon Johnson signed with a pen he handed to Martin Luther King Jr., has been rendered, in the careful phrasing of Justice Elena Kagan, "all but a dead letter."

This article will not leave a single stone unturned in examining how we got here, what the ruling in *Louisiana v. Callais* actually does, what it means for the 2026 elections and beyond, what the Roberts Court's long campaign against minority voting rights looks like when you trace it from beginning to end, and — crucially — what the only real structural solution looks like, which is the congressional mandate of a single statewide multi-member district for every state in the United States of America, elected by ranked choice voting using the Single Transferable Vote method.

Because there is a solution. It is not complicated. It is arithmetic. And it requires Congress to do precisely what the Constitution says it has the power to do, and what it has — maddeningly — refused to do for decades.

## Part 1: The Original Architecture of Disenfranchisement — From the Founding to the Civil War

### 1.1 The Constitution's First and Most Consequential Compromise

You cannot understand the voting rights crisis of 2026 without understanding that the American constitutional system was built, from its very first words, around the management of white political power. This is not a polemical assertion. It is the finding of every serious constitutional historian who has ever examined the founding documents without romanticizing them.

The Constitutional Convention of 1787 in Philadelphia lasted four months. Of those four months, a significant portion was consumed by one question: how to count enslaved people. Southern slaveholding states wanted enslaved people counted for purposes of Congressional apportionment — which determined how many Representatives each state received — but explicitly did not want them counted as voters or citizens. Northern states, many of which had already or were moving toward abolition, initially resisted counting enslaved people at all for apportionment purposes, since doing so would hand disproportionate political power to slaveholders.

The result was the Three-Fifths Compromise, codified in Article I, Section 2 of the Constitution: "Representatives and direct Taxes shall be apportioned among the several States which may be included within this Union, according to their respective Numbers, which shall be determined by adding to the whole Number of free Persons, including those bound to Service for a Term of Years, and excluding Indians not taxed, three fifths of all other Persons."

"Three fifths of all other Persons." Enslaved human beings, reduced to a fraction of their humanity, not for purposes of representation — they received none — but for the sole purpose of magnifying the political power of the white men who enslaved them.

The practical effect was enormous. The Three-Fifths Compromise gave the slaveholding states approximately 20 additional seats in the House of Representatives during the antebellum period compared to what they would have received if enslaved people were not counted at all. This translated to dominance in the Electoral College and in the Senate. It is why slaveholders held the presidency for 32 of the first 36 years of the republic. It is why the Supreme Court was dominated by slaveholder-appointed justices for most of the antebellum period, which in turn explains the cultural confidence behind *Dred Scott v. Sandford* (1857), in which Chief Justice Roger Taney declared that Black Americans had "no rights which the white man was bound to respect."

The Three-Fifths Compromise was not a bug in the constitutional design. It was a feature. It was the price of Southern participation in the Union. And it established a principle that would endure, in various forms, for the next two and a half centuries: that the rules of political representation in America could be engineered to amplify the power of the dominant racial group at the expense of everyone else.

### 1.2 The Electoral Structure: How Single-Member Districts Were Born

Article I, Section 4 of the Constitution, known as the Elections Clause, states: "The Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives, shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof; but the Congress may at any time by Law make or alter such Regulations, except as to the Places of chusing Senators."

This is the constitutional foundation for everything that follows. Congress has explicit, plenary authority over how House elections are conducted. It has exercised that authority many times. Most consequentially, Congress exercised it in 1842, when the Apportionment Act of 1842 required that all House members be elected from single-member districts — one representative per district, winner take all.

Before 1842, the rules were more varied. Many states elected their entire congressional delegation through statewide at-large elections, where all voters in the state voted for all available seats. Some states used multi-member districts. The move to mandatory single-member districts was, like so many decisions in American electoral history, partly driven by partisan calculation. Whigs, who dominated Congress, believed single-member districts would benefit them against Democrats in certain states. But the structural consequences were profound and enduring.

A single-member, winner-take-all district is the foundation upon which gerrymandering is built. You cannot gerrymander a statewide at-large election. You cannot gerrymander a system in which all voters in a state vote for all available seats and representation is distributed proportionally. The line-drawing that makes gerrymandering possible only exists because of the 1842 decision to require single-member districts. That decision was made by Congress. It can be unmade by Congress. The Constitution says so explicitly.

### 1.3 The Antebellum Period: Voting in a Slave Republic

Prior to the Civil War, voting in America was restricted to a dramatically narrow slice of the population. Women, enslaved people, free Black people in most states, Native Americans, and in many states propertyless white men were all excluded from the franchise. The United States in 1850 was not a democracy by any meaningful modern definition. It was an oligarchy of propertied white men, with democratic procedures operating within that oligarchy.

Free Black men had the right to vote in some Northern states, and in fact had voted in limited numbers during the colonial and early republic periods. But over the course of the early nineteenth century, as sectional tensions over slavery intensified and as the ideology of white supremacy hardened into a coherent political program, state after state stripped free Black men of the right to vote. By 1840, only Massachusetts, Vermont, New Hampshire, Maine, and Rhode Island allowed Black men to vote without significant restrictions.

In the South, the question did not arise at all. Slavery was total. The political system of the antebellum South existed to maintain slavery. Black Americans — enslaved and free — were simply outside the political community.

### 1.4 Dred Scott and the Constitutional Legitimization of Exclusion

The *Dred Scott v. Sandford* decision of 1857 deserves more attention than it typically receives in conversations about voting rights, because it represents the moment at which the federal judiciary explicitly endorsed and codified the exclusion of Black Americans from the political community as a constitutional matter.

Chief Justice Taney's majority opinion held that Black Americans — whether enslaved or free — were not citizens of the United States and therefore had no standing to sue in federal court. Taney went further: he wrote that the Framers had considered Black Americans "as a subordinate and inferior class of beings" who had "no rights or privileges but such as those who held the power and the Government might choose to grant them."

This was the constitutional framework in place at the start of the Civil War. The Fourteenth Amendment, ratified in 1868, explicitly overruled *Dred Scott* by declaring that all persons born in the United States were citizens. But *Dred Scott* illustrates a crucial pattern that persists to the present: the Supreme Court has, throughout American history, been willing to deploy the language of constitutional interpretation to legitimize the exclusion of Black Americans from political power, and has done so again in *Louisiana v. Callais*.

## Part 2: Reconstruction and Its Violent Destruction — The First Attempt at Multiracial Democracy

### 2.1 The Reconstruction Amendments: An Extraordinary Constitutional Moment

The three years from 1865 to 1870 represent perhaps the most remarkable period of constitutional transformation in American history. Three amendments were ratified in rapid succession, each designed to address a different dimension of the catastrophe of slavery and its aftermath.

The Thirteenth Amendment (1865) abolished slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for crime. It was ratified in December 1865, eight months after the end of the Civil War, with the votes of the former Confederate states required for ratification — states that had been readmitted to the Union under military Reconstruction.

The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) accomplished three things of constitutional significance. Section 1 declared that all persons born or naturalized in the United States were citizens, overruling *Dred Scott*. It required states to provide equal protection of the laws to all persons and prohibited deprivation of life, liberty, or property without due process of law. Section 2 modified the apportionment formula — the new rule was that if a state denied the right to vote to any male inhabitant over 21 (the obvious target was Black men), that state's representation would be reduced proportionally. This was meant to give states a financial incentive to extend the franchise. It was never enforced. Section 5 explicitly granted Congress the power to enforce the amendment by appropriate legislation — this is the constitutional foundation for the original Voting Rights Act and for the proposed reforms we will discuss.

The Fifteenth Amendment (1870) prohibited the federal government and the states from denying any citizen the right to vote on the basis of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. It was the most directly targeted of the three amendments at the specific problem of racial discrimination in voting.

Together, these three amendments represented a genuine constitutional revolution. They transformed the United States from a slave republic into — at least on paper — a multiracial democracy. And for a brief, extraordinary moment, the paper became reality.

### 2.2 Reconstruction Democracy: What It Actually Looked Like

During the period of Congressional Reconstruction (roughly 1867–1877), federal troops were stationed throughout the former Confederate states, protecting Black voters and enforcing the new constitutional order. The results were transformative. Black voter registration surged across the South. Black candidates won election to offices at every level of government.

Between 1870 and 1901, twenty-two Black men served in the United States Congress — two in the Senate (Hiram Revels and Blanche Bruce, both from Mississippi) and twenty in the House. Black men served as state governors, lieutenant governors, secretaries of state, treasurers, and in every imaginable state legislative position. In South Carolina, Black legislators held a majority in the state House of Representatives for much of Reconstruction.

This was genuinely revolutionary. For the first time in American history, Black Americans were full participants in democratic governance. And the response from white supremacists was ferocious.

### 2.3 Massive Resistance: The Ku Klux Klan and the Destruction of Reconstruction

The organized campaign to destroy Reconstruction democracy is one of the most important and least adequately taught episodes in American history. Understanding it is essential for understanding why the Voting Rights Act was necessary, why it worked, and why its destruction is so catastrophic.

The Ku Klux Klan was founded in Pulaski, Tennessee in 1865 by former Confederate officers. Within a few years, it had become a paramilitary organization operating throughout the South with the explicit mission of restoring white supremacy by terrorizing Black voters, their families, and white Republicans who supported Reconstruction.

The KKK's methods were systematic. Black men who attempted to vote were beaten, shot, hanged, burned alive, or had their homes and churches destroyed. White Republican officeholders faced assassination. The terror was not random. It was targeted, strategic, and effective. In county after county across the South, Black voter registration collapsed not because Black Americans stopped wanting to vote, but because the cost of attempting to vote — the realistic possibility of death — was too high.

Congress passed three Enforcement Acts between 1870 and 1871, including the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871, giving the federal government authority to prosecute Klan terrorism. The Grant administration used these laws aggressively in South Carolina, suspending habeas corpus and prosecuting hundreds of Klansmen. For a brief period, this worked. The first Klan was suppressed.

But the political will in the North for enforcing Reconstruction was ebbing. Northern Republicans were tired of the costs of military occupation. Northern Democrats were actively hostile to Black political rights. And the economic depression of 1873 shifted political attention away from civil rights. In the 1874 midterm elections, Democrats swept the House for the first time since before the Civil War.

### 2.4 The Compromise of 1877 and the Formal End of Reconstruction

The contested presidential election of 1876 between Republican Rutherford B. Hayes and Democrat Samuel Tilden is often cited as the formal end of Reconstruction. The election results in three Southern states — Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina — were disputed, with both parties claiming victory. The dispute was resolved by the Compromise of 1877, in which Hayes received the presidency in exchange for the withdrawal of federal troops from the South.

With federal troops gone, the brief experiment in multiracial democracy ended almost immediately. The white supremacist "Redeemer" governments that took power across the South set about systematically dismantling the political power that Black Southerners had built during Reconstruction. They did so through a combination of legal mechanisms, economic coercion, and violence.

The legal mechanisms included poll taxes (requiring payment of a tax to vote, which Black sharecroppers and tenant farmers could rarely afford), literacy tests (administered with blatant discrimination — white applicants were asked simple questions while Black applicants were asked to recite and interpret obscure passages of the state constitution), grandfather clauses (exempting from voting restrictions those whose grandfather had voted — which excluded almost all Black men but exempted almost all white men), and white-only Democratic primaries (since the South was essentially a one-party state, exclusion from the Democratic primary meant exclusion from effective political participation).

Black voter registration in Mississippi, which had exceeded 190,000 during Reconstruction, fell to approximately 1,300 by 1900. In Louisiana, which had over 130,000 registered Black voters in 1896, that number fell to fewer than 1,400 by 1904. This was not gradual drift or demographic change. This was systematic, organized, legally-backed disfranchisement.

### 2.5 Plessy v. Ferguson and the Constitutional Legitimization of Apartheid

The Supreme Court's 1896 decision in *Plessy v. Ferguson* is best known for the "separate but equal" doctrine it established for public accommodations. But its significance for voting rights is equally profound, because it represented the federal judiciary's explicit ratification of the apartheid regime that Southern states were constructing.

Justice Henry Billings Brown, writing for the 7–1 majority, held that the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee of equal protection did not prohibit laws requiring racial segregation, provided the facilities provided were equal. Justice John Marshall Harlan's famous lone dissent declared that "our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens." Harlan was right then. The tragedy is that his framing — color-blindness as the constitutional ideal — would be weaponized in *Louisiana v. Callais* 130 years later to justify the destruction of race-conscious remedies for racial injustice.

The *Plessy* decision was the legal foundation for Jim Crow. It established that the federal courts would not intervene to protect Black civil rights. It told Southern states that they could construct their apartheid systems — separate schools, separate trains, separate water fountains — without judicial interference. And it confirmed that the Fourteenth Amendment, which had been passed to ensure equal citizenship, had been effectively nullified by judicial interpretation.

### 2.6 The Nadir: Voting Rights in the Early Twentieth Century

The period from approximately 1890 to 1920 is sometimes called the "nadir of American race relations" — a term coined by historian Rayford Logan to describe the period when anti-Black violence, disenfranchisement, and legal oppression reached their absolute peak. By this point, Black voter registration across the Deep South had effectively collapsed. The constitutional amendments that had been passed to ensure Black political participation were dead letters in every meaningful practical sense.

It is worth pausing to absorb the scale of what happened. The Fifteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, ratified in 1870, explicitly prohibited the denial of voting rights on the basis of race. That prohibition sat in the text of the supreme law of the land for nearly one hundred years while Black Americans were systematically denied the right to vote across eleven states. The federal government did essentially nothing. The federal courts did essentially nothing. The Supreme Court devised doctrine after doctrine — state action requirements, intent requirements, narrow standing rules — to avoid confronting the plain meaning of the constitutional text.

This is not ancient history. Voters who cast their first ballots under Jim Crow were still living when the Voting Rights Act was signed in 1965. The parents of people alive today in 2026 were adults during this period. The material consequences — in wealth, education, political representation, housing, health — continue to compound across generations.

## Part 3: The Civil Rights Movement and the Voting Rights Act of 1965

### 3.1 The Long Movement: From Reconstruction to the 1960s

The civil rights struggle did not begin in the 1950s. It began the moment Reconstruction ended and continued, in various forms, throughout the long years of Jim Crow. What changed in the 1950s and 1960s was not that Black Americans began fighting for their rights — they had never stopped — but that the combination of postwar social change, television, Cold War politics (it was embarrassing to lecture the world about democracy while treating Black Americans as subhumans), and a new generation of brilliantly organized activist leadership produced a political moment that could not be contained.

The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP), founded in 1909, had been litigating against Jim Crow for decades. Thurgood Marshall, the NAACP's chief counsel, had won dozens of cases challenging segregation before *Brown v. Board of Education* in 1954 finally overturned *Plessy* in the context of public education. But litigation, as important as it was, could not produce the mass democratic mobilization that was needed to actually change the political landscape.

That mobilization was organized by the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC), founded in 1957 by Martin Luther King Jr. and other Black ministers; the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), organized in 1960; and a constellation of local organizations and individual heroes whose names history has not always preserved. The Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955–1956), the lunch counter sit-ins (1960), the Freedom Rides (1961), the Birmingham Campaign (1963), and the March on Washington (1963) built toward a political crisis that demanded congressional action.

### 3.2 The Selma Campaign and Bloody Sunday

Of all the episodes in the civil rights movement, the campaign in Selma, Alabama in 1965 was perhaps the most directly consequential for the legislation that followed.

Selma was, in many ways, a carefully chosen target. Dallas County, Alabama, where Selma was the county seat, had a population that was approximately 57% Black. But in 1965, only 335 Black residents were registered to vote, compared to 9,800 white residents. The Selma-to-Montgomery marches were organized to demand voting rights, and the march of March 7, 1965 — which became known as Bloody Sunday — was intended to reach the state capital in Montgomery, where the marchers planned to present their demands to Governor George Wallace.

Six hundred marchers, led by SNCC chairman John Lewis and SCLC's Hosea Williams, set out from Brown Chapel AME Church and walked to the Edmund Pettus Bridge at the edge of Selma. As they crossed the bridge and saw the Alabama State Troopers and posse members waiting for them, the troopers advanced without warning. They beat the marchers with clubs, used tear gas, and rode horses into the crowd. John Lewis was beaten with a club, fracturing his skull. Amelia Boynton Robinson was beaten unconscious and left for dead in the road. Dozens of others were injured.

Cameras recorded everything. The footage was broadcast on national television that evening, interrupting a broadcast of "Judgment at Nuremberg." The juxtaposition was not lost on American viewers. Millions of white Americans who had been able to maintain comfortable distance from the reality of Jim Crow now could not. The political pressure became irresistible.

President Lyndon Johnson addressed a joint session of Congress on March 15, 1965 — the largest television audience for any political speech in American history to that point. He concluded with the words of the civil rights movement itself: "And we shall overcome." The Voting Rights Act passed the Senate on May 26, 1965 by a vote of 77–19, and the House on July 9, 1965 by a vote of 333–85. Johnson signed it on August 6, 1965.

### 3.3 The Architecture of the Voting Rights Act

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 was a remarkably sophisticated piece of legislation. It had multiple components, each designed to address different dimensions of the voting rights problem.

Section 2, the most broadly applicable provision, stated that no voting qualification, practice, or procedure could be imposed by any state or political subdivision in a manner that denied or abridged the right to vote on account of race or color. This was a broad, nationwide prohibition.

But Section 2's original formulation required plaintiffs to prove discriminatory *intent* — which, as we will discuss at length, was an enormous practical burden. The transformation of Section 2 in 1982 to allow *effects*-based challenges was the single most important amendment to the VRA.

Section 4 established a coverage formula: states and counties with a history of voting discrimination — defined by both the use of discriminatory tests and devices and by low voter registration or turnout rates — were "covered jurisdictions." As originally enacted, the covered jurisdictions included Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina, Virginia, and parts of North Carolina — essentially the Deep South.

Section 5, the "preclearance" provision, was the heart of the VRA's enforcement mechanism. It required covered jurisdictions to obtain federal approval — "preclearance" — before implementing any change in voting laws, procedures, or practices. This reversed the normal burden: instead of requiring voters to sue after a discriminatory change had already taken effect (a process that could take years), Section 5 required states to affirmatively demonstrate that changes were not discriminatory before implementing them. The Department of Justice or the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia could deny preclearance.

This was revolutionary. It acknowledged something that decades of litigation had made clear: that discriminating states would respond to judicial defeats by simply devising new discriminatory mechanisms. Preclearance was designed to stay ahead of that.

Section 10 directed the Attorney General to examine the imposition of poll taxes in state and local elections, and the Twenty-Fourth Amendment, ratified in 1964, had already eliminated poll taxes in federal elections. Section 203, added in 1975, extended VRA protections to language minority groups, requiring election materials in other languages in jurisdictions with significant non-English-speaking populations.

### 3.4 The Results: What the VRA Actually Did

The Voting Rights Act worked. That is perhaps the most important thing to understand about it, because it explains why its destruction is so devastating. This was not a law that had failed to achieve its purposes. It was a law that had achieved its purposes with remarkable effectiveness — and that is precisely why certain people worked so hard to destroy it.

In 1965, Black voter registration in Mississippi was approximately 6.7%. By 1967, it had risen to 59.8%. In Alabama, it went from 19.3% to 51.6% in the same two-year period. Across the covered jurisdictions, the transformation was similarly dramatic. The combination of prohibiting discriminatory tests and devices, deploying federal examiners to register voters, and providing federal observers to monitor elections produced an immediate and enormous expansion of the Black electorate.

The political consequences followed. Black elected officials, essentially absent from the South in 1965, were elected in increasing numbers in the years that followed. Maynard Jackson became the first Black mayor of Atlanta in 1973. Andrew Young won a congressional seat from Georgia in 1972. By the mid-1980s, the number of Black elected officials in the United States had grown from fewer than 500 in 1965 to more than 6,000.

### 3.5 The Problem That Section 2 Did Not Initially Address: Vote Dilution

The initial VRA addressed the most direct problem — the outright denial of the right to vote through registration obstacles and intimidation. But it did not fully address a more subtle problem: even when Black Americans could vote freely, they could still be denied effective political representation through the manipulation of district lines.

Imagine a county in Alabama where Black voters make up 40% of the population, spread across several towns. If the county is divided into five districts, and the district lines are drawn to ensure that Black voters are a minority in each district, Black voters may be able to cast ballots freely — no poll taxes, no literacy tests, no violence — and still never elect a single candidate of their choice. Their votes are simply always on the losing side. This is "vote dilution."

The Supreme Court, in early cases like *Allen v. State Board of Elections* (1969), recognized that the VRA applied to some forms of vote dilution. But the scope of this protection was uncertain, and in 1980, the Supreme Court dramatically curtailed it.

### 3.6 City of Mobile v. Bolden and the Intent Requirement

*City of Mobile v. Bolden* (1980) is one of the most consequential and least widely known Supreme Court decisions of the twentieth century. The case involved Mobile, Alabama, which had a large-scale, citywide elections system for its city commission rather than district-based elections. Under this at-large system, the three-member commission had never included a Black member, even though Black residents made up approximately 35% of Mobile's population.

The plaintiffs argued that this at-large system diluted Black voting power in violation of the Fourteenth Amendment and Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act. The Supreme Court, in a plurality opinion by Justice Potter Stewart, held that to succeed on either the constitutional or statutory claim, plaintiffs had to prove that the at-large system was created or maintained with a *discriminatory purpose* — with the intent to discriminate against Black voters.

This was an extraordinarily high bar. Proving discriminatory intent requires evidence of subjective motivation — essentially, demonstrating that the people who drew the district lines or created the electoral system did so specifically to harm Black voters. States and localities that wanted to discriminate had learned, after a century of civil rights litigation, to be careful not to put their racist intentions in writing. The intent standard effectively gave discriminators a blueprint for how to discriminate with legal impunity: simply don't announce your intentions.

### 3.7 Congress Strikes Back: The 1982 Amendment and John Roberts' First Defeat

The *Bolden* decision produced immediate and fierce congressional reaction. Civil rights advocates, voting rights experts, and the entire civil rights community lobbied Congress to amend Section 2 to eliminate the intent requirement and restore a results-based test: if the effect of an electoral system was to deny minority voters equal opportunity to participate in the political process, it violated the VRA.

And here, for the first time, we encounter John Roberts.

It is 1981 or 1982. Roberts is a young attorney in the Reagan White House Office of Counsel. He is brilliant, ambitious, and deeply committed to a conservative constitutional vision that is skeptical of expansive civil rights protections. And he is assigned to work on the Reagan administration's position on the proposed amendment to Section 2.

Roberts produced a series of memoranda arguing strenuously against a results-based test for Section 2. He argued that such a test would "establish essentially a racial quota system" for elected officials. He contended that it would require "a fixed percentage of minority elected officials" regardless of actual voter preferences. He pushed back against the broad remedial approach that Congress was considering, arguing for a narrower, more restrictive interpretation.

Roberts lost. Congress, in an overwhelming bipartisan vote, passed the 1982 amendment to Section 2. The amendment added language explicitly rejecting the intent requirement: the right to vote was abridged if the "totality of circumstances" showed that the "political processes leading to nomination or election in the State or political subdivision are not equally open to participation" by minority voters. The Senate Report accompanying the amendment made clear that Congress intended to overrule *Bolden* and establish a results-based test.

The new Section 2 made the VRA's coverage of vote dilution claims both broader and more workable. It allowed plaintiffs to prove discrimination by examining electoral outcomes, demographic patterns, historical context, and the totality of circumstances — without having to prove what was in the minds of the people who drew the maps.

Roberts did not forget this defeat. Forty years later, from the other side of the bench, he would be in a position to accomplish what he could not accomplish in the Reagan White House.

## Part 4: The Gingles Framework and the Golden Age of Minority Representation

### 4.1 Thornburg v. Gingles: The Foundational Case

In 1986, the Supreme Court issued its decision in *Thornburg v. Gingles*, interpreting the newly amended Section 2. The case involved challenges to North Carolina's multi-member state legislative districts, which plaintiffs argued diluted Black voting power.

Justice William Brennan, writing for the Court, established a framework for analyzing Section 2 vote dilution claims in the redistricting context. The framework required plaintiffs to establish three threshold preconditions — now universally known as the "Gingles preconditions":

First, the minority group must be sufficiently large and geographically compact to constitute a majority in a single-member district. This was the "compactness" precondition.

Second, the minority group must be politically cohesive — that is, its members must tend to vote as a bloc for certain candidates.

Third, the majority must vote sufficiently as a bloc to enable it — in the absence of special circumstances — usually to defeat the minority's preferred candidate. This was the "racially polarized voting" precondition.

If these three preconditions were met, courts then examined the "totality of circumstances" — including a list of factors drawn from the Senate Report accompanying the 1982 amendment — to determine whether the challenged practice denied minority voters an equal opportunity to participate in the political process.

The *Gingles* framework was workable, widely understood, and produced substantial results. Over the following three decades, hundreds of cases applying the *Gingles* test resulted in the creation of majority-minority districts across the country — districts specifically designed to give Black, Latino, Asian-American, and Native American voters the opportunity to elect representatives of their choice.

### 4.2 The Majority-Minority District System: How It Worked

The practical result of the *Gingles* framework was the creation of what became known as "majority-minority districts" — congressional and state legislative districts in which minority voters constituted a majority of the population or the voting-age population or the registered voters, depending on the jurisdiction and the nature of the challenge.

In Louisiana, for example, the demographics of the state — approximately 32% Black — combined with racially polarized voting (the well-documented pattern in which white and Black voters tended to prefer different candidates) meant that under a *Gingles* analysis, the state had an obligation to draw its congressional districts in a way that gave Black voters a realistic opportunity to elect candidates of their choice. Louisiana has six congressional seats. The *Gingles* framework and subsequent litigation produced a system in which Black voters had an opportunity district in roughly one of those six seats — proportional to their share of the population.

Similarly, across the South, across the Southwest in states with large Latino populations, and in other states with significant minority communities, the *Gingles* framework produced opportunity districts that provided minority communities with meaningful representation.

The numbers are stark. In 1965, when the VRA was signed, fewer than 500 Black Americans held elected office in the United States. By 2021, that number had grown to more than 10,000. The Congressional Black Caucus grew from 9 members in 1971 to 57 members in the 119th Congress. The Congressional Hispanic Caucus has similarly grown. This is not the product of demographic change alone — it is the product of an enforceable legal framework that required fair representation.

### 4.3 The Tension: Racial vs. Partisan Considerations

The *Gingles* framework created a productive but legally complex tension. Creating majority-minority districts required drawing district lines with explicit attention to race — specifically, ensuring that minority voters constituted a majority or near-majority within the district. But the Supreme Court had also held, in a series of cases beginning with *Shaw v. Reno* (1993), that drawing districts with race as the "predominant factor" could itself violate the Equal Protection Clause.

This created what legal scholars called the "racial gerrymandering paradox": the VRA might require drawing a majority-minority district, but if the legislature drew the district with excessive attention to race, that very compliance might be challenged as an unconstitutional racial gerrymander. States were caught between the obligation to comply with Section 2 and the constitutional prohibition on racial gerrymandering — between the mandate to consider race and the prohibition on considering it too much.

The courts developed a framework for navigating this tension: if a state was drawing a district to comply with the VRA, that provided a compelling interest justifying the consideration of race. The question was whether the state's use of race was narrowly tailored to that compelling interest. If the VRA actually required the district, then the use of race was justified. If the VRA did not actually require the district — if Section 2 would not have required it under the *Gingles* analysis — then the use of race could not be justified on VRA-compliance grounds.

This framework worked, imperfectly, for decades. The *Callais* decision exploits this tension to destroy it entirely, as we will discuss at length.

## Part 5: The Roberts Court Begins Its Work — Shelby County and Brnovich

### 5.1 John Roberts Ascends: A Chief Justice With a Mission

When John Roberts was confirmed as Chief Justice of the United States in September 2005, the legal community understood that he brought a consistent and deeply held set of views about civil rights law, voting rights in particular, and the proper role of the judiciary in enforcing the Reconstruction Amendments.

Roberts had continued to make his views known on these questions throughout his legal career. As a lawyer in private practice and in the George W. Bush administration, he had consistently taken positions skeptical of expansive VRA enforcement. His judicial opinions as a D.C. Circuit judge were similarly cautious. His confirmation hearings revealed a man who had thought deeply about these questions and had arrived at conclusions that, while expressed in the careful language of judicial restraint, were clear in their direction.

Roberts was philosophically committed to a particular vision of constitutional equality — one in which the Constitution is, in Justice Harlan's phrase from *Plessy*, "color-blind." On this view, any government action that takes account of race — including government action designed to remedy historical racial discrimination — is constitutionally suspect, because the Equal Protection Clause prohibits the government from treating people differently based on their race. The fact that the people being helped by a race-conscious remedy are the same people who suffered from centuries of race-conscious oppression is, on this view, constitutionally irrelevant.

This vision is intellectually coherent, even if many legal scholars find it unconvincing. The problem is that it leads to a specific legal outcome: race-conscious remedies for historical discrimination, including majority-minority districts designed to provide minority communities with representation they were denied for a century, are presumptively unconstitutional. And making them unconstitutional was precisely what Roberts set out to do.

### 5.2 Shelby County v. Holder (2013): Killing the Heart of the VRA

*Shelby County v. Holder* was decided on June 25, 2013, by a 5–4 vote. Roberts wrote the majority opinion, joined by Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito. The case presented a direct challenge to the constitutionality of Section 4(b) of the VRA — the coverage formula that determined which states and counties were required to obtain preclearance before changing their voting laws.

Roberts' argument was elegantly simple, even if the legal reasoning was intensely criticized. He acknowledged that in 1965, the formula for identifying covered jurisdictions had been justified by the conditions of that era — widespread racial discrimination in voting. He acknowledged that Congress had reauthorized the VRA multiple times, most recently in 2006 with near-unanimous votes. But he argued that conditions had changed dramatically since 1965, and that using a coverage formula based on 40-year-old data was unconstitutional because it treated states unequally — some states had to get permission before changing their voting laws while others did not.

The legal analysis required interpreting the Constitution's guarantee of "equal sovereignty" among the states — a doctrine Roberts elevated to constitutional status, drawing it from a dissent by Justice Kennedy in an earlier case. Roberts held that Congress could only justify the unequal treatment of states if it used a formula based on current conditions rather than historical data.

Roberts then declined to actually strike down Section 5 preclearance itself — he held only that the coverage formula in Section 4(b) was unconstitutional, so that Section 5 had no operative effect. He wrote, with characteristic reassurance, that there was always Section 2 to protect minority voters.

Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg's dissent is a masterpiece of scathing judicial rebuttal. She wrote: "Throwing out preclearance when it has worked and is continuing to work to stop discriminatory changes is like throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because you are not getting wet." She detailed the extensive evidence of continued voting discrimination that Congress had compiled before the 2006 reauthorization — thousands of pages of testimony, hundreds of pages of findings, documented examples of discriminatory changes that preclearance had stopped. She made clear that Congress had done exactly what the Court's precedents required it to do — build an extensive legislative record justifying the continuation of the preclearance regime.

The Court struck it down anyway.

### 5.3 The Immediate Aftermath: What Happened the Day After Shelby County

The speed with which states acted after *Shelby County* is perhaps the most striking evidence that preclearance had been working exactly as Congress intended — that without it, discriminatory changes would flood the electoral system.

Within hours of the decision, Texas Attorney General Greg Abbott announced that a strict voter ID law that had been blocked by preclearance would take effect immediately. Within days, North Carolina began moving a sweeping voter suppression bill that included strict voter ID requirements, elimination of same-day registration, curtailment of early voting, and the elimination of a program that made it easier for teenagers to pre-register to vote.

A 2022 study by political scientists found that counties that had previously been subject to preclearance increased their voter roll purge rates, reduced polling place availability, and imposed new voting restrictions at significantly higher rates than counties that had never been covered by preclearance. The effects fell disproportionately on Black and Latino voters.

The promise that Section 2 would fill the gap left by the elimination of effective Section 5 preclearance was already questionable in 2013. Section 2 requires plaintiffs to file a lawsuit after a discriminatory change has been implemented, wait years for a decision, and potentially appeal through multiple rounds of courts — all while the discriminatory law is in effect. Section 5 had reversed this burden, requiring states to prove compliance before changes took effect. The two provisions were not equivalent. One protected minority voters. The other subjected them to the costs of years of litigation.

### 5.4 Brnovich v. Democratic National Committee (2021): Alito Comes for Section 2

If *Shelby County* was the first major blow, *Brnovich v. Democratic National Committee* (2021) was the second. The case involved two Arizona voting restrictions: a rule requiring that ballots cast in the wrong precinct be entirely discarded, and a law prohibiting "ballot harvesting" (the collection of mail-in ballots by political organizations for delivery to election offices, used particularly in Native American communities with limited mail service).

The lower courts had found that these restrictions disproportionately burdened minority voters — particularly Native American voters for whom ballot harvesting was essential given the significant distances many lived from post offices and election offices. The question before the Supreme Court was how Section 2 applied to voting *restrictions* (as opposed to redistricting), and what a plaintiff had to prove to win a Section 2 challenge to such restrictions.

Justice Alito, writing for the 6–3 majority, rejected the lower courts' analysis and announced a new test for Section 2 challenges to voting restrictions. His analysis focused on a set of factors — the size of the burden imposed, the extent to which the burden fell on minority voters, whether the requirement was a commonplace feature of election administration — that effectively made it nearly impossible to win a Section 2 challenge to facially neutral voting restrictions.

Alito's opinion was widely criticized by voting rights experts as a wholesale rewriting of Section 2 that bore little relationship to the text of the statute or Congress's intent. As Justice Kagan's dissent noted, Alito was effectively imposing a new test that Congress had never enacted, substituting his policy judgments for those of the legislature.

The practical result was equally clear: as legal scholar Richard Hasen noted in the immediate aftermath of *Brnovich*, since that decision there has not been a single successful Section 2 challenge to a voting restriction. Zero. The section of the law that was supposed to prohibit discriminatory voting practices has, in application to voting restrictions, simply stopped working.

### 5.5 Allen v. Milligan (2023): A Brief Reprieve

In June 2023, the Supreme Court handed down *Allen v. Milligan*, a 5–4 decision that upheld a lower court ruling finding that Alabama's congressional map violated Section 2 by packing Black voters into a single district while cracking the remaining 27% of Black Alabamians across six other districts.

The majority opinion, written by Chief Justice Roberts and joined by the three liberal justices plus Justice Kavanaugh, reaffirmed the *Gingles* test and held that Alabama had to draw a second majority-Black congressional district. The decision was celebrated by voting rights advocates as evidence that Section 2 still had teeth for redistricting cases.

Alabama's response was to draw a new map that still didn't create a second majority-Black district — a defiance so brazen that the district court found it contemptuous and appointed a special master to draw remedial maps. Eventually, a map with a second majority-Black-majority or near-majority district was implemented.

But *Allen v. Milligan* contained the seeds of its own undoing. Justice Kavanaugh's concurrence, joined by Justice Roberts, hinted strongly that the conservative majority was not fully committed to the *Gingles* framework — that the tension between Section 2 and the Equal Protection Clause would need to be resolved, and that resolution might not favor Section 2. The Louisiana case, already working its way through the courts, would provide the vehicle.

## Part 6: Louisiana v. Callais — The Final Blow

### 6.1 The Louisiana Map: A Case Study in the VRA's Operation

Louisiana's congressional redistricting after the 2020 Census illustrates perfectly both how the VRA was supposed to work and how its destruction will work in practice.

Louisiana has six congressional districts. Black Americans make up approximately 32% of the state's population. Under a simple proportionality analysis, Black voters might expect to have some representation opportunity in approximately two of the six seats. Under the VRA's *Gingles* framework, Black voters could argue that the conditions for a Section 2 violation existed if the state drew maps that did not give them the opportunity to elect candidates of their choice in proportion to their population.

After the 2020 Census, Louisiana drew a congressional map that included one majority-Black district — District 2, the New Orleans–area district represented by Democrat Troy Carter. Civil rights groups immediately challenged the map, arguing that the *Gingles* preconditions were satisfied and that Louisiana had an obligation to draw a second majority-Black district given that Black voters constituted 32% of the population.

In June 2022, U.S. District Judge Shelly Dick agreed, holding that the Louisiana map likely violated Section 2, because Black Alabamians... wait — Louisianians were sufficiently numerous, geographically compact, politically cohesive, and faced a racially polarized white majority that consistently voted against Black-preferred candidates. She ordered the state to draw a new map.

Louisiana appealed. The Supreme Court, in February 2022, stayed Judge Dick's order while the appeal proceeded — allowing the gerrymandered map to remain in effect for the 2022 elections. This meant that the 2022 elections were conducted under a map that a federal court had found was likely illegal. This pattern — gerrymandered maps remaining in effect during years of litigation — is itself a feature of the single-member district system, and it is one that a statewide multi-member district would eliminate.

In June 2023, after *Allen v. Milligan*, the Supreme Court vacated the stay and sent the case back to the lower court. Louisiana was ordered to draw a new map with a second majority-Black district. The legislature drew SB8 — a new map that created a second majority-Black district (District 6) stretching roughly 250 miles from Shreveport through Alexandria and Lafayette to Baton Rouge.

Almost immediately, a group of non-Black plaintiffs — voting rights plaintiff Callais and others — challenged SB8 as an unconstitutional racial gerrymander, arguing that the new district had been drawn with race as the predominant factor. A three-judge federal court agreed, striking down the new map.

### 6.2 The Trap: Using the 14th Amendment to Destroy the 15th

This is the trap. A state cannot draw majority-minority districts without attending carefully to race — that is the entire point of a majority-minority district. But if it attends too carefully to race, it may be accused of making race the "predominant factor," which (under prior doctrine) required strict scrutiny. And strict scrutiny would only be satisfied if the state could demonstrate both a compelling interest in using race and that the use of race was narrowly tailored.

The compelling interest was, for decades, compliance with the Voting Rights Act. If Section 2 required a majority-minority district, then the state had a compelling interest in drawing one. The *Callais* decision exploits this structure by making it impossible to establish that Section 2 actually required the district in the first place.

### 6.3 What Alito's Opinion Actually Does

The *Callais* majority opinion, written by Justice Alito and joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett, is a masterwork of what legal scholars call "stealth overruling" — changing the law dramatically while claiming to be doing no such thing.

Alito does not explicitly say he is overruling *Gingles*. He says he is "clarifying" and "applying" *Gingles*. But what he does is transform the *Gingles* test into something so demanding that plaintiffs will rarely, if ever, be able to satisfy it.

The first *Gingles* precondition asks whether minority voters are sufficiently large and geographically compact to constitute a majority in a single-member district. In *Callais*, Alito requires that the illustrative map presented by Section 2 plaintiffs must meet all of the state's "nonracial redistricting goals" — including, critically, the state's political goals. What this means in practice is that if a state says it wants to protect incumbents, plaintiffs' illustrative maps must protect incumbents too. If the state says it wants compact districts, the illustrative map must be similarly compact. And if the state says it wants to draw maps that favor Republicans — since partisan gerrymandering is legal under *Rucho* — then plaintiffs' maps cannot establish the first *Gingles* precondition by showing a district that would displace a Republican incumbent.

As Justice Kagan's dissent devastatingly explains, this is not a clarification. This is the imposition of a new requirement that Congress never enacted and that *Gingles* never contained. It means that whenever a state can point to any nonracial goal — including explicitly partisan goals — that its gerrymandered maps serve, Section 2 plaintiffs cannot establish the first *Gingles* precondition. And since *Rucho* held that partisan gerrymandering is entirely legal and unreviewable by federal courts, states now have an effectively unlimited license to gerrymander for partisan purposes, and can then use those partisan goals as a complete defense against Section 2 racial gerrymandering claims.

The second and third *Gingles* preconditions ask about racial cohesion and racial bloc voting — whether minority voters vote cohesively and whether the majority votes as a bloc against minority-preferred candidates. In *Callais*, Alito requires that evidence of racial cohesion and racial bloc voting must control for partisan preferences. The problem is that in the contemporary United States, race and party are highly correlated: Black voters vote overwhelmingly Democratic, and white voters in the Deep South vote overwhelmingly Republican. It is often statistically impossible to cleanly separate racial and partisan preferences in racially polarized voting analysis, because the two variables are so closely related. By requiring that analysis control for party while making partisan gerrymandering unreviewable, Alito has created a statistical impossibility that effectively neutralizes the second and third *Gingles* preconditions.

Finally, on the "totality of circumstances," Alito holds that historical evidence of racial discrimination — the documented history of Jim Crow, of systematic disenfranchisement, of racially polarized voting that was itself a product of racial oppression — is insufficient. Instead, plaintiffs must show "an objective likelihood of intentional discrimination" in current conditions. This is nothing more than the restoration of the intent standard that Congress explicitly rejected in the 1982 amendment to Section 2.

Let that sink in. Congress, in 1982, explicitly amended Section 2 to eliminate the intent requirement that the Supreme Court had imposed in *City of Mobile v. Bolden*. Congress made crystal clear that it intended a results-based test, not an intent-based test. The Senate Report accompanying the amendment specifically addressed this point. And now, 44 years later, the Roberts Court has effectively restored the intent requirement — not explicitly, not by saying "we are reestablishing an intent requirement," but through a series of doctrinal adjustments that collectively make it essentially impossible to win a Section 2 redistricting case without proof of intentional discrimination.

### 6.4 The Partisan Gerrymandering Escape Hatch

Perhaps the most insidious element of *Callais* is the interaction with *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019). In *Rucho*, the Roberts Court held that federal courts could not adjudicate claims of partisan gerrymandering — that such claims presented a "political question" unreviewable by federal courts. Even if a map was drawn with the explicit purpose of guaranteeing one party's dominance, even if statistical evidence demonstrated with mathematical certainty that the map was designed to waste opposition votes, federal courts could not remedy it.

*Callais* now holds that if a state claims it drew its maps for partisan reasons, that assertion can defeat a Section 2 racial vote dilution claim. So the sequence works like this: a state draws a map that cracks and packs Black communities in ways that would historically have constituted a Section 2 violation. When challenged, the state says: "We weren't trying to dilute Black voting power. We were trying to maximize Republican representation. It just happens that those two things are correlated in our state." Federal courts cannot review partisan gerrymandering claims under *Rucho*. And now, under *Callais*, the claim of partisan motivation is a complete or nearly complete defense to a Section 2 racial discrimination claim.

As Justice Kagan's dissent states: "The new *Callais* requirements will effectively insulate any practice, including any districting scheme, said by a State to have any race-neutral justification. That justification can sound in traditional redistricting criteria, or else can sound in politics and partisanship. As to the latter, the State need do nothing more than announce a partisan gerrymander."

A state can now announce that it is engaged in racial discrimination — functionally, if not formally — and be legally protected from challenge, because it has characterized its racial discrimination as partisan discrimination instead.

### 6.5 The Political Context of the Decision

It would be willfully naive to discuss *Louisiana v. Callais* without acknowledging its political context. The decision was handed down on April 29, 2026 — less than six months before the 2026 midterm elections. The Republican Party has, for at least the past decade, been systematically investing in redistricting as a key electoral strategy. The REDMAP initiative, described in Part 7 of this article, was explicitly designed to control state legislatures so as to control post-Census redistricting. The results in 2011–2012 and again after the 2020 Census gave Republican legislatures across the country the ability to draw maps that locked in structural Republican majorities even in states where Republicans won only a minority of votes.

The *Callais* decision will accelerate this process. States like Florida, Tennessee, and others are already considering aggressive remapping in the wake of the decision. Tennessee Republican Senator Marsha Blackburn, within hours of the ruling, posted online urging the state legislature to reconvene and redistrict to create "another Republican seat in Memphis" — one of the most explicit admissions that the ruling will be used to eliminate Black-majority districts under cover of partisan motivation.

The election law community has estimated that up to a quarter or more of the Congressional Black Caucus and roughly a tenth of the Congressional Hispanic Caucus could face map changes that would previously have triggered Section 2 scrutiny. The impact on state legislatures and local government bodies could be even more severe. A generation of hard-fought minority representation — representation that was produced by the Voting Rights Act and the *Gingles* framework — is now directly at risk.

### 6.6 Justice Kagan's Dissent: A Future Roadmap

Justice Kagan's dissent, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Jackson, is one of the most important documents in the history of the Supreme Court's voting rights jurisprudence. It deserves careful reading.

Kagan begins with history: the long struggle for Black voting rights, the passage of the VRA, Congress's deliberate choice to create a results-based test in 1982, and the decades of case law that had faithfully applied *Gingles*. She then systematically demolishes each element of the majority's reasoning.

On the first *Gingles* precondition: Kagan demonstrates that the majority's requirement that illustrative maps satisfy the state's political goals is a new invention, inconsistent with *Gingles*' text and the statute's history, that will effectively immunize partisan gerrymandering from Section 2 scrutiny whenever race and party are correlated — which is to say, in the overwhelming majority of cases in the contemporary South.

On racial cohesion and bloc voting: Kagan shows that requiring plaintiffs to control for partisan preferences in jurisdictions where race and party are highly correlated effectively makes the second and third *Gingles* preconditions nearly impossible to satisfy.

On the totality of circumstances: Kagan demonstrates that requiring proof of intentional discrimination directly contradicts Congress's 1982 amendment and the plain text of Section 2.

Her conclusion is unsparing: "The majority's opinion empties Section 2 of its force. In holding that plaintiffs have failed to satisfy the *Gingles* preconditions (while calling what it has done a 'clarification'), it puts Section 2 beyond reach for most redistricting plaintiffs. And in doing so, it converts the most important provision of the most important civil rights statute in American history into a dead letter."

Kagan also makes explicit the pattern of the Roberts Court: *Shelby County* gutted Section 5, with the promise that Section 2 would fill the gap. *Brnovich* gutted Section 2 as applied to voting restrictions. *Callais* guts Section 2 as applied to redistricting. There is no backstop left. There is no next promise of protection. The VRA, which took centuries of struggle and the blood of civil rights martyrs to pass, has been systematically dismantled by a Court majority that, as Kagan writes, "has shown itself to be the most hostile to voting rights in at least a century."

## Part 7: The Architecture of Gerrymandering — How the System is Rigged

### 7.1 REDMAP and the Investment in Redistricting

To understand why *Callais* matters so much in immediate political terms, you need to understand the Republican redistricting investment that has transformed American politics over the past fifteen years.

The Redistricting Majority Project, known as REDMAP, was launched by the Republican State Leadership Committee in 2010. The explicit strategy was elegantly simple: invest in state legislative races in 2010, a Census year, so that Republicans would control the state legislatures that would draw the new congressional district maps after the 2010 Census. For a relatively modest investment — approximately $30 million — Republicans could potentially lock in structural congressional majorities for the entire decade of the 2010s.

The strategy worked with devastating effectiveness. Republicans gained more than 700 state legislative seats in the 2010 elections, flipping control of chambers in critical states including Ohio, Michigan, Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, and North Carolina. In all of these states, Republicans then drew congressional maps that gave them structural advantages in the 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018, 2020, and 2022 elections.

The numbers tell the story. In Wisconsin in 2012, Democratic candidates for the state legislature received 51.4% of the votes — a statewide majority — but won only 39 of 99 seats. In North Carolina, Republicans won 49% of congressional votes but 9 of 13 seats. In Pennsylvania, Republicans won 49% of congressional votes but 13 of 18 seats. These disparities were not accidents. They were the designed outcome of carefully engineered district maps.

The political science term for this disparity is the "efficiency gap" — the difference between the two parties in the rates at which their votes are "wasted" (either cast for losing candidates or cast in excess of what was needed to win). In Wisconsin after the 2011 redistricting, the efficiency gap favored Republicans by approximately 13 percentage points — one of the largest in American history.

### 7.2 Pack and Crack: The Technical Mechanics

Gerrymandering in the modern era is a precision technology, not a rough craft. Contemporary redistricting is conducted using sophisticated GIS software, block-by-block demographic data from the Census Bureau, granular voter registration data, and predictive models that can forecast with high confidence how any given map configuration will translate into election outcomes — not just in the next cycle but in the next three or four cycles.

The two core techniques remain packing and cracking, as described in detail on the collabskus.neocities.org resource:

**Packing** concentrates opposition voters into as few districts as possible, giving them enormous wins in those districts — margins of 70%, 80%, 90% — while "wasting" a large number of votes on margins far larger than needed to win. A Democratic district won 90%–10% wastes 40 percentage points of votes. That same vote share, distributed across three districts in a 50%-plus win in each, could produce three Democratic seats instead of one.

**Cracking** splits communities that tend to vote the same way across multiple districts, diluting their voting power so that they are always a minority. A city that votes 65% Democratic gets split across four suburban districts where Democrats constitute 40% of each district's voters — enough to be competitive but not enough to win.

Together, pack and crack transform majority support into minority representation. In a properly gerrymandered state, a party can win 55% of the statewide vote and receive 30% of the seats.

### 7.3 The Technology Revolution: Big Data and Partisan Optimization

For most of American history, gerrymandering was limited by information. Mapmakers in the nineteenth century had rough census population data and general knowledge of neighborhood voting patterns. They could draw crude gerrymanders, but they were imprecise instruments.

That changed dramatically with the advent of personal computing in the 1980s and the expansion of available data in the 1990s. By the 2010 redistricting cycle, mapmakers had access to:

- Block-by-block Census data on race, age, and housing tenure
- Precinct-level voting data from decades of elections
- Individual voter registration data in most states, including party affiliation, voting history, and in some cases consumer data correlated with political preferences
- Geographic Information System (GIS) software capable of drawing, analyzing, and optimizing thousands of map configurations simultaneously
- Predictive modeling sophisticated enough to estimate the partisan lean of any given map configuration with high confidence

The 2011 redistricting in North Carolina was particularly notable for the level of technical sophistication deployed. Republican mapmakers, working with the consulting firm Hofeller Strategies (run by Thomas Hofeller, who would become known as the "GOP's redistricting guru"), used demographic and voting data to draw maps that were specifically optimized to produce the maximum Republican advantage across the broadest range of plausible electoral scenarios.

In 2019, after Hofeller's death, his daughter discovered hard drives containing his working files and turned them over to litigation teams in redistricting cases. These files revealed the explicit partisan reasoning behind map-drawing decisions, including communications that made clear that the stated justifications for district boundaries — compactness, community of interest, county lines — were post-hoc rationalizations for maps that had been drawn to maximize Republican advantage.

### 7.4 Prison Gerrymandering: The Rural Advantage

One of the less-discussed but deeply consequential dimensions of gerrymandering is the practice of prison gerrymandering — counting incarcerated people as residents of the prison's location for redistricting purposes.

The Census Bureau, since its founding, has counted incarcerated people at the location of their prison rather than at their home address. For most of American history, this practice had limited electoral significance because prison populations were relatively small. But the explosion of incarceration in the United States following the "War on Drugs" in the 1980s and 1990s — which produced a fivefold increase in the prison population between 1980 and 2010 — has made prison gerrymandering a significant distortion.

The pattern is strikingly consistent: prisons are disproportionately located in rural areas, often in communities with declining populations that have actively sought them as economic development. The incarcerated population is disproportionately drawn from urban communities of color. The result is that rural (often predominantly white) legislative districts get credit for large prison populations that cannot vote — inflating those districts' population counts and increasing their political representation — while the urban communities from which those incarcerated people came see their population counts deflated and their representation diminished.

Some states have begun to address prison gerrymandering — New York, California, Colorado, Maryland, Nevada, New Jersey, Washington, and others have passed laws requiring that incarcerated people be counted at their home addresses for redistricting purposes. But most states have not, and the federal Census Bureau's practice remains unchanged.

### 7.5 Felon Disenfranchisement: The Longest Shadow

No discussion of voting rights in America is complete without examining felon disenfranchisement — the practice of stripping voting rights from people convicted of felonies. The United States is one of a small number of democracies in the world that practices felon disenfranchisement; most democratic nations restore voting rights immediately upon release from prison, or have never stripped them in the first place.

In the United States, 48 states disenfranchise people while they are incarcerated. Many states go further: 17 states disenfranchise people on parole, and 11 states disenfranchise people on probation. In the most extreme cases — Maine and Vermont are notable exceptions — some states permanently disenfranchise certain categories of people even after they have completed their full sentence and returned to the community.

The racial dimensions of felon disenfranchisement are stark. Black Americans are incarcerated at approximately 5 times the rate of white Americans, a disparity that reflects not just differences in criminal behavior (studies generally show that differences in offending rates are much smaller than differences in incarceration rates) but differences in policing, prosecution, and sentencing. The result is that felon disenfranchisement strips voting rights disproportionately from Black Americans — in some states, more than 1 in 10 Black adults are disenfranchised due to criminal convictions.

The historical roots of modern felon disenfranchisement laws are explicitly racial in many states. Post-Reconstruction state constitutional conventions in the South deliberately tailored their felon disenfranchisement statutes to cover the offenses that Black men were disproportionately prosecuted for — often minor property crimes or vagrancy offenses — while excluding offenses more commonly associated with white defendants. The explicit goal was to use the criminal justice system to accomplish the political disenfranchisement that the Fifteenth Amendment had (in theory) prohibited.

## Part 8: The Case for Statewide Multi-Member Districts — The Mathematical Solution

### 8.1 The Fundamental Problem with Single-Member Districts

Everything we have discussed in Parts 1 through 7 — the gerrymandering, the racial vote dilution, the manipulation of district lines to predetermine election outcomes, the century-long struggle to make minority representation possible under a system designed to prevent it — flows from a single structural decision: the 1842 mandate of single-member, winner-take-all districts.

A single-member district is a winner-take-all system. The candidate who gets the most votes wins 100% of the representation for that district. Every other voter — sometimes 49.9% of the district — gets nothing. Their votes are "wasted" in the sense that they do not contribute to representation.

This structure creates the mechanism by which gerrymandering operates. The value of gerrymandering is precisely the ability to waste opposition votes — to ensure that opposition voters are always in the losing 40% or 45% across many districts, rather than being in the winning 50%+1 in a proportional number of districts. If you eliminate the mechanism by which votes can be wasted — by eliminating single-member districts — you eliminate the mechanism by which gerrymandering operates.

This is not a theoretical claim. It is mathematics.

### 8.2 How Multi-Member Districts Work: The Basic Mechanics

A multi-member district is simply a district that elects more than one representative. Instead of California's 52 single-member congressional districts, California might have 13 four-member districts, or 8 five-member districts, or — in the most radical and most effective version of the reform — one statewide district electing all 52 representatives.

The key insight is this: in a multi-member district with proportional representation, a party needs only approximately 1/(N+1) of the vote to be guaranteed at least one seat, where N is the number of seats in the district. This threshold is called the "Droop quota." The mathematics:

**In a 3-seat district:** Droop quota = 100/(3+1) + 1 = 26%. Any candidate or party with 26% support is mathematically guaranteed a seat.

**In a 5-seat district:** Droop quota = 100/(5+1) + 1 = 17.7%. Any candidate or party with 17.7% support is mathematically guaranteed a seat.

**In a 10-seat district:** Droop quota = 100/(10+1) + 1 = 10%. Any candidate or party with 10% support is mathematically guaranteed a seat.

**In a 52-seat statewide district (California):** Droop quota = 100/(52+1) + 1 ≈ 2.9%. Any candidate or party with approximately 3% support can win a seat.

Notice what this means for racial and ethnic minority communities. Black Americans make up approximately 13.6% of the U.S. population. In a 5-seat district, Black voters who vote cohesively have more than enough votes (17.7% threshold) to elect at least one representative of their choice — *without any need for majority-minority districts, without any need for Section 2 litigation, without any dependence on the good faith of legislatures or the sympathy of federal judges*. In a 7-seat district, they clear the threshold twice over, and could expect to elect two representatives of their choice.

Latino Americans make up approximately 19.1% of the U.S. population. Native Americans, depending on the jurisdiction, may be 2-10% of the population. Asian Americans are approximately 6% nationally. In multi-member districts of appropriate size, all of these communities would have effective representation as a matter of arithmetic — not as a product of litigation, legislative grace, or judicial enforcement.

This is the argument for statewide multi-member districts from first principles. It is not a partisan argument. It is not a liberal or conservative argument. It is arithmetic. The math simply does not permit the kind of vote dilution that single-member districts allow.

### 8.3 The Single Transferable Vote: How RCV Makes It Work

Multi-member districts, by themselves, do not guarantee proportional outcomes. The voting method used within the district matters enormously. The voting method specifically designed for multi-member districts to produce proportional outcomes is the Single Transferable Vote — the form of ranked choice voting that is used in the Fair Representation Act.

Under STV, voters rank candidates in order of preference: 1st choice, 2nd choice, 3rd choice, and so on. They can rank as many or as few candidates as they wish. The ranking of additional choices never hurts the voter's top-ranked candidate — it is simply a backup that comes into play only if the first choice is already elected (with surplus votes to distribute) or eliminated.

The counting process works as follows:

1. Establish the Droop quota: total valid votes divided by (seats + 1), plus 1.
2. Count first-preference votes for each candidate.
3. Any candidate who meets or exceeds the quota is immediately elected.
4. Surplus votes (votes received by elected candidates beyond the quota) are transferred to voters' next preferences at a proportional transfer value.
5. If more seats remain to be filled, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated, and those votes are transferred to voters' next preferences at full value.
6. Steps 4 and 5 continue until all seats are filled.

The result is a set of elected candidates that closely reflects the distribution of voter preferences. Votes are rarely truly "wasted" — most voters contribute to the election of at least one candidate, because their vote either directly elects their first choice, or gets transferred to elect their second or third choice.

STV is not a new or untested system. It has been used in Ireland for all elections since 1921. It is used for the Australian Senate. It is used in Scottish local council elections and Northern Ireland Assembly elections. Malta uses it. It has been used in multiple American cities, including Cambridge, Massachusetts (where it was used from 1941 to 2001), and is currently used in Portland, Oregon for city council elections.

The empirical evidence from jurisdictions using STV is consistent: it produces significantly more proportional outcomes, significantly lower rates of wasted votes, significantly better representation of minority communities, and (in most jurisdictions) higher voter satisfaction with electoral outcomes.

### 8.4 The Statewide Single District: The Most Radical and Most Effective Option

The reform this article advocates goes beyond the Fair Representation Act's proposal of multi-member sub-state districts. It calls for a single statewide multi-member district for every state in the United States.

Under this system, every state would be a single district for the purpose of electing its U.S. House members. All voters in California would vote in a single statewide election for all 52 California House seats. All voters in Texas would vote in a single statewide election for all 38 Texas House seats. All voters in Wyoming would vote in a single statewide election for Wyoming's one House seat — effectively the same as the current system in single-member states.

The advantages of a single statewide district over the Fair Representation Act's multi-member sub-state districts are several:

**Gerrymandering is completely impossible.** There are no lines to draw in a statewide election. You cannot gerrymander a state's borders. The only "map" is the state itself.

**The threshold for minority representation is minimized.** In a 52-seat California statewide district, a community with even 3% statewide support can elect a representative. In a 38-seat Texas statewide district, a community with 3% statewide support — including Native Americans, who constitute approximately 1% of Texas' population, the combined share of all Native American communities may approach a threshold — can elect a representative. Communities of color in large states would not need to be geographically concentrated to win representation.

**Community cohesion is respected.** The current system requires minority communities to be "geographically compact" — one of the *Gingles* preconditions — because districts have geographic boundaries. A Black community spread across an entire metropolitan area, whose members share economic and political interests but are not concentrated in a single geographic block, faces challenges under the current system. Under a statewide multi-member district, geographic dispersion is irrelevant. What matters is the size of the community and its political cohesion.

**It produces genuine proportionality.** A statewide district will produce outcomes that are approximately proportional to the statewide distribution of voter preferences — not perfectly proportional, because the Droop quota creates some rounding effects, but much closer to proportional than any single-member system can achieve.

**It eliminates the redistricting cycle.** Currently, congressional district lines are redrawn after every ten-year Census, producing a decade-long redistricting cycle that generates enormous amounts of litigation, political conflict, and uncertainty. A statewide district has no lines to redraw. The redistricting problem disappears entirely.

### 8.5 The VRA After Callais: Why Structural Reform Is the Only Path

The *Callais* decision makes the case for structural reform undeniable. For sixty years, advocates for minority voting rights operated within a system of single-member districts and relied on the Voting Rights Act, enforced by federal courts, to prevent the worst abuses of that system. They accepted the constraints of the *Gingles* framework. They litigated district by district, election cycle by election cycle, in an endless arms race against state legislatures that always had superior resources, superior political motivation, and the ultimate advantage: they were drawing the maps.

The Roberts Court has now taken away the legal tools that made that litigation possible. Section 5 preclearance is gone. Section 2 as applied to voting restrictions has been gutted by *Brnovich*. Section 2 as applied to redistricting has now been gutted by *Callais*. The litigation pathway is closed. There is no federal court option.

What remains? State courts, in states where the state constitution provides stronger protections than the federal one. Some states — Pennsylvania, North Carolina (when Democrats controlled the state supreme court), Ohio — have seen successful state constitutional challenges to gerrymanders. But state courts are subject to political pressure, subject to the retaliatory targeting of judges who rule against partisan gerrymanders, and in any case provide a patchwork solution at best.

There is only one comprehensive solution: Congress must pass a law under its Article I, Section 4 authority that changes how House elections are conducted. And the specific change that is needed is the replacement of single-member districts with statewide multi-member districts elected by the Single Transferable Vote.

This is not a proposal to amend the Constitution. It does not require a constitutional convention or ratification by three-fourths of the states. It requires Congress to pass a statute, and the President to sign it. The constitutional authority is explicit and unambiguous. The precedent is clear — Congress created single-member districts by statute in 1842, and it can change that mandate by statute in 2027.

## Part 9: How It Would Work in Practice — State-by-State Analysis

### 9.1 The Large-State Experience: California and Texas

**California** has 52 congressional districts under the current apportionment, giving it the largest congressional delegation. California is one of the most politically and demographically diverse states in the nation — approximately 38% white non-Hispanic, 39% Latino, 15% Asian, and 6% Black.

Under the current system, California's congressional delegation is entirely Democratic — all 52 members are Democrats. Republicans, who regularly receive 30-40% of the statewide vote, have zero representation in California's congressional delegation. This is entirely legal, entirely democratic in a narrow technical sense (each district was won fairly), and utterly disproportionate to the actual political preferences of California's voters.

Under a statewide multi-member district with STV, the result would be dramatically different. A party receiving 40% of the statewide vote in California would expect to elect approximately 21 of the 52 seats. Republicans would have substantial representation. More importantly, the vast diversity of California's communities — urban progressives in San Francisco and Los Angeles, moderate suburban voters in the Central Valley and Orange County, rural conservatives in the north and east, the enormous and diverse Asian-American community, the Latino community (which is not monolithic but spans a wide range of political perspectives) — would all have meaningful representation.

California's Latino community — approximately 39% of the population — would be expected to elect approximately 20 of California's 52 representatives under a simple proportionality analysis. The Asian-American community — approximately 15% — would be expected to elect approximately 8. The Black community — approximately 6% — would be expected to elect approximately 3. The 6% figure, which under a single-member system might translate to zero seats in many states, represents a potentially electable threshold under a statewide STV system.

**Texas** has 38 congressional districts, with a population that is approximately 41% white non-Hispanic, 40% Latino, 12.5% Black, and 5% Asian. Under the current maps, drawn by a Republican-controlled legislature, Republicans hold 25 of 38 seats (66%) despite receiving approximately 53% of the statewide vote in recent elections — a significant structural Republican advantage.

Under a statewide multi-member district with STV, the outcome would be far more proportional. Democrats receiving 47% of the statewide vote would expect approximately 18 of 38 seats. The Latino community — 40% of Texas' population — would expect approximately 15 seats. The Black community — 12.5% — would expect approximately 5 seats. Native American communities in Texas — small but present — would have a realistic path to representation they currently lack.

### 9.2 The Mid-Size State Experience: Louisiana and Alabama

**Louisiana** — the state at the center of *Callais* — has six congressional districts. Its population is approximately 57% white and 32% Black, with smaller Latino and Asian communities.

Under the current single-member system, after *Callais*, Louisiana can be expected to revert to a map with one majority-Black district, or perhaps zero majority-Black districts if the state is willing to push the boundaries that *Callais* has now opened. The majority of Black Louisianians will find their votes wasted in districts where the white majority always wins.

Under a statewide multi-member district with six seats and STV, the math is straightforward. The Droop quota for a 6-seat district is 100/(6+1) + 1 = 14.3% + 1. Black voters constitute approximately 32% of Louisiana's voting-age population. Assuming political cohesion (which the historical record of Louisiana politics strongly supports), Black voters would expect to win at least 2 of the 6 seats — roughly proportional to their share of the population. This outcome would be guaranteed by arithmetic, not by federal litigation, not by the goodwill of the state legislature, and not by the enforceability of the Voting Rights Act.

**Alabama** — which has 7 congressional districts and a Black population of approximately 27% — would see similar results. The Droop quota for a 7-seat district is 100/(7+1) + 1 = 13.5%. Black voters at 27% of the population would expect to win approximately 2 of the 7 seats — more than double the single Black-majority district they currently have and equal to their proportional share. No litigation required.

### 9.3 The Small-State Experience: Wyoming and Vermont

The proposal for statewide multi-member districts faces its most significant practical challenge in states with very small congressional delegations. Wyoming has one congressional seat. Vermont has one. Alaska has one. Several other states have two or three.

For states with a single congressional seat, the current system and a statewide multi-member district are identical — they are one-seat statewide elections. For states with two or three seats, the multi-member district adds some proportionality but limited minority representation, because the Droop quota for a 2-seat district is 34% and for a 3-seat district is 26%.

This is the one genuine limitation of the single-statewide-district approach. For small states with small delegations, proportionality is limited by arithmetic. There is no obvious fix for this — you cannot give Wyoming more congressional seats without violating the constitutional requirement that apportionment be based on population. (The DC Statehood movement and the Puerto Rico Statehood movement, if successful, would add additional seats; similarly, a proposal to expand the House from 435 to 600 or more members would both improve small-state proportionality and increase the overall size of the threshold.)

One important note: the House has not been expanded since 1929, when it was fixed at 435 members. The United States in 1929 had a population of approximately 120 million. Today it has approximately 335 million. The ratio of representatives to constituents has fallen dramatically. Many electoral reform advocates argue that expanding the House to 600 or more members would both improve the responsiveness of the body and improve the proportionality of representation in small states. This is a separate but related reform worth examining alongside the multi-member district proposal.

### 9.4 Native American Communities: The Case for Statewide Districts

No group in America demonstrates the failure of the current single-member district system for minority representation more clearly than Native Americans.

Native American communities face a distinctive set of challenges. Their populations are often distributed across large geographic areas — including reservation lands that may span county or state lines — and are often too small, in any given county or region, to constitute a majority in a single-member district. They are frequently cracked across multiple districts, diluting their voting power to insignificance.

Montana has the highest percentage of Native American residents of any contiguous state — approximately 6.7% of its population. Montana has two congressional districts and has never elected a Native American to the U.S. House of Representatives. This is not because Native Americans in Montana don't have political preferences or don't vote. It is because the single-member district system, combined with geographic dispersion, ensures that Native American communities are never a majority or near-majority in any single district.

Under a statewide multi-member system in Montana (which currently has 2 congressional seats, producing a Droop quota of 34%), Native Americans at 6.7% of the population would still fall below the threshold for guaranteed representation. But expanding the House and creating a statewide multi-member district with 3 or 4 seats would lower the threshold sufficiently that Native American voters, voting cohesively, would have a realistic path to electing a representative.

In larger states with significant Native American populations — Arizona (5.3% Native American), New Mexico (9.4% Native American), Oklahoma (9.1% Native American), South Dakota (9.2%), North Dakota (5.9%), Alaska (15.6%) — statewide multi-member districts would transform Native American political representation. Arizona has 9 congressional seats. The Droop quota for a 9-seat statewide district is 10.9%. Arizona's Native American population at 5.3% would not quite clear the threshold alone, but combined with other minority communities and in the right coalition dynamics, Native American candidates could realistically win seats. In New Mexico's 3-seat statewide district (Droop quota 26%), a unified Native American vote at 9.4% would fall below the threshold, but in a 4-seat expansion (Droop quota 21%) would be more competitive.

The point is not that statewide multi-member districts solve every minority representation problem for every group in every state. The point is that they systematically improve minority representation compared to the current system while eliminating gerrymandering entirely — and they do so through arithmetic rather than through litigation and federal court enforcement.

### 9.5 The Latino Experience: From Fragmentation to Representation

Latino Americans constitute approximately 19.1% of the U.S. population — the largest minority group — and their representation in Congress remains significantly below their population share. The Congressional Hispanic Caucus has grown substantially since the 1970s, but Latino members still constitute only about 9-10% of the House, roughly half of their proportional share.

The sources of this underrepresentation are multiple. Geographic fragmentation — Latino communities are spread across urban areas, suburbs, agricultural areas, and coastal communities across the country — makes it difficult to create Latino-majority districts outside of certain concentrated areas in Texas, California, Florida, Arizona, New Mexico, and New York. Outside of these states, significant Latino communities exist in Illinois, Colorado, Nevada, North Carolina, Georgia, and dozens of other states where they are too dispersed to form district majorities but numerous enough to constitute a significant bloc.

Under a statewide multi-member district system, Latino communities in every state with a significant Latino population would have a realistic path to representation. In Texas (40% Latino, 38 seats, Droop quota ≈ 2.7%), Latino voters could elect approximately 15 representatives — more than triple their current representation and roughly proportional to their population. In California (39% Latino, 52 seats, Droop quota ≈ 2.9%), approximately 20 Latino representatives would be the expected proportional outcome.

More importantly, in smaller states with significant Latino populations where the community currently has almost no representation — Colorado (21% Latino, 8 seats), Nevada (19% Latino, 4 seats), Florida (26% Latino, 28 seats), Arizona (32% Latino, 9 seats) — statewide multi-member districts would dramatically improve Latino representation.

## Part 10: The International Evidence — What Proportional Systems Actually Produce

### 10.1 Ireland and the Single Transferable Vote

Ireland has used the Single Transferable Vote for all of its national elections since 1921 — more than a century of continuous operation. The Irish experience provides the most comprehensive empirical evidence of how STV works in practice.

Ireland elects its Dáil (lower house of parliament) through multi-member constituencies of 3 to 5 members each. The constituencies are drawn by an independent commission with strict criteria. Results are remarkably proportional — parties typically receive seat shares within a few percentage points of their vote shares. Independent candidates, who have no party structure, regularly win seats because STV allows voters to support an independent first-choice without wasting their vote — they can rank party candidates as backup choices. Independent members consistently constitute a significant portion of the Dáil.

The Irish system is notable for several characteristics that are relevant to the American context. First, within-party competition: because multiple members of the same party run in the same constituency, parties must field more than one candidate and voters can choose between them, providing a form of intra-party accountability that is absent from single-member primary systems. Second, geographic representation within proportionality: because constituencies are sub-national (county-level, roughly), geographic communities retain representation while proportionality is achieved. Third, coalition governance: because proportional systems typically produce multi-party results without a clear majority, governance requires coalition building — which tends to moderate policy outcomes and incentivize cross-party cooperation.

### 10.2 Australia's Senate

Australia uses the STV for its Senate elections, with each state electing either 6 (regular elections) or 12 (double dissolution elections) senators using STV. The Australian Senate demonstrates both the benefits and the challenges of STV with large district magnitudes.

The benefits: the Australian Senate includes representatives from multiple parties, including the Australian Greens (typically 12-14% of the national vote, routinely winning seats), independent senators, and various minor parties. The Senate genuinely reflects the diversity of Australian political opinion in a way that the House of Representatives (which uses instant-runoff voting in single-member districts) does not.

The challenges: with very large district magnitudes, the effective threshold for winning a seat drops very low, and parties can game the system through "preference deals" — arrangements in which parties with small vote shares direct their surplus votes to allied parties. This has occasionally produced senators elected with extremely small primary vote shares through a cascade of preference transfers. The Australian Electoral Commission has made adjustments to the counting rules to address the most egregious gaming, but the issue illustrates that very large district magnitudes require careful attention to counting rules.

The American context is different in important ways. Federal election rules would be set by Congress, not by individual states. The Fair Representation Act and the statewide multi-member district proposal would include specific rules for the STV counting process that could address these gaming concerns.

### 10.3 Scotland and Northern Ireland

Scotland has used STV for local council elections since 2007, with each council ward electing 3 or 4 members. The results have been notably more proportional than the previous first-past-the-post system, with significant gains in representation for women and for parties that previously were systematically disadvantaged by the single-member system.

Northern Ireland uses STV for all of its elections — Westminster Parliamentary elections, Northern Ireland Assembly elections, and local council elections — and has done so since the 1970s. The adoption of STV in Northern Ireland was directly related to the need for minority community representation in a deeply divided society: the Catholic/nationalist and Protestant/unionist communities needed to be able to elect representatives from their communities in proportion to their population, and STV provided the mechanism for doing so without the need for litigation or explicitly designated community-based districts.

The parallel to the American situation is striking. Northern Ireland faced a problem — a historic minority community (Catholics/nationalists) that was systematically underrepresented and disenfranchised by a majoritarian electoral system used by a hostile majority — and the solution was a proportional system using STV. The United States faces the same fundamental problem. The solution is available.

### 10.4 Countries That Have Experimented and Reverted

It is important to acknowledge that some jurisdictions that adopted forms of proportional representation have subsequently reverted to plurality or majoritarian systems, and that these cases deserve examination.

New Zealand adopted a Mixed Member Proportional system (MMP) in 1996 after a referendum, replacing the first-past-the-post system. New Zealand's subsequent elections have produced proportional outcomes and greater representation for Māori New Zealanders and for smaller parties. There has been no reversion.

Italy adopted proportional representation after World War II but has repeatedly modified its electoral system, with ongoing debates between proportional and majoritarian approaches. The Italian experience illustrates the challenge of political instability in highly proportional systems with large numbers of parties — though it is worth noting that the United States' two-party system would make Italian-style fragmentation unlikely even under a proportional system.

The cities of Burlington, Vermont and Aspen, Colorado adopted instant-runoff voting (a single-winner version of RCV) in the early 2000s and subsequently repealed it. Burlington's repeal came after a 2009 mayoral election in which the Condorcet winner (the candidate who would have beaten every other candidate in a one-on-one matchup) was not elected — the incumbent mayor was defeated despite broad second-choice support. This is a known limitation of IRV in single-winner races, but it is not applicable to STV in multi-winner races, which uses a different algorithm designed specifically to produce proportional outcomes.

The relevant comparison is not IRV in single-winner races but STV in multi-winner races. The empirical evidence for STV in multi-winner contexts — Ireland, Australia, Scotland, Northern Ireland, Cambridge MA — is consistently positive.

## Part 11: The Objections — And Why They Don't Hold Up

### 11.1 "It Will Produce Extreme Fragmentation and Instability"

The most common objection to proportional representation from American commentators is that it will produce a fragmented, multi-party system with unstable governments — the kind of situation that critics associate with Weimar Germany or contemporary Italy.

This objection misunderstands both the proposal and the political science.

First, the statewide multi-member district proposal is for the House of Representatives, not for the executive. The United States has a presidential system, not a parliamentary one. The President is elected separately, through the Electoral College, and governs independently of Congress. The "government instability" concern in parliamentary systems arises because the government (the cabinet) depends on maintaining a parliamentary majority — if the coalition fractures, the government falls. This dynamic does not exist in the American system. The President serves a fixed four-year term regardless of congressional elections.

Second, the two-party system in the United States is deeply rooted, with structural features that would likely persist even under a proportional system. The Duverger's Law tendency toward two-party systems under plurality voting is somewhat reversed by proportional systems, but the degree of party fragmentation depends on many factors, including district magnitude, electoral thresholds, and political culture. With district magnitudes of 4-7 (the range in the Fair Representation Act proposal), effective thresholds of 14-26% would likely support 3-4 major parties rather than 10+ minor parties.

Third, the empirical evidence from countries using proportional representation does not support the instability narrative in most cases. The Nordic countries (Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Finland) all use proportional representation and have been models of political stability and effective governance for decades. Ireland uses STV and has been politically stable. The Netherlands uses a highly proportional system and has been stable. Germany uses MMP with a 5% threshold and has been extremely stable — in fact, Germany's consensual coalition politics has often been credited with producing better long-term policy outcomes than majoritarian systems in the UK or the US.

Fourth, and most directly relevant: the United States under the current system is not stable. The polarization, dysfunction, inability to pass legislation, debt ceiling crises, government shutdowns, and incapacity to address major national challenges that characterize contemporary American democracy are products of the current system, not arguments for preserving it. A system that consistently fails to address climate change, gun violence, healthcare costs, housing affordability, and infrastructure decay is not a model of stability — it is a model of paralysis.

### 11.2 "It Will Reduce Geographic Representation for Rural Communities"

This objection deserves serious engagement. Critics of statewide multi-member districts argue that they will disadvantage rural communities, because in a statewide at-large election, candidates and parties will focus on urban population centers where most voters live, ignoring rural areas.

There are several responses to this objection.

First, the objection assumes that under the current system, rural communities are well-represented. This is questionable. Rural America has experienced decades of economic decline — loss of manufacturing jobs, consolidation of agricultural operations, closure of rural hospitals and schools, reduction of rail and bus service — despite having disproportionate representation in Congress due to the mathematical structure of the Senate (every state gets two senators regardless of population) and the gerrymandering-enhanced Republican majority in the House. The representatives ostensibly advocating for rural communities have, in many cases, been more focused on culture-war issues and tax cuts for wealthy donors than on the economic revitalization of rural areas.

Second, under STV with a large district, rural communities that vote cohesively can absolutely elect representatives of their choice. If a particular regional identity — say, rural agricultural communities in the San Joaquin Valley of California — is sufficiently cohesive and numerous to clear the Droop threshold, candidates representing that community can win seats in a statewide STV election. The system does not inherently privilege urban voters; it privileges voters who vote cohesively for candidates and parties that represent their interests.

Third, candidates campaigning in a statewide STV election have an incentive to seek second and third-preference votes across the entire state, including from rural voters. Because votes transfer, a candidate who is a clear first choice for urban voters but who can also attract second-preference votes from suburban and rural voters who rank their local candidate first will do better than a candidate who ignores non-urban voters entirely. STV creates incentives for geographic coalition-building.

Fourth, the Senate already provides rural areas with disproportionate geographic representation. Small states with large rural populations — Wyoming, Montana, North Dakota, South Dakota, Idaho, Nebraska — each get two Senate seats regardless of population. The House, under the Constitution, is supposed to represent people, not land. A proportional House would actually fulfill its constitutional function better than the current gerrymandered system.

### 11.3 "Ranked Choice Voting Is Confusing and Will Disenfranchise Unsophisticated Voters"

This objection is sometimes raised in good faith and sometimes as a pretext. The "voter confusion" concern deserves a careful response.

The empirical evidence from jurisdictions that have adopted ranked choice voting does not support the claim that it causes significant confusion or disenfranchisement. In Ireland, where STV has been used for over a century, voter errors are rare and the system is broadly understood. In Maine, which has used ranked choice voting for congressional elections since 2018, exit polls show high voter comprehension and satisfaction. In Alaska, which adopted ranked choice voting in 2020, voters have navigated the system without significant difficulty.

The design of the ballot matters. A well-designed ranked choice ballot — with clear instructions, numerical ranking positions clearly marked, and non-required ranking (voters who only want to make one choice can do so) — is not substantially more complicated than a standard ballot. The key design principles are: (1) voters rank candidates in order of preference, (2) ranking additional candidates never hurts your first choice, (3) you can rank as many or as few candidates as you want.

The concern that "unsophisticated voters" will be disenfranchised is, in the context of voting rights history, a charge that deserves particular scrutiny. The "literacy test" and "poll tax" rationale — that certain voters are too unsophisticated to participate in the electoral system — has historically been deployed specifically against minority voters. We should be deeply skeptical when the same logic is applied to electoral reforms that would primarily benefit minority voters.

A federal program to educate voters about the new system — funded by the federal government as a condition of implementing the reform — would be both appropriate and constitutional. The Fair Representation Act already includes provisions for federal payments to states to implement ranked choice voting, including voter education.

### 11.4 "It's Too Radical a Change"

The most diffuse objection is simply that statewide multi-member districts represent too large a departure from existing practice — that the disruption to the existing electoral system would be too great, and that incremental reforms are preferable.

The response to this objection must begin with a clear-eyed assessment of the current moment. The Voting Rights Act has been dismantled. Federal courts are closed to partisan gerrymandering claims. The most extreme partisan and racial gerrymanders in American history are in place or being planned. The current system is not merely imperfect — it is systematically antidemocratic. It is designed to ensure that the party with the most votes does not necessarily get the most seats. It is designed to waste minority votes. It is designed to entrench power rather than reflect the will of the governed.

Incrementalism in the face of systematic structural injustice is not moderation. It is complicity. The civil rights movement understood this. The people who told Martin Luther King Jr. to wait — who praised his goals while urging him to be patient, to move slowly, to not disturb the peace — were not moderates protecting the system from radicalism. They were defenders of the status quo seeking to prevent change.

Moreover, the change is not as radical as it sounds. The United States used statewide at-large elections for much of its early history, before the 1842 Apportionment Act mandated single-member districts. Multi-member districts with proportional representation are the norm in most advanced democracies. The American single-member, winner-take-all system is the outlier globally, not the international standard. Adopting the system used by most of the world's stable democracies is not radical.

And the constitutional authority is clear. The 1842 mandate for single-member districts was a congressional statute. It can be repealed by a congressional statute. The Elections Clause gives Congress explicit authority to make this change.

### 11.5 "It Won't Pass Congress"

This is the most honest of the objections, because it is genuinely difficult to see the path by which a bill mandating statewide multi-member districts passes both chambers of Congress.

The Fair Representation Act has been introduced in every Congress since 2017 and has consistently failed to advance. It has never had more than a handful of cosponsors, and Republican support for any structural electoral reform that would reduce Republican structural advantages is essentially nonexistent.

But the political calculus can change. The 2026 midterm elections are being conducted under maps that, post-*Callais*, will be increasingly hospitable to the kind of extreme Republican gerrymandering that produces outcomes like North Carolina's 2022 elections (56% of votes, 71% of seats for Republicans). If Democrats win back the House and the Senate in 2026 or 2028, and simultaneously win the presidency, the window for structural reform opens.

The Voting Rights Act itself was unimaginable as a political possibility in 1960. Five years later, it passed with enormous margins in both chambers. Political possibilities change rapidly, especially in moments of crisis. The *Callais* decision, coming on the day it did, creates exactly the kind of crisis that changes political possibilities.

Justice Kagan's dissent and legal scholar Richard Hasen's analysis both note that the *Callais* decision could "kick off the start of a new civil rights movement and more serious talk of Supreme Court reform in the midst of crucially important midterm elections." A new civil rights movement — organized around the concrete, legally sophisticated demand for statewide multi-member districts — is not only possible. Given what the Court has now done, it may be inevitable.

## Part 12: The Path Forward — What Congress Must Do

### 12.1 The Constitutional Foundation: Article I, Section 4

The legal foundation for congressional action is explicit, well-established, and not subject to serious constitutional challenge.

Article I, Section 4 of the United States Constitution states: "The Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives, shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof; but the Congress may at any time by Law make or alter such Regulations, except as to the Places of chusing Senators."

The Supreme Court has confirmed multiple times that this clause grants Congress broad, plenary authority over the conduct of federal elections. In *Smiley v. Holm* (1932), the Court held that Congress's power under this clause extends to all aspects of the electoral process. In *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton* (1995), the Court affirmed the breadth of Congress's Elections Clause power, while holding that it does not extend to altering the qualifications for federal office established in the Constitution itself.

The mandate of single-member districts — currently found in 2 U.S.C. § 2c — is a congressional statute enacted pursuant to this same Elections Clause power. Congress in 1842 exercised its Elections Clause authority to require single-member districts. Congress today can exercise the same authority to require multi-member districts. The constitutional symmetry is perfect.

Additionally, Congress has authority under Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment — which explicitly grants Congress "power to enforce" the amendment "by appropriate legislation" — and Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment — which similarly grants Congress enforcement power. Given that the entire *raison d'être* of multi-member districts in this context is to protect minority voting rights that the Roberts Court has gutted, these enforcement provisions provide additional constitutional authorization.

### 12.2 The Legislation: What a Statewide Multi-Member District Act Would Look Like

Drawing on the Fair Representation Act (H.R. 4632 in the 119th Congress) and extending it to the more radical statewide-district approach, here is what the core legislation would need to include:

**Section 1: Findings and Purpose.** Congress finds that the current single-member district system for electing members of the House of Representatives enables partisan and racial gerrymandering, fails to provide fair representation to minority communities, and is inconsistent with the principles of democratic self-governance. Congress further finds that it has constitutional authority under Article I, Section 4 and the enforcement clauses of the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments to remedy these harms.

**Section 2: Repeal of 2 U.S.C. § 2c.** The existing mandate for single-member districts is repealed.

**Section 3: Statewide Multi-Member Districts.** Every state is constituted as a single congressional district for the purpose of electing its apportioned Representatives to the House. All Representatives from a given state shall be elected in a single statewide election.

**Section 4: Ranked Choice Voting / Single Transferable Vote.** Elections for the House of Representatives shall be conducted using the Single Transferable Vote method. Voters shall rank candidates in order of preference. The Droop quota shall be used to determine the number of votes required for election. Detailed tabulation rules (handling of surplus votes, elimination procedures, batch elimination, treatment of exhausted ballots) shall be specified in statute.

**Section 5: Payments to States.** The federal government shall provide funding to states to implement the new voting system, including ballot design, tabulation equipment upgrades, voter education campaigns, and poll worker training.

**Section 6: VRA Compliance.** Nothing in this Act shall be construed to diminish any protection afforded to voters under the Voting Rights Act of 1965, as amended, or any other federal law. The statewide multi-member district system shall be implemented in a manner that provides to minority voters at least as great an opportunity to participate in the political process as provided by the previous system.

**Section 7: Effective Date.** This Act shall take effect beginning with elections held following the next regular decennial Census and reapportionment, provided that nothing in this section shall prevent voluntary early adoption by any state with the consent of the Secretary of State.

### 12.3 Supreme Court Reform: The Unavoidable Question

Legal scholar Richard Hasen, one of the country's leading voting rights experts, has written in the immediate aftermath of *Callais* that the decision makes Supreme Court reform "unavoidable" — that a Court so consistently and systematically hostile to minority voting rights cannot simply be relied upon to enforce future legislative remedies.

This is correct and deserves serious engagement. Even if Congress passes a statewide multi-member districts act with STV, the Roberts Court's conservative majority could find constitutional reasons to strike it down. We have seen how creative the Court's conservative majority can be in inventing doctrinal reasons to reach desired outcomes. The *Callais* decision's exploitation of the interaction between the Equal Protection Clause and Section 2 is a perfect example: a doctrine that was supposed to protect minority rights is turned into a weapon against them.

The proposals for Supreme Court reform include:

**Term limits.** Justices would serve fixed terms (18 years is the most commonly proposed length) rather than lifetime appointments. This would regularize the appointment process, reduce the stakes of any individual appointment, and ensure that the Court's composition changes more regularly in response to democratic elections. A constitutional amendment would likely be required, but legislation providing that justices "rotate" to lower federal courts after 18 years of Supreme Court service has been proposed as a potentially constitutional alternative.

**Expansion.** Adding seats to the Supreme Court — often called "court-packing," though supporters prefer "court depacking" or "court reform" — is constitutional (the size of the Court is set by statute, not the Constitution, and has changed multiple times in American history), but politically toxic. The Biden administration's Supreme Court Reform Commission declined to recommend expansion. The political obstacles are real. But if the Court continues on its current trajectory — actively dismantling democratic rights while claiming to be neutral arbiters of law — expansion may become the only effective remedy.

**Jurisdiction-stripping.** Congress can, under Article III of the Constitution, define the appellate jurisdiction of the Supreme Court (though not its original jurisdiction). Congress could theoretically remove certain categories of election law cases from the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction, though the constitutional permissibility of broad jurisdiction-stripping is contested.

**Supermajority requirements.** Legislation requiring a supermajority (say, 6 of 9 justices) to strike down an act of Congress in the area of voting rights or civil rights has been proposed. Whether this is constitutional is uncertain.

None of these options is simple or politically easy. But the systematic demolition of voting rights protections by a six-justice conservative supermajority appointed by presidents who lost the popular vote, confirmed by senators representing a minority of the American population, requires a response commensurate with the scale of the problem.

### 12.4 The Role of States: What Can Be Done Now

Even before Congress acts, there are things that can be done at the state level.

Several states have already adopted ranked choice voting for some elections. Maine uses it for congressional elections. Alaska uses it for all elections. The District of Columbia adopted it in 2024. Several cities — including Portland, Oregon; Cambridge, Massachusetts; and numerous others — use ranked choice voting for local elections.

States can also reform their own legislative district systems. Some states use at-large multi-member districts for state legislative elections in certain contexts. Oregon, whose Portland city council already uses STV for multi-member wards, could expand this system.

State constitutional amendments can provide stronger voting rights protections than the federal floor that *Callais* has now lowered dramatically. New Mexico, Colorado, and several other states have state constitutional provisions that offer more expansive voting rights protections.

And state courts, operating under state constitutional law rather than federal constitutional law, are not bound by *Callais*. The Supreme Court's opinion in *Callais* concerns federal constitutional law (the Equal Protection Clause) and federal statutory law (the VRA). State courts interpreting state constitutional provisions — such as Pennsylvania's redistricting jurisprudence, or various state ERAs and equal protection provisions — can provide stronger protections.

None of these state-level options provides a comprehensive national solution. But they matter. They provide laboratories of democracy in which proportional systems can be tested and refined, building political support and institutional capacity for the national reform.

### 12.5 The Civil Rights Movement That Is Now Required

The *Callais* decision is not an ending. It is an inflection point. The systematic dismantling of the Voting Rights Act by the Roberts Court — from *Shelby County* through *Brnovich* to *Callais* — has not made the problem of racial inequality in American politics go away. It has made it worse. And it has made the inadequacy of litigation-based, district-by-district remediation undeniable.

What is needed now is what was needed in 1965: a mass movement demanding structural legislative reform, supported by the kind of moral clarity that can move the political system even when the legal system has failed. The difference between 1965 and 2026 is that in 1965, the demand was to implement the Fifteenth Amendment — to actually enforce what was already in the Constitution. In 2026, the demand must go further: to structurally redesign the system in which elections take place so that gerrymandering becomes mathematically impossible.

The demand is concrete: statewide multi-member districts for every state, elected by the Single Transferable Vote. The constitutional authority is explicit: Article I, Section 4. The bill is already introduced in Congress: the Fair Representation Act (H.R. 4632). The evidence that it works is overwhelming: Ireland, Australia, Scotland, Northern Ireland, and dozens of other jurisdictions have used proportional representation for decades with consistently better outcomes for minority representation and democratic responsiveness.

What is required now is the will to demand it. The *Callais* decision has, in a single ruling, made the stakes unmistakable. A quarter or more of the Congressional Black Caucus could lose their seats. A generation of hard-won minority representation could be erased. The halls of Congress, state legislatures, and local government bodies could be bleached of color.

Or Congress could act. It could pass the Fair Representation Act. It could go further and mandate statewide multi-member districts. It could restore, by statute, the protections that the Roberts Court has demolished.

The Constitution says Congress can. History says Congress must.

## Part 13: The Broader Democratic Crisis and the Urgency of Now

### 13.1 The 2026 Elections: An Uncertain and Dangerous Moment

The *Callais* decision came on April 29, 2026 — less than six months before the November 2026 midterm elections. The immediate electoral implications are uncertain but potentially significant.

In many states, the primary election calendar is already underway or nearly complete by the time *Callais* was decided. Completely redrawing congressional maps in response to the ruling before the November elections would be extraordinarily disruptive — it would require new qualifying periods, potentially new primaries, reconfigured campaigns, and enormous voter confusion.

Kyle Kondik, the elections analyst and managing editor of Sabato's Crystal Ball, was candid about this uncertainty in the immediate aftermath of the ruling: "2025–2026 redistricting has taught us that things that don't seem possible can become possible. We're just going to have to take it on a state-by-state basis as reactions to this ruling come." He added that the redistricting battle could stretch into 2027 and 2028.

Tennessee Republican Senator Marsha Blackburn's post-ruling call for the state legislature to reconvene and redistrict to create "another Republican seat in Memphis" illustrates exactly what *Callais* enables: the targeted dismantling of Black-majority or Black-opportunity districts under the cover of partisan motivation. Memphis, Tennessee has a majority-Black population. Tennessee Republican legislators will now be free to crack Memphis's Black voters across multiple suburban and rural Republican-leaning districts, eliminating the effective Black-majority district in western Tennessee, and will be able to defend this action under *Callais* by claiming it was done to maximize Republican partisan advantage, not to dilute Black voting power — even though those are precisely the same thing in Tennessee.

### 13.2 Florida and the Immediate 2026 Battleground

Florida is perhaps the most consequential immediate battleground for the post-*Callais* redistricting landscape. Florida has 28 congressional districts and is one of the states that was still engaged in redistricting disputes for the 2026 elections cycle at the time of the *Callais* ruling.

Florida's Republican-controlled state government under Governor Ron DeSantis had already been aggressive in redistricting, drawing maps that were challenged as racial gerrymanders for eliminating a Black-majority district in northern Florida. Those challenges, which had been proceeding through the courts under Section 2 of the VRA and the Florida Constitution, are now dramatically complicated by *Callais*.

Florida has a state constitutional provision (passed by Florida voters in 2010 as Amendment 6) that explicitly prohibits drawing districts that "diminish minority voters' ability to elect representatives of their choice." This state constitutional provision is separate from Section 2 of the VRA and is not directly affected by *Callais*. Florida courts interpreting the state constitution may be able to provide some protection that the federal VRA can no longer provide.

But the political dynamics in Florida — with a governor and legislature committed to maximizing Republican representation and a Republican-appointed federal judiciary that has shown sympathy for Republican redistricting claims — make the outcome deeply uncertain.

### 13.3 The Long View: From Bleeding to Winning

It would be dishonest to end this article without acknowledging the depth of the crisis and the difficulty of the path ahead. The Roberts Court has accomplished something remarkable in its systematic dismantling of the Voting Rights Act: it has, over the course of 25 years (from Roberts' first year on the Court in 2005 to the *Callais* decision in 2026), reduced the most powerful civil rights statute in American history to a largely ceremonial document. The mechanisms for enforcing minority voting rights in the redistricting context are, for now, essentially gone.

The immediate task is damage control: state courts applying state constitutional provisions, voting rights organizations building Section 2 cases using whatever scraps remain under *Callais*, civic organizations working to register and mobilize voters in communities that will be targeted by post-*Callais* remapping.

The medium-term task is political: electing politicians who are committed to structural electoral reform, building the public understanding and political will for the Fair Representation Act or a statewide multi-member district statute, and making the case — in churches, in schools, in living rooms, in editorial pages, in town halls — for why the current system is broken and what a better system looks like.

The long-term task is transformational: restructuring American democracy so that it actually reflects what the Constitution promises — equal protection for all, a government deriving its just powers from the consent of the governed, representation proportional to the actual preferences of the electorate.

The failure of the *Callais* decision is not that it is legally wrong (though it is). It is that it is morally wrong. It is wrong that a country that prides itself on being a democracy uses electoral systems specifically designed to ensure that certain communities of people cannot elect representatives of their choice. It is wrong that the political process has been captured by a minority of voters who, through the manipulation of district lines, maintain power regardless of what the majority of the electorate wants. It is wrong that the same Court that killed Section 5 by promising that Section 2 would protect minority voters has now killed Section 2 with the implication that there is nothing left to protect them.

The people who are harmed by this system are real. They are the Black voters of Alabama who have been represented by the same single Black-majority district since the 1970s despite making up 27% of the population. They are the Native American voters of Montana who have never sent a Native American to Congress in the state's history. They are the Latino voters of Florida whose communities are cracked and packed across districts drawn to ensure Republican dominance. They are the Asian-American voters of Texas and Virginia who lack proportional representation in the state legislatures that make decisions about their lives.

They are us, or they are our neighbors. And they deserve better. The path to better is clear: statewide multi-member districts, Single Transferable Vote, and a Congress with the will to mandate them.

## Conclusion: The Arithmetic of Justice

Here is a simple truth about democracy: it doesn't work if votes don't count.

The United States has spent two and a half centuries building a democracy that doesn't quite work — that has, at every stage, found ways to ensure that certain voters' votes count less, that certain communities' preferences are systematically ignored, that the rules of the game are drawn to benefit the powerful at the expense of the rest.

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 was the most powerful legislative response to this failure in American history. It did not cure the disease, but it provided real protection. It enabled the election of thousands of minority officials across fifty states. It created space for communities that had been denied representation for a century to finally have a voice in the decisions that affected their lives. It was imperfect, but it was real.

The Roberts Court has spent 25 years methodically destroying it, ruling by ruling, doctrinal shift by doctrinal shift, until on the morning of April 29, 2026, it delivered the final blow. Section 2, "all but a dead letter."

The answer is not more litigation. The litigation pathway is closed. The answer is not better candidates or better organizing within the current system. The current system is designed to nullify organizing. The answer is structural. It is legislative. It is constitutional in its authorization, democratic in its purpose, and arithmetic in its operation.

Every state in the United States should constitute a single congressional district, electing all of its Representatives by the Single Transferable Vote. In such a system, every community — Black, Latino, Asian-American, Native American, white rural, white suburban, urban progressive — would have representation proportional to its actual numbers, guaranteed by mathematics rather than litigation, by structure rather than judicial enforcement.

The Droop quota does not care whether you are Black or white. It does not care whether you live in Memphis or in the suburbs of Shelby County. It does not care whether the state legislature likes your politics. If your community is large enough to clear the threshold — and every major community in every major state is — you elect a representative of your choice. Full stop.

Congress must act. The Constitution says it can. History says it must. The mathematics says it will work.

The only remaining question is whether the political will exists. And the only way to create political will is to demand it: in elections, in organizing, in the streets, and in the halls of Congress.

Voting rights are not a gift. They have never been a gift. They have been won, and lost, and won again, in one of the longest and most painful struggles in human history. The *Callais* decision is not the end of that struggle. It is a chapter in it. And the chapter that must follow — the one that we must write — is the chapter in which Congress finally, definitively, builds a democratic system that cannot be gerrymandered, cannot be litigated to death, and cannot be dismantled by six unelected justices serving the interests of the party that appointed them.

A statewide multi-member district for every state. Ranked choice voting by the Single Transferable Vote. The arithmetic of justice.
