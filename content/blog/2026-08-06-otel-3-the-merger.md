## Part 1: Two Answers to the Same Question

Yesterday's post ended with the industry facing a genuine, expensive problem: a landscape of tracing and metrics tools, both open-source and commercial, that mostly refused to speak to one another, forcing any organization that wanted to switch vendors or backends to go back through its own code and rewrite how that code reported on itself. By the middle of the 2010s, two separate efforts had emerged, independently, to fix exactly this problem — and, in one of the more instructive ironies in recent open-source history, the fix itself briefly became a second version of the same problem, before the two fixes merged into each other and became the single subject of this entire series.

This post tells that story precisely: who started each project, what each one actually offered, why having two of them was worse than having either one alone, and the exact sequence of events in 2019 that ended with a single, unified project called OpenTelemetry. We will also do something this series intends to do consistently whenever people and companies with genuine, sometimes competing commercial interests make a joint decision: follow the incentives, honestly, for each party involved, rather than simply repeating the version of the story that reads as an uncomplicated tale of open-source altruism.

## Part 2: OpenTracing, Born From Dapper's Lineage

Yesterday's post introduced Benjamin H. Sigelman as the lead author of Google's 2010 Dapper paper, the document that first described the modern shape of a distributed trace to the outside world. In February 2015, Sigelman left Google. Not long after, he co-founded a company called LightStep, built around commercial distributed tracing, putting him in an interesting position: he now ran a business that would benefit enormously if distributed tracing became a widespread, standard practice across the industry, rather than something confined to a handful of large companies that had each built their own internal Dapper-style tooling.

In November 2015, an effort called OpenTracing began, associated with Sigelman and LightStep, aimed at solving one specific, narrower problem: defining a standard application programming interface — a fixed, agreed set of function calls a piece of code could use to describe "start a span here, attach this attribute to it, end it there" — without dictating anything about what should happen to that data afterward, or which specific backend should ultimately store and display it. This was a deliberately narrow, API-only approach, and the reasoning behind it is worth stating precisely: if a library author, building something like a database driver or an HTTP client that many different applications would depend on, wanted to add built-in tracing support, they needed a way to call "start a span" that would not force every single downstream application using their library to also adopt one specific tracing vendor's product. An API-only standard, with no default implementation baked in, let library authors trace their own code once, while leaving the actual choice of tracing backend up to whoever eventually assembled a full application out of that library.

The Cloud Native Computing Foundation accepted OpenTracing as its third hosted project in October 2016, following Kubernetes and Prometheus, and OpenTracing reached its own 1.0 specification release two months later, in December 2016. Around the same period, in October 2016, Uber publicly released Jaeger, the tracing system introduced in yesterday's post, built with native, first-class support for the OpenTracing API from the outset — giving OpenTracing an early, credible, large-scale real-world implementation to point to, which matters enormously for any standard trying to establish itself: a specification with no serious implementation behind it is mostly just a document.

## Part 3: OpenCensus, Google's Broader Answer

Independently, and starting slightly later, Google pursued a related but meaningfully different approach, rooted in a piece of internal infrastructure called Census. Census was Google's own internal instrumentation framework — the plumbing Google's engineers had built and refined over years to automatically capture traces and application-level metrics across the company's vast internal collection of services, largely without individual engineering teams needing to think about it, because services built on Google's internal networking and remote-procedure-call systems got this instrumentation essentially for free as a side effect of using those systems at all.

Google opened this internal work to the public as OpenCensus, released in early 2017. The specific business reasoning Google gave, in its own public statements at the time, is worth stating plainly, because it illustrates the theme this post keeps returning to: OpenCensus was not released purely as an act of open-source generosity, though Google did also describe a genuine institutional culture of open-sourcing internal tools. Google's own engineering blog, in a post explaining "the value of OpenCensus," pointed to a very concrete cost problem: the team behind Google's commercial Stackdriver Trace product had been spending significant, ongoing engineering effort building and maintaining separate instrumentation libraries across seven different programming languages, purely so that Stackdriver's customers, writing code in any of those languages, could send trace data to it. Sharing that underlying instrumentation work as an open, shared project — one that other backends, not just Stackdriver, could also plug into — meant Google no longer bore that entire maintenance cost alone, and meant Google's own customers could adopt tracing more easily even in situations where Stackdriver was not the final destination.

OpenCensus was led on the product side by Morgan McLean, described in Google's own materials at the time as the product manager for tracing, debugging, and profiling at Google. Structurally, OpenCensus made a different design choice than OpenTracing: rather than being purely an API with no implementation, OpenCensus shipped both an API and a working, ready-to-use implementation together, along with actual exporters — the pieces of code responsible for sending collected data onward to a specific backend — for systems including Prometheus, Zipkin, and Google's own Stackdriver. This "batteries included" approach lowered the effort required to get started, at the cost of being a somewhat heavier, more opinionated piece of software to adopt than OpenTracing's minimal API-only footprint.

A detail with real significance for later parts of this series: Microsoft formally joined the OpenCensus project as a contributor. This was Microsoft's first serious institutional involvement in this entire lineage of standards efforts, and it will reappear directly in Part 5 of this series, when we examine Microsoft's own journey away from its proprietary Application Insights software development kit and toward OpenTelemetry as the recommended path for new applications on Azure.

## Part 4: Why Two Standards Was Worse Than One

By 2018, the industry had, in effect, replaced one large fragmentation problem with a smaller but structurally identical one. Instead of dozens of incompatible proprietary vendor SDKs, there were now two competing, well-funded, genuinely well-designed open standards, each with real corporate backing, each solving a version of the same underlying problem, and each incompatible with the other in ways that mattered in practice.

Consider the position of an author maintaining a popular open-source library — a database driver, a web framework, a message-queue client — used by thousands of downstream applications. Adding tracing support to that library meaningfully helped every application that depended on it, but which standard should the library author choose? Instrumenting for OpenTracing meant applications using OpenCensus would not automatically benefit, and vice versa. Instrumenting for both meant carrying two separate sets of instrumentation code, testing both, and maintaining both indefinitely — a meaningfully larger and more annoying burden than instrumenting for one standard would have been, let alone for zero.

The people best positioned to see this problem clearly, and with the strongest incentive to want it solved, were exactly the library authors and the observability vendors sitting in the middle of this ecosystem — companies like Datadog, Splunk, Dynatrace, Honeycomb, and LightStep itself, each of whom, in an ideal world from their own commercial perspective, wanted to receive data from as many instrumented applications as possible, regardless of which upstream standard those applications had happened to adopt. A world with two competing standards was, for a backend vendor, close to the worst of both: still meaningfully fragmented, but now fragmented in a way that looked, from the outside, embarrassingly similar to the exact vendor lock-in problem the standards themselves had been created to solve.

## Part 5: The Merger, Precisely Dated

Discussions about unifying the two projects began within both communities during 2018, and by early 2019 those discussions had produced a concrete public plan. The two projects' own governing committees jointly announced that OpenTracing and OpenCensus would merge into a single new project. The framing used at the time, by people involved on both sides including Sigelman and McLean, was carefully chosen: the new project was described not as replacing either predecessor from scratch, but as functioning as the next major version of both of them at once — an explicit promise that existing OpenTracing and OpenCensus users would not simply be abandoned, but would have a clear, supported migration path into the unified project, typically through compatibility "bridge" software that let older instrumentation continue functioning while a codebase gradually migrated.

The new, merged project was named OpenTelemetry. Its first commit to a public source-code repository was made in April 2019. The project formally joined the Cloud Native Computing Foundation as a sandbox-level project — the CNCF's entry tier for new, unproven projects — on May 17, 2019. In parallel, both predecessor projects moved into what their own maintainers called a "read-only" or maintenance-only state during the second half of 2019, meaning no significant new development would continue on either OpenTracing or OpenCensus separately going forward; all future effort was to be directed into OpenTelemetry instead. OpenTracing was subsequently formally archived by the CNCF's technical oversight body.

It is worth being precise about a phrase used earlier in this post, because it recurs constantly throughout this series and deserves its first careful application right here: announced is not the same as implemented, which is not the same as stable, which is not the same as adopted. The 2019 merger announcement was a governance and intent decision — an agreement, by the people controlling both projects, about the direction all future work would take. It did not, on its own, instantly produce a finished, production-ready replacement for either OpenTracing or OpenCensus; that took years of subsequent engineering work across many programming languages, some of which is still ongoing as this series is being written, and some of which we will examine honestly, including its remaining rough edges, throughout the following seventeen posts.

## Part 6: Following the Motivations, Honestly

This series has already committed, more than once, to examining the actual incentives behind claims of cooperation rather than accepting a tidy narrative of pure altruism, and the 2019 merger is an excellent case study in doing exactly that, because nearly every party involved had a real, distinct, and entirely reasonable self-interested reason to want it to succeed, and none of those reasons need to be treated as scandalous simply for being self-interested.

Google's cloud business depends on customers being willing to run workloads on Google Cloud rather than being frightened away by the prospect of lock-in to any single cloud provider's proprietary tooling; a genuinely portable, vendor-neutral telemetry standard that works equally well across Google Cloud, AWS, and Azure reduces exactly that fear, and correspondingly makes customers more comfortable adopting Google Cloud services in the first place, since leaving later would not mean rewriting their entire observability layer. Microsoft's motivations for engagement, first through OpenCensus and then through OpenTelemetry, follow a closely related logic on Azure's behalf, and we will trace Microsoft's specific journey — including the eventual, formally announced deprecation of its own older proprietary telemetry SDK — in full in Part 5 of this series.

Observability vendors such as Splunk, Datadog, Dynatrace, Honeycomb, and LightStep itself benefited from a different angle: a shared, neutral instrumentation standard effectively outsources and commoditizes the expensive, unglamorous work of getting telemetry data out of applications in the first place, work that every vendor had previously been forced to duplicate independently in their own proprietary agents. With that groundwork shared and standardized, each vendor could redirect its own engineering effort toward the part of the business that actually differentiates one vendor from another and that customers are willing to pay for: the storage, analysis, visualization, and alerting layered on top of the data, rather than the comparatively undifferentiated plumbing that gets the data there in the first place.

And ordinary end-user organizations — the companies actually running the software being observed, with no stake in the observability tooling market itself — had perhaps the simplest and least complicated motivation of all: a genuine, straightforward desire to stop being locked into whichever vendor happened to have the market's largest sales team on the day they first adopted a monitoring tool, and to gain the ability to switch backends, or use several at once, without redoing years of accumulated instrumentation work across every service they operated.

None of this is a story about pure self-sacrifice, and this series does not think it needs to be one for the outcome to be genuinely good for the industry. Standards succeed, historically, far more often when every major party involved can see a clear, honest benefit to themselves in cooperating than when cooperation is expected to rest on goodwill alone, and the 2019 merger is a good illustration of a standard whose cooperative incentives were unusually well aligned across nearly every relevant party at once.

## Part 7: From Merger to Graduation — the Path to 2026

The merger in 2019 was, by design, only the beginning. The Cloud Native Computing Foundation's project maturity model has three tiers — sandbox, incubating, and graduated — each representing an increasing level of demonstrated adoption, governance rigor, and technical maturity, checked periodically by the foundation's technical oversight committee. OpenTelemetry advanced from the sandbox tier to the incubating tier on August 26, 2021, roughly two years after its initial sandbox acceptance, a period during which the project built out API and software-development-kit support across roughly a dozen programming languages and, notably, brought full support for both tracing and metrics up to a stable specification level for the first time.

OpenTelemetry reached the CNCF's highest tier, graduation, on May 11, 2026, with the CNCF's public announcement following on May 21, 2026, at that year's Observability Summit in Minneapolis — an event, worth noting explicitly, that occurred only a few months before this very post was written, which is precisely why this series treats OpenTelemetry's current governance and maturity status, covered in full in Part 20, as a live and still-developing story rather than settled history. By the time of graduation, the CNCF reported more than twelve thousand contributors from more than twenty-eight hundred distinct companies had participated in the project, and noted that OpenTelemetry had achieved the second-highest rate of ongoing development activity of any project the CNCF hosts, trailing only Kubernetes itself among more than two hundred and forty hosted projects. Graduation, per the CNCF's own stated process, required a completed third-party independent security audit covering core project components including the OpenTelemetry Collector — a piece of infrastructure this series covers in full in Part 13 — along with a formal governance review confirming the project's decision-making processes had matured sufficiently to be trusted at the foundation's highest tier.

## Part 8: How to Spot the Lie — Reading a Foundation Press Release

Today's test applies specifically to announcements like the CNCF's graduation announcement just described, and more broadly to any press release issued by a nonprofit foundation about a project it hosts, because these documents occupy an unusual position: they are not vendor marketing in the strict sense, since the CNCF does not sell OpenTelemetry and has no direct profit motive tied to the announcement's specific wording, but they are also not neutral academic assessments, since a foundation's own institutional interest lies in its hosted projects looking successful, which reflects well on the foundation's own judgment in accepting and nurturing them in the first place.

Start by asking precisely what a milestone like "graduation" actually certifies, according to the foundation's own published criteria, rather than what the surrounding celebratory language implies. Graduation, in the CNCF's own stated process, certifies specific, checkable things: a completed security audit of named components, evidence of adoption by a documented number of organizations, and a formal review of the project's governance structure. It does not certify, and cannot certify, things the announcement's tone might nonetheless suggest to an inattentive reader — that every language implementation is equally mature, that every feature is production-ready everywhere, or that the project's funding and contributor base are guaranteed to remain stable indefinitely into the future. We will return to exactly this distinction, applied specifically to which OpenTelemetry signals are and are not fully mature as of this writing, in Part 4 tomorrow.

Second, treat large contributor and company counts with the same care you would apply to any large number presented without a clear denominator. A figure like twelve thousand contributors is genuinely impressive and genuinely indicates broad participation, but it does not, by itself, distinguish between a person who has merged hundreds of substantial pull requests over years and a person who has made one small documentation fix; open-source contribution statistics of this kind virtually always follow a heavily skewed distribution, where a comparatively small number of maintainers do the large majority of the deep, architecturally significant work, while a much larger number of occasional contributors make smaller, valuable but less central contributions. Neither category is unimportant, but they answer different questions, and a headline number rarely tells you which mix you are actually looking at.

Finally, and most usefully as a durable habit: apply the same three-part standard-verification test introduced in yesterday's post — a public specification independent of any one vendor, multiple genuinely independent implementations, and neutral governance not controlled by a single company — directly to OpenTelemetry itself, rather than simply trusting that a CNCF-hosted project must automatically satisfy it. As it happens, and as the following seventeen posts in this series will demonstrate in specific technical detail, OpenTelemetry does genuinely satisfy all three criteria reasonably well. But the point of this test is that you should be able, and willing, to check that for yourself against the evidence, rather than accepting it purely on the strength of an impressive-sounding press release — including this one, and including everything else this series tells you going forward.

## Part 9: What Comes Tomorrow

We have now covered where OpenTelemetry came from and who built the coalition that created it. Tomorrow's post turns to a question we have referenced constantly without yet answering with full technical precision: what, exactly, is OpenTelemetry, component by component? We will draw a sharp, careful line around what the project actually is — a specification, a set of APIs and software-development kits, an intermediary piece of infrastructure called the Collector, a shared wire protocol, and a dictionary of agreed naming conventions — and, just as importantly, around what it deliberately is not, including the single most common misconception newcomers to this field bring with them: the belief that OpenTelemetry is itself a monitoring dashboard or a place data gets stored, which it categorically is not.
