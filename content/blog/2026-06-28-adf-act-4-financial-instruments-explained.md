## Part 1: Why We Need to Understand Financial Instruments

The Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 does something that much financial legislation does not do well: it lists, by name, the specific financial instruments that the new fund is authorized to use. Section 3 of the Act specifies eight categories of instruments. Understanding each of them — not as abstract financial jargon, but as concrete mechanisms for moving money from savers and investors into infrastructure projects — is essential to understanding what the law is actually trying to do.

Before we go through the instruments one by one, let us establish the basic framework that all of them share.

### The Fundamental Problem: Getting Money from Those Who Have It to Those Who Need It

Every financial instrument, no matter how complex, solves the same basic problem: connecting people who have money they want to invest with activities that need money to get done.

On one side of this transaction are savers and investors. These might be:
- Ordinary Nepali citizens who have money in a bank account or savings
- Large institutional investors — pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds — that hold money on behalf of thousands or millions of beneficiaries
- Foreign investors looking for opportunities
- Nepali workers abroad (the diaspora) who earn money and want to invest it back home

On the other side are projects that need money:
- A hydropower plant that will generate electricity
- A highway that will connect a remote region to markets
- An airport that will make a region accessible to commerce and tourism
- An industrial park that will attract manufacturing businesses

The challenge is getting the money from the first group to the second group in a way that:
- Gives investors sufficient confidence that they will get their money back (plus some return)
- Structures the risk appropriately (not all investors can afford or are willing to absorb the same level of risk)
- Provides the project with money at terms it can sustain (if a power plant must pay 20% annual interest on its financing, it will never be profitable)
- Aligns incentives so that project operators are motivated to perform well

Different financial instruments achieve this in different ways. Let us go through each one.

## Part 2: Bonds — Borrowing from a Crowd

### What a Bond Is

A bond is one of the simplest financial instruments to understand at its core, even though bond markets can become extraordinarily complex.

Here is the basic idea: suppose you need to borrow Rs. 10 billion (roughly $75 million) to build a power plant. You could borrow this from a single bank. But banks may not want to lend that much to a single project, or their interest rates might be too high. An alternative is to borrow from many people at once by issuing bonds.

When you issue a bond, you are creating a piece of paper (or more accurately these days, an electronic record) that says: "I, the issuer, promise to pay the bondholder a fixed amount of interest every year for a specified number of years (say, ten years), and then to return the original amount at the end." The bond specifies the face value (the original amount), the interest rate (called the "coupon"), and the maturity date (when the original amount is returned).

A bondholder is a lender. They are not buying a share of ownership in the project; they are lending money to the project on defined terms. The project developer is the borrower. The contract is the bond itself.

### Why Bonds Are Useful for Infrastructure

Bonds are particularly well-suited to infrastructure financing for several reasons.

First, infrastructure projects generate predictable, steady cash flows over long periods. A hydropower plant, once built, generates revenue from electricity sales for 30, 40, or 50 years. Bondholders receive fixed interest payments — they want predictability, not excitement. The steady cash flow from infrastructure matches the bond's steady payment obligations.

Second, bonds can be sold in the secondary market — meaning a bondholder who needs their money before the bond matures can sell the bond to another investor. This liquidity makes bonds more attractive to investors who are otherwise reluctant to lock up money for 10 or 20 years.

Third, bonds can be issued in small denominations, allowing many investors — including retail investors, not just large institutions — to participate. A Rs. 10 billion bond issuance could be divided into 100,000 bonds of Rs. 100,000 each, making it accessible to middle-class Nepali savers.

### Types of Bonds in the Act

The Act references several specific types of bonds:

**Debentures (Rinpatra):** In Nepali financial law, "rinpatra" (literally "debt instrument/paper") is the general term for debt securities issued by companies and institutions, equivalent to what are commonly called bonds or debentures in English. The Act authorizes the fund to issue such instruments to raise money from the public or institutional investors, and to use the proceeds to finance infrastructure projects.

**Government-guaranteed bonds:** When the government guarantees a bond, it is promising that if the issuer (in this case, the fund) cannot make its interest or principal payments, the government will step in and make them instead. This guarantee dramatically reduces the risk for bondholders, which means the issuer can borrow at lower interest rates. The Act specifically provides that bonds issued by the fund with Nepal government backing will be treated the same as government development bonds or citizens' savings bonds for regulatory and trading purposes — a provision that effectively gives them the highest quality rating in the Nepali market.

**Project-specific bonds:** These are bonds issued specifically for a single infrastructure project — "this bond will finance the construction of the Sunkoshi Power Plant," for example. The bond is repaid from the revenues of that specific project. This is sometimes called project finance, and it is a distinct discipline with its own valuation methodologies, risk analysis frameworks, and legal documentation requirements.

**Green bonds:** Green bonds are a subset of bonds where the issuer commits to use the proceeds only for environmentally beneficial projects — renewable energy, clean water, sustainable transport, climate adaptation. The Act specifically references green bonds and mandates that Nepal Rastra Bank (the central bank) will classify "green sector projects" — explicitly including renewable energy generation and transmission, urban infrastructure, and climate adaptation projects. The Act also provides that the government can offer special tax incentives and concessions to investors in bonds issued for green sector projects.

Green bonds have grown from an obscure instrument to a major global market since the European Investment Bank issued the first "Climate Awareness Bond" in 2007. As of recent years, the global green bond market has grown to hundreds of billions of dollars annually. By issuing green bonds, the fund could access international investors who specifically allocate capital to sustainable investments and who might not otherwise consider Nepal-focused bonds.

### How to Think About Bond Risk

Not all bonds carry the same risk. The key risk for a bondholder is default risk — the risk that the issuer will not be able to make interest or principal payments. Default risk is related to:
- The financial strength of the issuer
- The quality and viability of the underlying project (for project bonds)
- Whether there is a government guarantee
- The broader economic and political stability of the country

Credit rating agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch internationally; Nepali agencies domestically) assess default risk and assign ratings that guide investors. A highly rated bond (triple-A) has very low default risk and pays a low interest rate. A lower-rated bond pays a higher interest rate to compensate investors for higher risk.

For the Alternative Development Finance Fund's bonds to succeed in attracting investment, they must either have government guarantees (which transfers default risk to the government), or demonstrate sufficient financial strength and project quality to earn credible ratings that attract investors without requiring full government backstop.

## Part 3: Equity — Owning a Share

### What Equity Is

Equity is ownership. When you buy equity (stock or shares) in a company or a project, you are buying a share of ownership in that entity. You are not a lender; you are an owner. This distinction has profound implications.

As an owner, you have a residual claim on the value of the entity — after all debts and other obligations are paid. If the entity does well and generates profits, those profits belong to equity holders, proportional to their ownership share. This can generate high returns: if you own 10% of a power plant that generates Rs. 1 billion in annual profit, you receive Rs. 100 million per year.

But as an owner, you also bear the downside. If the entity loses money or becomes insolvent, equity holders are the last in line. Lenders (bondholders, banks) get paid first from the remaining assets. Equity holders get what is left — which could be nothing.

Equity investment in infrastructure is therefore inherently riskier than lending to infrastructure. Equity investors demand higher expected returns to compensate for this higher risk. But equity also has an advantage: unlike loans that must be repaid on a fixed schedule, equity does not create a repayment obligation. A project that struggles financially does not default on its equity; it simply pays a lower dividend (or no dividend at all). This makes equity more flexible for project finance than debt.

### How the Fund Uses Equity

The Act authorizes the fund to acquire equity — to buy shares — in infrastructure projects. This can happen in several ways:

**Direct equity investment:** The fund buys shares in a project company (a company set up specifically to build and operate a single infrastructure project — called a "Special Purpose Vehicle" or SPV in project finance). By owning shares, the fund becomes a part-owner of the project and earns returns through dividends and capital appreciation.

**Equity fund establishment:** The fund can create separate equity funds for specific sectors or purposes. Domestic and foreign investors can invest in these equity funds, which then invest in portfolios of infrastructure projects. This is more efficient than investing in individual projects, as it provides diversification.

**Equity conversion:** The Act explicitly authorizes the fund to convert a loan investment into an equity investment. This is a standard tool in development finance — if a project is struggling to service its debt but the underlying business is fundamentally viable, converting some or all of the debt to equity reduces the immediate cash burden, gives the lender (now equity holder) potential upside if the project recovers, and avoids a formal default.

### What Equity Investment Means for Risk Allocation

When the fund takes equity in a project, the risk allocation changes significantly compared to lending. As an equity holder, the fund bears downside risk — if the project fails, the fund loses its equity investment. As an equity holder, the fund is also represented in the project's governance — it has rights to information, to attend board meetings, and to vote on major decisions. This governance role is valuable, as it allows the fund to monitor projects it has invested in and to intervene if management goes off track.

The equity model also has implications for government risk. If the fund's equity investments perform badly, the fund's capital base erodes, which may ultimately require the government to recapitalize it — meaning taxpayers bear the loss. This is one of the reasons the Act's provisions on project analysis, risk management, and governance are so important: they are the first line of defense against equity losses.

## Part 4: Hybrid/Blended Instruments

### What Blended Finance Is

The Act authorizes what it calls "sammilit bitta upakaran" — blended or combined financial instruments — that incorporate elements of both debt and equity. This is the concept known in international development finance as "blended finance": structuring a single investment so that different layers of capital bear different levels of risk and receive different levels of return.

The classic blended finance structure works as follows:

**First loss layer (equity/grant):** A public institution (like the new fund), a government grant, or a philanthropy takes the riskiest slice of the investment. This layer absorbs the first losses if the project underperforms. Because this layer is taking the risk that commercial investors find most frightening, its presence makes the rest of the investment structure safer.

**Mezzanine layer (subordinated debt):** A moderately risk-tolerant investor — perhaps a development finance institution or a patient impact investor — takes a middle layer. This layer is paid before the equity layer but after senior debt in the event of a problem.

**Senior debt layer (commercial loans, bonds):** Commercial banks, pension funds, or bond investors take the safest layer. They are paid first from project revenues. They bear very little risk because the layers below them absorb losses first.

The key insight is that a small amount of first-loss capital from a public source can "crowd in" a much larger amount of commercial capital. If the first-loss layer represents 10% of total project cost, and it convinces commercial investors to provide the other 90%, then every rupee of public first-loss capital mobilizes nine rupees of private capital. This leverage effect is the central argument for public development finance.

### Asset Monetization

The Act also authorizes "sampattwi maudrikikaran" — asset monetization. This refers to the process of taking an existing public asset (such as an operating road, a functioning power distribution network, or an airport) and using it as the basis for raising finance.

Here is a simple example of how this works. Suppose the Nepal government owns a toll road that generates Rs. 500 million in annual toll revenues. The government needs money for other projects. It could "monetize" the toll road by creating a special purpose vehicle that owns the right to collect those tolls for, say, 30 years. It then issues bonds backed by those future toll revenues, raising Rs. 10 billion today. Investors receive the toll revenues over 30 years as repayment. The government gets Rs. 10 billion immediately to invest elsewhere.

Asset monetization is common in India (the National Monetization Pipeline), the United Kingdom (numerous PFI-era structures), and many other countries. It is not privatization — the government does not sell the underlying asset permanently, only the revenue stream for a defined period. But it does involve locking in future government revenues to service private debt, which creates its own risks: if toll revenues are lower than expected, the government may need to make up the difference. Asset monetization can also reduce the government's flexibility to change toll rates or other terms of the asset's operation.

## Part 5: Remittance Investment Funds

### Nepal's Remittance Economy

We mentioned in the first article that remittances are Nepal's most important source of external income. Let us be more precise. According to Nepal Rastra Bank data, remittance inflows in recent years have been approximately Rs. 1.1 to 1.4 trillion per year — equivalent to perhaps $8-10 billion annually. This money comes primarily from Nepali workers in Gulf countries, Malaysia, South Korea, Japan, and elsewhere. It is sent home to families who use it for consumption, housing, education, and healthcare.

Remittances are a form of private financial transfer, not investment. The workers who send money home are, in most cases, simply providing for their families. The money arrives in family bank accounts and is spent on immediate needs. Very little of it is channeled into productive investment — into businesses, into savings that fund long-term infrastructure, or into financial instruments.

### What a Remittance Investment Fund Does

The Act authorizes the establishment of a "Bipreshan Kosh" (remittance fund) — a structured investment vehicle specifically designed to capture some of remittance income as investment. The idea is to create an investment product that:

- Is accessible to Nepali workers abroad, who can invest a portion of their earnings rather than sending it all to family accounts
- Offers these workers an attractive return, better than what they would get from foreign bank accounts
- Channels the invested money into Nepali infrastructure projects that generate employment, economic growth, and social benefits in Nepal
- Potentially provides some government incentive (tax benefit, currency guarantee) to make the product more attractive

Remittance investment bonds and funds have been tried in other countries. India has issued "diaspora bonds" — the Resurgent India Bonds (1998) and India Millennium Deposits (2000) — that raised billions of dollars from the Indian diaspora at times of currency stress. Israel has operated a diaspora bond program (State of Israel Bonds) continuously since 1951, raising tens of billions of dollars from Jewish communities worldwide for Israeli development. Ethiopia has had remittance investment programs, with mixed success.

The model can work, but it requires several things: investor confidence in the issuing institution, attractive and credible returns, a reliable mechanism for participation (accessible to workers in Gulf countries who may have limited banking access), and a clear, credible use of proceeds.

## Part 6: Guarantee Mechanisms

### What a Guarantee Is

A guarantee is a promise by one party to cover the obligations of another party if that party fails to fulfill them. In the context of development finance, guarantees are used to reduce the risk that investors face when lending to or investing in projects that they perceive as risky.

Here is a concrete example. Suppose a power company wants to borrow Rs. 5 billion to build a small hydropower plant. The company has reasonable revenues and a sound project plan, but it is in Nepal — which foreign commercial banks might perceive as risky — and the power plant will not generate revenues until it is complete in four years. Commercial banks might refuse to lend, or might require an interest rate of 18% that makes the project uneconomic.

Now suppose the Alternative Development Finance Fund provides a guarantee. The guarantee says: "If the power company defaults on its loan, we will pay up to Rs. 3 billion of the outstanding balance." With this guarantee, commercial banks now face much lower risk — they are protected against most of the downside. They can lend at a much lower rate — perhaps 9%. This lower rate makes the project economically viable, and it proceeds.

The key point is that the guarantee may never be called. If the power plant is built and operates successfully, the fund never pays anything. The guarantee has enabled a Rs. 5 billion investment to happen — creating employment, generating electricity, and improving the economy — without the fund spending a single rupee. The fund has used the credibility of its balance sheet rather than its cash.

### Partial Credit Guarantees and Risk-Sharing

The Act authorizes the fund to establish a "jamanat kosh" (guarantee fund) — a fund dedicated to providing guarantees to infrastructure projects. This is separate from the main fund, allowing the guarantee capacity to be tracked and managed separately from direct investment capital.

The Act also authorizes the fund to provide guarantees in conjunction with Nepal government guarantees, or with guarantees from international financial institutions. This "stacking" of guarantees — government + DFI + international institution — can cover a very high proportion of investor risk, making even quite challenging projects financeable.

## Part 7: Fund-of-Funds

### What a Fund-of-Funds Is

A fund-of-funds (FoF) is a meta-fund — an investment fund that itself invests in other investment funds, rather than directly in projects or companies.

Here is the logic. Suppose there are ten different private infrastructure funds in Nepal — each focused on a different sector (energy, roads, agribusiness) or region, each with different investment strategies. An individual investor might want to invest in Nepali infrastructure but cannot afford to evaluate and invest in all ten funds separately. A fund-of-funds allows the investor to invest once in the FoF, which then allocates the money across all ten underlying funds. The investor gets diversification; the FoF manager does the work of evaluating and selecting underlying funds.

The Act authorizes the fund to establish a fund-of-funds ("fund af fund") specifically for infrastructure investment. This structure is particularly useful for attracting institutional investors — pension funds, insurance companies — who want Nepal infrastructure exposure but lack the in-house expertise to evaluate individual projects or sector funds directly.

## Part 8: The Instruments Taken Together — A Complete Picture

Looking at all of these instruments together, we can see that the Act is trying to build a complete financial toolkit:

- **Bonds** tap the savings of ordinary investors and institutional investors who want steady, predictable returns
- **Equity** brings in investors willing to take more risk for potentially higher returns, and aligns their incentives with project performance
- **Blended structures** allow the public sector to take first-loss risk, making commercial investment viable in projects that would otherwise be too risky
- **Guarantees** enable the public sector's credit standing to reduce risk without deploying cash
- **Remittance funds** mobilize the enormous but currently unproductive pool of diaspora savings
- **Asset monetization** converts existing public assets into immediate investment capital
- **Fund-of-funds** aggregates small investments and routes them efficiently to diverse projects

This is a sophisticated toolkit. It reflects the global state of the art in development finance. The question — which we will explore throughout this series — is whether Nepal's institutional environment, capital market depth, legal framework, and governance quality are sufficient to make these instruments work in practice, rather than on paper.
