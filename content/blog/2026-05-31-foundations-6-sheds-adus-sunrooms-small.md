# Part 6 of 10 — Small Structures, Real Foundations: Sheds, ADUs, Sunrooms, and Garages in Newport News

"It's just a shed." This is the sentence that precedes a startling number of expensive foundation problems. A shed is just a shed — until it is a shed that has shifted three inches downhill, whose double doors no longer close, whose floor is a trip hazard, and whose contents are being damaged by water seeping through a cracked slab. At that point it is a project. A project that costs more to fix than the original shed cost to build, and more than a proper foundation would have cost in the first place.

Newport News, Virginia sits in a specific intersection of geological, climatic, and regulatory conditions that shapes what a proper foundation looks like for every structure type. This article answers the specific questions a homeowner or builder in the Hampton Roads area should ask before starting any project from a simple storage shed to a full accessory dwelling unit.

## Part 1: Newport News and Hampton Roads — The Local Context

### Geography and Geology

Newport News sits on the Virginia Peninsula — the strip of land between the York River to the north and the James River to the south, at the western end of Chesapeake Bay. The city extends from the inner Newport News shipyards and city center near the James River northward into more suburban and rural terrain.

The geology of Hampton Roads is dominated by Coastal Plain sediments — thick sequences of marine sand, silt, and clay deposited in ancient shallow seas over the last 70 million years, during a time when sea level was much higher and the Virginia piedmont was a coastline. These sediments underlie essentially the entire Coastal Plain of Virginia, from the "fall line" (approximately along Interstate 95) to the modern coast.

**Key stratigraphic units in Newport News and Hampton Roads**:

**Yorktown Formation (Pliocene, approximately 2.5 to 5 million years old)**: The most commonly encountered unit in shallow residential excavations. Alternating sands and silty/clayey materials, with characteristic fossil shells (oysters, scallops, bryozoans) visible in some exposures. Variable engineering properties: the sandy units have moderate bearing capacity (N = 10 to 30); the clayey units are softer (N = 4 to 12). Permeability is variable — sandy zones drain well; clayey zones poorly.

**Windsor and Tabb Formations**: Younger Pleistocene sands and clays that form the surface in many parts of the Peninsula. Generally well-compacted (overconsolidated by past glacial-era sea level lowering and desiccation), with moderate bearing capacity.

**Alluvial and Tidal Deposits**: Along tidal creeks, the James River shore, and low-lying areas, soft alluvial muds, peat, and tidal marsh deposits can be found. These are the problem areas — very low bearing capacity (N < 4 in some areas), high compressibility, significant settlement potential.

**Fill**: A significant portion of developed Newport News sits on historical fill — areas formerly tidal or low-lying that have been filled with a mixture of dredge material, construction debris, and general fill over the past century of development. Fill quality is highly variable. Never assume fill is competent without investigation.

**Practical summary for most Newport News residential lots**: On upland areas (most established neighborhoods), near-surface soils are typically medium-stiff to stiff clay and silty clay (Yorktown or Windsor Formation), adequate for shallow foundation bearing with allowable pressures of 1,500 to 3,000 psf. Low-lying lots near tidal areas may have soft alluvial soils or fill requiring special treatment.

### Frost Depth

Newport News has one of the shallowest design frost depths in the continental United States:
- **Design frost depth**: 6 inches (per Virginia Construction Code, consistent with IRC Table R301.2(1) for Zone 1, coastal Virginia)
- **Practical footing depth**: Most builders use 12 to 18 inches below grade as standard practice, which provides comfortable margin beyond the 6-inch frost requirement

This shallow frost depth is a major advantage — it means exterior footings do not need to be deep for frost protection, dramatically reducing foundation costs compared to northern climates. A monolithic slab or simple shallow footing is structurally appropriate for frost purposes in this climate.

### Climate and Moisture

Hampton Roads has a humid subtropical climate (Köppen Cfa):
- Average annual rainfall: approximately 45 to 47 inches, distributed relatively evenly throughout the year
- High humidity: relative humidity averages 65% to 75% year-round, with summer peaks of 80%+
- No dry season — the soil stays moist throughout the year, with seasonal fluctuations

The high humidity has direct implications for foundations:
- **Crawl spaces must be encapsulated**, not vented (as discussed in Article 3 — vented crawl spaces in humid climates accumulate moisture from warm, moist outdoor air condensing in the cool crawl space)
- **Vapor retarder under slabs is critical**
- **Wood-to-concrete connections must use pressure-treated lumber** — untreated lumber in contact with moist concrete will absorb moisture and become a substrate for wood-destroying organisms

### Sea Level Rise and Flooding

Hampton Roads is one of the fastest-subsiding coastal regions in the United States. The combination of glacial isostatic adjustment (the land is still slowly adjusting after the Wisconsin Glaciation removed its ice load thousands of miles to the north) and compaction of deep sediments under the region's weight produces subsidence of approximately 3 to 5 millimeters per year. Combined with sea level rise of approximately 3 to 4 millimeters per year (global component) and variable local components, the effective relative sea level rise in Hampton Roads is among the highest in the nation — on the order of 5 to 9 mm/year.

**Practical implications for foundations**:
- Properties in FEMA Special Flood Hazard Areas (Zone AE or VE) are subject to the Virginia Construction Code's flood-resistant construction requirements (based on ASCE 24 — Flood Resistant Design and Construction)
- In flood zones, the lowest floor of habitable space must be at or above the Base Flood Elevation (BFE) plus freeboard (typically 1 to 2 feet above BFE for Hampton Roads jurisdictions)
- Foundations in Zone V (coastal high-hazard area, subject to wave action) must use open foundation systems (pile or pier) rather than solid walls — a solid foundation wall in Zone V can be undermined by wave scour and is a code violation
- Even properties not currently in a flood zone should consider their elevation relative to BFE and projected sea level rise over the building's life (30+ years)

Check FEMA flood maps at msc.fema.gov for any specific Newport News property before designing foundations.

### Local Codes and Permits

Newport News enforces the **Virginia Uniform Statewide Building Code (USBC)**, which incorporates the 2021 editions of the International Building Code (IBC) and International Residential Code (IRC) with Virginia amendments. The City of Newport News Development Department handles building permits.

**Permit thresholds**: In Newport News (as in most Virginia jurisdictions), building permits are required for:
- Any structure over 256 square feet in floor area (even detached accessory structures)
- Any structure with a foundation (regardless of size, in many cases)
- Electrical, plumbing, or HVAC work in any structure
- Attached structures (sunrooms, additions) — always require permits

Below 256 sq ft: Structures under this size may not require a building permit in Newport News for the structure itself, but check current code requirements — this threshold has changed and varies by jurisdiction. Even without a structural permit, zoning setback requirements and HOA restrictions may still apply.

**Zoning**: Before designing any accessory structure, verify zoning requirements:
- Setback requirements from property lines (typically 3 to 5 feet minimum for sheds; more for garages and ADUs)
- Maximum lot coverage (the total percentage of lot area that can be covered by all structures)
- Maximum structure height
- ADU-specific regulations (Virginia enacted statewide ADU-enabling legislation in 2021; local jurisdictions may have specific development standards)

**Contact**: Newport News Building Permit Office, 2400 Washington Ave, Newport News, VA 23607. Phone: (757) 933-2311. Online permit portal available at nnva.gov.

## Part 2: Foundation for a Garden Shed in Newport News

### Project Description

A typical residential storage shed: 10 ft × 12 ft = 120 sq ft, wood frame with metal or vinyl siding and metal roofing, used for garden tools, lawn equipment, bicycles. No habitable space, no plumbing, no HVAC.

### Loads

Dead load: Wood frame shed, metal roofing = approximately 8 to 12 psf dead load
Live load: 10 to 20 psf (minimal occupancy — just access for storage)
Snow load (Newport News): Ground snow load = 15 psf (ASCE 7, Figure 7.2-1 for coastal Virginia); balanced roof snow load = significantly reduced for a sloped metal roof, often 5 to 10 psf design
Wind: Newport News is in a moderate wind zone; design wind speed 115 mph (3-second gust, Risk Category I per ASCE 7-22) for accessory structures

Total load on 120 sq ft structure (rough): 120 sq ft × (12 + 15 + 10) psf ≈ 4,440 lbs total = about 2.2 tons

This is a very light load. Almost any bearing surface can handle it. The challenge is not bearing capacity — it is levelness, stability, and drainage.

### Option 1: Crushed Stone Pad (No Permit Required for < 256 sq ft)

For the smallest sheds (under 256 sq ft) on flat, well-drained sites in Newport News:

**Design**:
- Excavate 6 to 8 inches of topsoil from the shed footprint plus 12 inches around the perimeter
- Grade the excavated area level (use a laser level or long board and level)
- Install landscape fabric over the excavated area to prevent weed growth
- Place and compact 4 to 6 inches of clean crushed stone (3/4-inch crushed limestone or granite)
- Place pressure-treated 4×4 or 4×6 runners (skids) directly on the gravel, supporting the shed floor frame
- Anchor the skids to prevent wind uplift — stake to ground or use anchor clips

**Cost**: $100 to $300 for materials; DIY-feasible.

**Limitations**: This is NOT a foundation in the code-structural sense. It works for small, light sheds on stable soil. It will NOT work on slopes, in low-lying wet areas, or for any structure with a concrete slab floor. It provides zero frost protection (not needed in Newport News at this scale). A shed on gravel skids is also potentially a windload vulnerability — in a hurricane or tropical storm, it can shift or tip. Anchor properly.

**Best for**: 8×10 to 10×14 lightweight sheds on stable, well-drained, level ground.

### Option 2: Concrete Block Piers with Treated Wood Perimeter Frame

The most common shed foundation in Hampton Roads:

**Design**:
- Place concrete masonry units (solid CMU, 8×8×16 or solid cap blocks) at each corner and at 4-foot intervals along the perimeter and under any central girder
- Embed in a shallow bed of crushed stone or concrete for stability
- Level precisely with shims or by adjusting stone depth
- Frame pressure-treated 4×6 or 6×6 perimeter beams on the piers
- Build floor system on the perimeter frame

This system is NOT a code-compliant foundation for permitted structures. It is a common-sense solution for small non-permitted structures. The piers provide adequate bearing; the treated wood frame provides lateral stability; the elevation above grade (typically 4 to 8 inches) promotes drainage and air circulation, reducing wood rot risk.

**Cost**: $150 to $500 materials; DIY-feasible.

**Limitations**: Can settle or shift over time; blocks can sink unevenly; no wind uplift resistance unless anchored; not code-compliant for structures requiring permits.

### Option 3: Concrete Slab (Proper Foundation for Permitted Shed)

For any shed requiring a permit (over 256 sq ft, or with concrete floor, or as a condition of the project), a concrete slab is the appropriate foundation.

**Design for Newport News**:
- Frost depth is only 6 inches, so a monolithic slab with an 8-inch-deep thickened edge is entirely code-compliant
- Slab thickness: 4 inches for a simple storage shed
- Thickened edge at perimeter: 8 inches deep × 12 inches wide
- Rebar: #3 bars in a 12×12-inch grid, supported at mid-slab height
- Concrete: 3,000 psi, water-cement ratio ≤ 0.50
- Gravel base: 4 inches of clean 3/4-inch crushed stone
- Vapor retarder: 10 mil poly under slab (not critical for a storage shed but good practice)

**Anchor bolts for wind resistance**: 1/2-inch J-bolts at 6 feet on center, embedded 7 inches into the concrete, 1-inch projection above slab for nut and washer. The shed's mudsill attaches to the anchor bolts.

**Steps**:
1. Stake and string the slab perimeter
2. Excavate 4 to 6 inches across the slab area, plus 12 inches of trench around the perimeter for the thickened edge
3. Compact the subgrade
4. Place and compact 4 inches of gravel
5. Optional: vapor retarder
6. Place rebar on chairs
7. Install anchor bolt locations on form boards
8. Pour concrete; strike off level with screed board; float and trowel finish
9. Cure with wet burlap or curing compound for 7 days

**Cost**: $500 to $2,000 for materials (concrete, rebar, gravel, forms); $1,500 to $4,000 for a contractor. A 12×16 slab (192 sq ft) requires approximately 1.2 cubic yards of concrete at the thickened edge plus 2.7 yards for the slab = approximately 4 yards total.

**Permit**: In Newport News, this slab typically requires a building permit. The permit fee for a small accessory structure slab is typically $75 to $150.

## Part 3: Foundation for a Sunroom Addition in Newport News

### Project Description

A sunroom is an addition to the house, typically with a large glazed area (glass or polycarbonate panels), used as a light-filled living, dining, or relaxation space. Sunrooms range from prefabricated kits (three-season rooms with limited insulation) to fully conditioned four-season additions.

Critically: a sunroom is an **addition to the house**. It is structurally connected to the existing house. This has significant foundation implications.

### Why Sunroom Foundations Are Particularly Tricky

**Differential settlement**: The new sunroom foundation and the existing house foundation are two different structural systems. If they settle at different rates — which is almost inevitable without careful design — the connection between them experiences differential movement that cracks the walls, breaks window seals, opens gaps at the roof junction, and jams doors.

**Load on existing foundation**: If the sunroom is structurally connected to the house and shares the house's foundation at the connection point, the existing foundation must be verified to handle the added load.

**Thermal bridging**: Sunrooms often have large glass areas and minimal floor insulation. A poorly insulated sunroom floor slab can be the building's single largest source of heat loss in winter and heat gain in summer.

### Foundation Options for Newport News Sunrooms

**Option 1: Independent Slab on Grade with Isolation Joint**

The sunroom has its own independent slab, not structurally connected to the house slab. A flexible isolation joint (compressible foam backer and sealant) at the connection between the house and sunroom allows independent movement. The sunroom roof and walls tie into the house structure, but the floors are decoupled.

This approach minimizes differential settlement damage because the two floors are allowed to move independently.

**Newport News design**:
- 4-inch slab with 8-inch-deep monolithic thickened edge (frost depth 6 inches, monolithic slab is adequate)
- 4 inches of gravel base
- #3 rebar at 12 inches on center
- 3,000 psi concrete
- Under-slab insulation: R-10 XPS (required by IECC 2021 for conditioned space in Climate Zone 3-4)
- Vapor retarder: 15-mil poly
- Isolation joint at house connection: 1/2-inch closed-cell polyethylene foam backer, polyurethane sealant
- Anchor bolts at 4 feet on center for sunroom wall framing

**Cost**: $3,000 to $8,000 for a typical 12×16 sunroom slab with insulation and vapor control.

**Option 2: Piers with Insulated Grade Beam**

On lots with soft near-surface soil, or where the sunroom slab must be elevated to match the house floor height (which may be 18 to 24 inches above grade for older houses on crawl spaces), piers with a grade beam are appropriate:

- Drilled concrete piers or helical piers to 4 to 6 feet depth (well below any soft near-surface layer)
- Reinforced concrete grade beam spanning between piers
- Insulated floor system on the grade beam (either a concrete slab or wood-framed floor with rigid foam beneath)

The piers anchor the sunroom foundation to stable soil, minimizing settlement. For a typical 12×16 sunroom, 4 to 6 piers are typically sufficient.

**Cost**: $5,000 to $15,000 depending on pier depth and number.

**Option 3: Cantilevered from House Foundation**

If the house has a continuous perimeter foundation (not a slab — a crawl space or basement with a perimeter wall), it may be structurally feasible to extend the floor system from the house over the sunroom area, essentially cantilevering it from the house foundation. The sunroom then has no independent foundation — it hangs off the house structure.

This avoids the differential settlement issue entirely. But it requires verification that the house foundation and existing floor framing can handle the cantilever loads — this requires a structural engineer's review.

This is typically the most structurally elegant solution for crawl space houses where the sunroom can be at the same floor level as the adjacent room without a significant grade change.

### Thermal Considerations for Sunrooms

Regardless of foundation type, the sunroom floor must be insulated for thermal comfort and energy code compliance. In Newport News (IECC Climate Zone 3):

- For conditioned sunrooms (heated and cooled): R-10 minimum under slab (IECC Table R402.1.3)
- For three-season rooms (not conditioned): No floor insulation requirement, but thermal comfort is dramatically improved with at least R-10 under the slab

Under-slab insulation for sunrooms: 2-inch XPS (R-10) directly below the concrete slab. The insulation must be rated for in-ground use and capable of supporting concrete load. XPS is appropriate; polyiso (polyisocyanurate) is NOT appropriate for in-ground use due to moisture sensitivity.

## Part 4: Foundation for an ADU (Accessory Dwelling Unit) in Newport News

### ADU Context in Virginia

Virginia's 2021 legislation (HB 1914/SB 1281) requires localities to allow ADUs in single-family residential zones by right, subject to reasonable regulations. Newport News has implemented ADU-enabling ordinances consistent with the state law. An ADU is a secondary dwelling unit on the same lot as a primary residence — it can be attached to the house (like a converted garage apartment), detached (like a backyard cottage), or above a garage.

ADUs in Newport News are subject to all standard building code requirements. They are habitable space and must meet the full IRC requirements for structural systems, fire and life safety, energy efficiency, and accessibility.

### ADU Foundation Requirements

An ADU is treated as a dwelling unit under the IRC and must meet all of the same foundation requirements as the primary house:
- Footing depth below frost line (6 inches in Newport News — easily satisfied)
- Minimum footing width and thickness per IRC Table R403.1
- All concrete and reinforcement per ACI 332
- Waterproofing or dampproofing for any below-grade space
- All energy code requirements including under-slab insulation for heated spaces

### Foundation Options for a Detached ADU in Newport News

**For a slab-on-grade ADU (1 story, 400 to 800 sq ft)**:

This is the most common configuration for a detached ADU in Hampton Roads — a single-story structure on a concrete slab with exterior-insulated walls.

**Newport News design**:
1. Geotechnical investigation: For an ADU, especially on a lot with unknown or marginal soil, a geotechnical evaluation is strongly recommended. This can be as simple as 2 to 3 hand-dug test pits examined by a licensed geotechnical engineer, plus basic bearing capacity assessment. Cost: $500 to $1,500.

2. Site preparation: Clear and grub the footprint; remove all topsoil and organic material (minimum 6 inches below slab bottom).

3. Granular fill: 4 to 6 inches of clean crushed stone, compacted.

4. Vapor retarder: 15-mil polyethylene, lapped 12 inches at seams, taped.

5. Under-slab insulation: 2-inch XPS (R-10) for conditioned space per IECC 2021 in Climate Zone 3. Total: R-10 minimum.

6. Rebar: #4 bars at 12 inches each way (more robust than a storage shed given ADU is habitable space), supported at mid-depth.

7. Concrete: 3,000 psi minimum, water-cement ratio ≤ 0.50.

8. Slab: 4 to 5 inches thick for a single-story wood-frame ADU.

9. Thickened edge: 12 inches wide × 18 inches deep (monolithic, below topsoil, exceeding 6-inch frost depth comfortably, providing margin for the significant loads of a habitable structure).

10. Anchor bolts: 1/2-inch J-bolts at 4 feet on center (same as primary house), 7-inch embedment. For elevated wind areas (Newport News Wind Zone II), spacing may need to be closer — verify with engineer or use the IRC Simplified Wind Design tables.

**For a two-story or heavy ADU**: Consult a structural engineer. Higher loads require larger footings, stronger concrete, more rebar, and possibly deeper bearing.

**For a pier-and-beam ADU (matching existing house foundation type)**:

If the main house is on a crawl space (common in older Newport News neighborhoods), a detached ADU on a crawl space with the same floor system provides better visual continuity and potentially better moisture management on low-lying lots.

- Perimeter footing: 12 inches wide × 18 inches deep, reinforced concrete
- Stem wall: 8-inch poured concrete or CMU, height to provide minimum 18-inch crawl space clearance
- Encapsulated crawl space: Full 20-mil poly ground cover, insulated foundation walls (R-10 ccSPF or rigid foam), no vents, supply air or dehumidifier
- Interior piers as needed for floor system support

**Flood zone considerations for ADUs**: If the ADU site is in a Special Flood Hazard Area, all requirements of the Virginia Construction Code flood-resistant construction provisions apply. For Zone AE:
- Finished floor of ADU at or above BFE + 1 foot (Newport News requirement)
- Foundation must be capable of resisting flood loads and hydrodynamic forces
- Concrete walls must be designed for flood conditions; open foundation systems may be required
- No utilities, mechanical equipment, or habitable space below BFE

This requirement can significantly change ADU design — on a flood-prone lot, the ADU may need to be on piers or elevated foundation walls, with an elevated floor level.

### Energy Code for ADUs

ADUs in Virginia are subject to IECC 2021:
- Slab perimeter R-10 minimum (Climate Zone 3)
- Wall insulation minimum R-20 cavity or R-13 + R-5 continuous
- Roof/ceiling R-49
- Windows U-0.30 maximum, SHGC 0.25 maximum
- Air sealing: 3 ACH50 or less (verified by blower door test in some code pathways)

The energy code applies to any ADU submitted for permit. This is not optional and significantly affects insulation specification.

### ADU Permit Process in Newport News

1. Confirm zoning allows ADU on the property (most single-family zones now do per Virginia law)
2. Engage architect or designer for drawings (structural, architectural, electrical/plumbing/mechanical plans required)
3. Submit permit application with full drawings to Newport News Development Department
4. Expect plan review time of 4 to 6 weeks for a residential ADU
5. Pay permit fee (typically $500 to $2,000 for a small ADU, based on construction value)
6. Schedule inspections:
   - Foundation inspection: After forms are set, rebar is placed, and before concrete is poured
   - Framing inspection: After framing is complete, before insulation
   - Insulation/energy inspection: After insulation is installed
   - Final inspection: All work complete

Total ADU construction cost for a 500-sq-ft, 1-story unit in Newport News: $120,000 to $200,000 depending on finish level, including foundation ($8,000 to $15,000 for slab with insulation), framing, mechanicals, insulation, and finishes.

## Part 5: Foundation for a Detached Garage in Newport News

### Loads on a Garage Slab

A garage slab experiences fundamentally different loads than a residential floor slab:
- **Vehicle loads**: A typical passenger vehicle weighs 3,000 to 5,000 lbs. An SUV or light truck: 5,000 to 8,000 lbs. A pickup truck: 5,000 to 10,000 lbs. A loaded moving truck or RV: 15,000 to 30,000+lbs.
- The load is concentrated at the four tire contact patches — each approximately 25 to 50 square inches
- Dynamic loads from driving, braking, and turning are higher than static loads

This means the garage slab experiences concentrated dynamic loads that a house interior slab does not. The failure mode is punching shear through the slab at tire contact points, or cracking from concentrated load.

**Recommendation**: Garage slabs in Newport News should be:
- **5 inches thick minimum** (versus 4 inches for a residential interior slab)
- **#4 rebar at 12 inches on center** (more robust than typical residential slab reinforcement)
- **3,500 to 4,000 psi concrete** (higher strength for concentrated load resistance)
- **Thickened to 8 to 10 inches at the door opening** (where vehicles drive in and loading is concentrated)

### Garage Slab Grade Relationship

Garages must be designed to prevent water from flowing from the garage into the house (or from outside into the garage):
- Garage slab surface must be at or below the adjacent house floor elevation (typically 1-1/2 to 4 inches lower than the house interior floor) to prevent fuel spills and water from entering the house
- Garage apron (the slab section outside the door) must slope away from the door at a minimum of 1% (1/8 inch per foot) — this drains rainwater away from the garage
- A control joint should be placed between the garage apron and the main garage slab to allow differential movement at this highly trafficked joint

### Attached vs. Detached Garage

**Detached garage**: Entirely separate structure; foundation is fully independent. Much simpler design.

**Attached garage**: Shares at least one wall with the house. This requires:
- Foundation that matches or is structurally compatible with the house foundation at the connection
- Fire separation wall between garage and house (IRC R302.5 — 5/8-inch Type X gypsum on garage side of shared wall)
- Elevated door sill from garage to house (minimum 4-inch step up, or floor of house must be at least 4 inches above garage slab)
- Carbon monoxide detector in any sleeping room adjacent to or above the garage

The attached garage foundation in Newport News: same design as the house foundation (typically a slab). The attached garage slab should be a separate pour with an expansion joint at the house connection, allowing differential settlement between the lighter garage slab and the more heavily loaded house foundation.

### Newport News Specific: Expansive Soil?

Hampton Roads soils are predominantly Coastal Plain sediments with varying clay content but generally not highly expansive (not smectite-dominated). The Yorktown Formation clays have moderate plasticity (PI typically 15 to 25) — enough to require attention but not the extreme conditions of DFW.

For garage slabs in Newport News:
- Standard reinforced concrete slab is appropriate
- Post-tensioning is not typically required (unlike Texas)
- Maintain soil moisture: avoid large trees directly adjacent to the slab that could dry the soil unevenly in summer drought

## Part 6: Special Conditions in Hampton Roads

### Termites

The Hampton Roads region has very high subterranean termite pressure — it is in Zone 1 (the highest risk zone) on the USDA termite inundation map. Eastern subterranean termites (Reticulitermes flavipes) are ubiquitous, and Formosan subterranean termites (Coptotermes formosanus) have been documented in the region.

Every foundation in Newport News should include termite prevention:

- **Soil treatment**: Chemical termiticide application to the soil below slab and around foundation perimeter before concrete is placed. Required by Virginia law for new construction under IRC R318.
- **Physical barriers**: Stainless steel mesh termite shields (TermiMesh, Termi-Mesh Australia products) under slab edges provide a mechanical barrier.
- **Pressure-treated wood**: All wood in contact with concrete or within 6 inches of grade must be pressure-treated (ACQ or similar preservative treatment). This is a code requirement, not just a good idea.
- **Foam board insulation termite management**: Any foam insulation on the exterior of the foundation creates a hidden pathway for termites. Solutions: foam with borate impregnation, physical barriers at the top and bottom of foam panels, or avoiding foam on exterior below grade in favor of interior insulation.
- **Annual inspection**: A professional termite inspection annually. In high-pressure areas like Newport News, this is worthwhile maintenance.

### Moisture and Wood Preservation

The humid climate means any wood near grade requires preservative treatment. Requirements:

- **Mudsills**: All mudsills (sill plates) must be pressure-treated, regardless of whether they are in contact with concrete or simply near grade.
- **Joists within 18 inches of grade**: Pressure-treated or naturally durable species (uncommon and expensive).
- **Posts and columns in crawl spaces**: Pressure-treated if wood; concrete-encased or steel if steel.
- **Decking in crawl spaces**: Any wood decking material in contact with or close to the crawl space ground should be pressure-treated.

**ACQ vs. CA vs. MCA treatment**: The treatment chemical matters for hardware compatibility. ACQ (alkaline copper quaternary) and CA (copper azole) — the two most common preservative treatments — are significantly more corrosive to uncoated steel than the old CCA (chromated copper arsenate) that was phased out for residential use in 2004. Use ONLY stainless steel, hot-dipped galvanized (ASTM A123), or specifically labeled connectors and fasteners with ACQ/CA-treated lumber. Standard bright or electroplated fasteners corrode rapidly.

### Flood Hazard and Elevated Foundations

For properties in Special Flood Hazard Areas, Newport News enforces ASCE 24 flood-resistant construction requirements. In Zone AE (riverine flooding):

- All new construction must have the lowest floor at or above BFE + 1 foot (the City's freeboard requirement)
- Foundations must be designed for flood loads including hydrostatic, hydrodynamic, and impact forces
- Enclosed areas below BFE must have flood vents (automatically equalize water pressure) per FEMA Technical Bulletin 1 — minimum 1 square inch net free area per square foot of enclosed area, with vents on at least two sides

In Zone VE (coastal high-hazard area, primarily along the oceanfront areas of Hampton Roads):
- Foundation must be an open foundation on piles or columns
- Solid foundation walls below BFE are prohibited
- Lowest horizontal structural member must be at or above BFE
- Piles must be designed to resist the additional forces of breaking waves and storm surge

### Military Installation Considerations

Newport News is adjacent to Joint Base Langley-Eustis and near Naval Station Norfolk. Some properties may have deed restrictions, building height limitations, or airspace easements related to military operations. These do not affect foundation design directly but affect overall project feasibility.

## Part 7: Inspection and Quality Control for Small Structure Foundations in Newport News

### What the Building Inspector Looks For

For a permitted foundation in Newport News, the building inspector's visit happens before concrete is poured (the "foundation inspection"). The inspector checks:

1. **Footing dimensions**: Width and depth as specified on the permit drawings
2. **Rebar placement**: Size, spacing, cover from soil, and whether it is properly supported (not lying on the ground)
3. **Anchor bolt placement**: Correct locations per drawings; proper embedment depth stenciled on the form
4. **Vapor retarder installation**: Properly placed, lapped, and continuous
5. **Gravel base**: Present and adequately compacted (inspector may probe for softness)
6. **Form integrity**: Forms are plumb and square, properly braced to prevent blowout during pour

If the inspector notes deficiencies, they issue a correction notice and the work must be corrected before re-inspection. Do not pour concrete before passing the foundation inspection — you will be required to remove it.

### Common Deficiencies in Newport News Small Structure Foundations

Based on typical residential construction issues in the region:

1. **Topsoil not removed**: Concrete poured over sod or organic topsoil. This is the most common and most serious deficiency.

2. **Rebar sitting on the ground**: Rebar not on chairs means it ends up at the bottom of the slab, providing no benefit for crack control.

3. **Vapor retarder punctured during rebar placement**: Workers walking on the plastic before the pour puncture it in dozens of places. Inspect before pour.

4. **Anchor bolts placed by eye rather than by measurement**: Bolts placed incorrectly will not align with the framing. Set the anchor bolt template from the drawings and measure twice.

5. **Water added to concrete on site**: As repeatedly noted — adds water, reduces strength and durability.

6. **Footing poured too shallow**: Particularly at the slab perimeter, where excavation was insufficient. The thickened edge needs its full depth.

7. **No drainage slope on garage apron**: A flat garage apron ponds water at the door opening.

---

## Conclusion: Small Structures Deserve Real Engineering

The irony of small structures is that they are taken less seriously in design and construction while their failures are experienced daily and intimately. A cracked garage slab is not a news event, but it is a daily annoyance and a depreciating asset. A shed that has shifted off its blocks and is listing 3 degrees is a safety hazard. An ADU with water intrusion is uninhabitable.

Newport News provides a relatively favorable foundation environment — shallow frost depth, moderate soil conditions, no extreme expansive clay. But the humidity, the termites, the flood hazard in low-lying areas, and the specific soil variability of Coastal Plain geology require attention. Getting these details right on a small structure is no harder or more expensive than getting them wrong — it simply requires knowing what the details are.

In tomorrow's Article 7, we scale up dramatically: multi-family buildings, apartment complexes, mixed-use developments, and the structural engineering challenges of foundations at scale. The same principles apply — soil, loads, drainage — but the complexity, the regulatory requirements, and the consequences of failure all increase with size.
