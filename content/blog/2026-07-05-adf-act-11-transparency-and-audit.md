## Part 1: Why Transparency Is Not Optional for Public Financial Institutions

Public financial institutions — institutions that use public money, carry government guarantees, and serve public purposes — have a fundamentally different obligation to transparency than private businesses. A private company can operate in relative secrecy because it is using private capital from willing investors who accepted the associated opacity. A public institution is using money collected through taxation, backed by the full faith and credit of the government, and serving social purposes that belong to the public. The public has both a right to know and a need to know what is being done in their name.

But transparency is not just a normative obligation. It is a functional necessity. Transparency creates accountability — the condition where decision-makers face consequences for poor decisions and credit for good ones. Accountability creates incentives for better performance. And for a development finance institution seeking to attract investors, transparency builds the credibility and trust that no amount of government guarantee can substitute for. Investors in bonds issued by the fund need to understand the fund's financial position, its portfolio quality, and its governance quality before they commit their capital.

Sections 35 through 39 of the Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 create the fund's transparency and accountability framework. This article examines those provisions in detail.

## Part 2: Quarterly Financial Reporting

Section 35 mandates that the fund prepare its financial statements every three months (quarterly) and provide them to:
- The Nepal government
- Shareholders

These quarterly statements must also be made publicly available on the fund's website.

Quarterly financial reporting is standard for significant financial institutions globally. It serves several purposes:

**Early warning:** Financial problems rarely appear suddenly. Trends of deteriorating loan quality, declining revenues, or rising costs usually develop over months. Quarterly reporting allows the government, shareholders, and external observers to detect concerning trends early, before they become crises.

**Investor confidence:** Investors in the fund's bonds and instruments need to monitor the fund's financial health regularly. Quarterly reporting provides the data they need to make informed decisions about their existing investments and any future commitments.

**Management discipline:** The requirement to produce and present quarterly financials creates internal discipline — management must track performance continuously, not just at year-end. Problems that might be deferred or minimized internally tend to surface when external reporting is required.

**Public accountability:** Making quarterly financials publicly available on the website allows anyone — journalists, civil society organizations, academics, opposition politicians, ordinary citizens — to monitor the fund's performance. This democratic accountability is particularly important for an institution that represents significant public financial risk.

The specific content of quarterly financial statements is delegated to subordinate regulations. At minimum, they should include balance sheet data (assets, liabilities, equity), income statement data (revenues, expenses, profit/loss), and key portfolio data (total investments, number of projects, loan delinquency rates).

## Part 3: The Annual Report

Section 48 (discussed here in the context of accountability even though it appears later in the Act) requires the CEO to prepare a comprehensive annual report that covers:

- The fund's overall progress during the year
- The activities of each sub-fund and financial instrument
- The status of each financial instrument-based sub-fund
- The contingent liabilities created by the government's guarantees on behalf of the fund
- Other relevant financial information

This annual report must be:
1. Approved by the General Shareholders' Meeting (the annual meeting of all shareholders)
2. Submitted to the Finance Ministry within six months of the end of the financial year

The Finance Ministry then makes the annual report publicly available — on the Ministry's website and the fund's website.

The annual report is the fund's primary public accountability document. It should tell a comprehensive story of what the fund did in the year, how its investments performed, what challenges it faced, and where it is heading. The quality of annual reports varies enormously across institutions — from genuinely informative documents that enable accountability to glossy public relations exercises that obscure more than they reveal.

The requirement for approval by the Shareholders' Meeting before submission to the Ministry creates an additional layer of accountability — shareholders must review and accept the report, which provides an opportunity for the pension funds and insurance companies that own significant stakes to raise questions about performance.

### The Liabilities Disclosure Requirement

One provision deserves special attention: the annual report must specifically disclose the contingent liabilities created by government guarantees on behalf of the fund. This is an important requirement for fiscal transparency.

When the government guarantees the fund's bonds or other obligations, those guarantees do not immediately appear in the government's budget as an expenditure. They are contingent — they only become actual expenditures if the guarantees are called (i.e., if the fund cannot meet its obligations). This "off-budget" nature of guarantees has historically been used to expand fiscal commitments without appearing to increase the deficit.

The requirement to disclose government guarantee liabilities in the fund's annual report creates a public record of these contingent obligations. This is important for public understanding of the total fiscal risk that the fund creates for taxpayers. It also creates a basis for parliamentary oversight of the government's guarantee policy.

## Part 4: Internal Audit and Internal Control Systems

Section 38 creates two important internal accountability mechanisms:

**Internal audit:** The fund's internal audit function must be carried out by the Office of the Comptroller General (Mahalekha Niyantrak Karyalaya) or an office designated by it. The Comptroller General is Nepal's supreme internal audit authority for public sector entities — it audits the accounts of all government bodies and publicly owned enterprises to ensure that financial management meets applicable standards.

Having the Comptroller General's office conduct (or oversee) the fund's internal audit is a strong accountability mechanism. The Comptroller General is independent of the Finance Ministry and reports to the government as a whole, not to any individual ministry. Its audit findings carry institutional authority and credibility.

**Internal control systems:** The fund must design and implement an internal control system under applicable law. An internal control system encompasses the policies, procedures, and automated controls that:

- Prevent financial irregularities (unauthorized transactions, misappropriation, fraud)
- Ensure that financial data is recorded accurately
- Identify and manage financial, operational, and compliance risks
- Ensure that financial reporting is complete and accurate

The Act specifically requires that the internal control system address financial risks, procedural risks, and operational risks. This comprehensive scope reflects the multiple ways in which a development finance institution can face losses — not just from bad investments, but from operational failures, fraudulent transactions, and process breakdowns.

Internal control systems are only as effective as their implementation and their culture. A system on paper that is systematically bypassed in practice provides false assurance. Building a genuine culture of financial discipline and compliance requires leadership commitment, training, consequence for violations, and regular testing of controls.

## Part 5: External Audit by the Auditor General

Section 39 is among the most important accountability provisions in the Act. It requires that the final (annual) audit of the fund and all its sub-funds be conducted by the Auditor General (Mahalekhaparikshak) of Nepal.

The Auditor General of Nepal is a constitutional office — established by the Constitution of Nepal itself, not just by ordinary law. The Auditor General is appointed by the President on the recommendation of the Constitutional Council, with a fixed term of six years. They cannot be removed except by a constitutional process. Their office audits all public entities and presents audit reports to the President, who transmits them to parliament.

The constitutional status of the Auditor General gives their audit function a special authority and independence. They are not appointed by — or removable by — the Finance Minister or the Cabinet. Their findings cannot be suppressed by executive decision. Their audit reports become part of the parliamentary record.

The audit under Section 39 must not only check whether the fund's accounts are accurate, but must also specifically comment on:

- Whether the fund's operations and financial transactions comply with applicable law and financial standards
- This is called "compliance audit" as well as "financial audit"

Compliance audit is particularly important for a public institution. It asks not just "are the numbers right?" but "are the operations conducted in accordance with the law?" — including whether governance rules are being followed, whether investment criteria are being respected, whether conflict-of-interest provisions are being observed.

The Auditor General's report on the fund will be available to parliament and to the public. Opposition politicians, civil society organizations, and journalists can use it to hold the fund accountable.

### What the Audit Process Looks Like in Practice

An audit by the Auditor General's office typically involves:

1. **Planning:** Identifying the key risks and areas requiring detailed examination
2. **Document review:** Examining the fund's books, records, board minutes, investment agreements, and financial statements
3. **Testing:** Sampling individual transactions to verify they are properly recorded, authorized, and comply with applicable rules
4. **Discussion with management:** Raising findings with management and giving them an opportunity to respond
5. **Reporting:** Preparing the audit report with findings, conclusions, and recommendations

The Auditor General's office in Nepal has grown in capacity over the years but still faces resource constraints — it is responsible for auditing an enormous number of public entities. The quality of individual audits varies. For a major new institution like the ADFF, the Auditor General's office will need to allocate significant specialist capacity — people with financial sector expertise who understand development finance instruments, project finance accounting, and the specific risks of the fund's operations.

## Part 6: The Triennial Independent Performance Evaluation

Section 49 creates one of the most sophisticated accountability mechanisms in the Act: a comprehensive performance evaluation conducted every three years by an independent evaluator outside Nepal ("antararastriya stharko swatantra mulyankankartha" — an independent evaluator of international standing).

This three-year evaluation must assess:

- The financial condition and instruments of the fund and its sub-funds
- The fund's sustainability as an institution
- The fund's assets and liabilities
- The fund's overall performance

The evaluator must submit their report — with prescribed details — to both the Finance Ministry and the fund's board.

This provision is notable for several reasons. First, it requires an evaluator of "international standing" — not just a Nepali auditing firm, but an institution with international expertise and credibility. This signals that the fund's performance will be benchmarked against international standards, not just domestic practice. Second, the three-year frequency strikes an appropriate balance — frequent enough to provide regular checkpoints, not so frequent as to be an administrative burden. Third, the mandatory report to both the Ministry and the Board creates accountability in two directions — the Ministry can use the evaluation to assess whether the fund needs corrective intervention, and the Board can use it to guide internal reform.

Independent institutional evaluations — sometimes called "institutional reviews" or "independent assessments" — are standard practice for multilateral development banks (the World Bank, ADB, and others all have independent evaluation offices) but less common for national DFIs. Their inclusion in the Act represents an international best-practice standard.

## Part 7: Parliamentary Reporting and the Implementation Monitoring Mechanism

Section 58 creates an unusual ongoing accountability mechanism: the law requires the Finance Ministry to conduct an implementation assessment of the Act itself every five years, and to report the results to the relevant committee of the Federal Parliament.

The first assessment must be conducted within one year of the Act's five-year anniversary, and subsequent assessments every five years thereafter.

This provision transforms the Act from a static piece of legislation into a dynamic, self-improving framework. The five-year assessment asks not just "is the fund complying with the Act?" but "is the Act itself working as intended? Should it be amended? Are there gaps? Are there provisions that are not being implemented as envisioned? Are there new developments — new financial instruments, new development challenges, changed economic conditions — that require legislative adaptation?"

The parliamentary committee that receives this report has the authority to scrutinize the Ministry's assessment, call for additional information, and recommend amendments to the Act. This creates a formal mechanism for legislative learning and adaptation — a recognition that complex enabling legislation cannot be written perfectly the first time and must evolve based on implementation experience.

## Part 8: The General Shareholders' Meeting

Section 46 requires the fund to hold an Annual General Shareholders' Meeting (Sadharan Sabha) within one year of establishment, and annually thereafter within six months of the end of each financial year.

The AGM gives shareholders — the government, pension funds, insurance companies — a formal forum to:
- Receive and approve the annual report and financial statements
- Review dividend proposals
- Elect or confirm board members where applicable
- Ask questions of management
- Vote on significant matters

The public company law provisions apply to the AGM by default (the Act states this explicitly). This means the fund's AGM procedures are governed by Nepal's Companies Act provisions for public limited companies, with necessary modifications. This creates a standardized legal framework for shareholder rights and meeting procedures.

## Part 9: An Honest Assessment — What the Transparency Framework Can and Cannot Do

The transparency and accountability framework in the Act is genuinely strong — stronger than what most comparable Nepali public institutions have. Quarterly financial reporting, mandatory website publication, Auditor General audit, independent international evaluation, parliamentary reporting — these are a comprehensive set of accountability mechanisms.

But accountability mechanisms only work if they are used. Let us be honest about the limitations:

**Data quality depends on accounting quality.** If the fund's internal accounting is poor — if loans are not properly classified, if impairment provisions are understated, if contingent liabilities are hidden in off-balance-sheet structures — then quarterly financial reports and audit reports will reflect that poor accounting rather than exposing it. Strong international accounting standards, consistently applied by competent accountants, are a prerequisite for meaningful reporting.

**The Auditor General's office has capacity limits.** Auditing a sophisticated development finance institution with multiple sub-funds, complex project finance instruments, and international transactions requires specialized expertise that the Auditor General's office may not fully have. The quality of the audit depends heavily on the capacity and independence of the audit team assigned to the fund.

**Transparency without response is ineffective.** If the fund's annual report reveals poor performance, high delinquency rates, and governance failures, but nobody acts on this information — if the Finance Ministry dismisses the findings, if parliament does not scrutinize the audit reports, if civil society lacks the capacity to analyze the data — then transparency does not generate accountability.

**Parliamentary oversight depends on political will.** Parliamentary committees can be powerful accountability mechanisms, but in Nepal (as in many countries) they sometimes struggle with technical complexity, party discipline, and limited research capacity. A parliamentary committee examining the fund's annual report needs members with financial literacy, staff with analytical capacity, and the political will to ask hard questions regardless of which party formed the government.

These are not arguments against the transparency provisions. They are arguments for complementing the formal mechanisms with investment in:
- Capacity building for the Auditor General's office
- Financial literacy for parliamentary committee members
- Support for civil society organizations that analyze public financial institutions
- A media environment that can report on and interpret financial accountability information
