## Part 1: Why the CEO Matters More Than Almost Anything Else

In any organization, the quality of leadership is determinative. But in a new development finance institution, the first CEO is especially critical. They will shape the institution's culture, hire the senior management team, establish the investment processes, build relationships with investors and project developers, and demonstrate to the world — by their first investment decisions — whether this institution is serious or just another political appointment vehicle.

Sections 18, 19, 20, and 21 of the Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 address the organizational structure and, specifically, the CEO of the fund. The provisions are unusually detailed and rigorous for a Nepali public institution law. This reflects, presumably, lessons learned from the appointment processes of other Nepali public institutions where politicized appointments have undermined institutional quality.

Let us examine every element of the CEO provisions with care.

## Part 2: The Organizational Structure Context

Section 18 establishes the fund's organizational structure. The structure will include:

- Civil servants assigned by the government
- Employees hired on contract by the fund for its operational needs
- Technical experts engaged on a service contract basis

The mix of civil servants (assigned from the bureaucracy), contract employees (hired specifically for the fund), and outside experts (engaged for specific needs) is pragmatic. It allows the fund to access both the institutional knowledge and security of the civil service and the flexibility and specialization of contract employment.

Critically, the terms of employment — salaries, service conditions, and benefits — for contract employees and expert staff are determined by the board with the Finance Ministry's consent. This allows the fund to pay competitive rates for specialized financial expertise, which is essential if it is to attract qualified personnel who have other options (including private sector employment). If the fund is limited to civil service pay scales, it will struggle to attract and retain the financial analysts, risk managers, investment officers, and lawyers it needs.

## Part 3: The CEO's Role and Responsibilities

Section 19 establishes the CEO as the fund's primary administrative officer — "pramukh prashashakiya padadhikari" — responsible for the fund's daily administration, management, and performance. The CEO works full-time. The CEO's responsibilities (Section 20) include:

- Preparing proposals for board meetings
- Submitting investment proposals to the board for approval
- Implementing board decisions and directives
- Preparing annual programmes, budgets, business plans, and at minimum five-year strategic plans and presenting them for board approval
- Implementing the approved programmes, budgets, and plans
- Monitoring the implementation of funded projects and reporting to the board
- Studying and reporting on the feasibility of alternative finance instruments
- Performing any other tasks the board assigns

This is a demanding and comprehensive role. The CEO is both the fund's chief strategist (developing the business plan and strategy) and its chief operator (ensuring implementation). The size and scope of this role requires exceptional capacity — and the appointment process reflects this.

## Part 4: The Selection Committee — An Unusual Structure

Section 19, Sub-section 5 establishes a selection (recommendation) committee to shortlist candidates for the CEO position. This committee is genuinely unusual and worth examining closely.

The committee consists of:
- **The Chair of the Civil Service Commission** — as convener
- **The Governor of Nepal Rastra Bank**
- **The CEO of the Investment Board Nepal**

This is a three-person committee comprising three of the most senior and prestigious institutional figures in Nepal's financial and governmental systems. The Civil Service Commission chair is the constitutional body responsible for appointments and career management in Nepal's civil service — they have deep expertise in merit-based selection processes. The Governor of Nepal Rastra Bank is the head of the central bank — they have the deepest financial sector expertise of any public official in Nepal. The CEO of the Investment Board Nepal is the head of the institution most closely related to the new fund's mandate (large infrastructure project facilitation).

Critically, none of these three people are in the chain of command for the new fund. They are not part of the Finance Ministry. They are not part of the fund's board. They have no direct interest in the appointment outcome. They are selected specifically because they combine financial expertise with institutional prestige and independence from the fund's governance structure.

The committee is responsible for:
- Determining its own selection process (it has full autonomy over methodology)
- Running a competitive process
- Recommending three candidates to the Cabinet

From those three, the Cabinet chooses one for appointment. The Cabinet retains the final decision — this is an elected government with democratic accountability, and it is appropriate that it makes the final appointment. But the Cabinet's choice is constrained to the three candidates recommended by the independent committee, preventing purely political appointments of unqualified individuals.

This structure represents a genuine attempt to create a merit-based selection process with political accountability at the final step. It draws on international practice — many central bank governor appointments, for example, involve independent recommendation processes before political appointment. Whether it works in practice depends on whether the three committee members genuinely conduct a rigorous, merit-based selection without being influenced by political pressure, and on whether the Cabinet genuinely chooses among the three candidates on merit rather than selecting whichever one has the best political connections.

## Part 5: Qualifications for the CEO

Section 19, Sub-section 3 specifies what qualifications the CEO must have. The CEO must:

- Hold at least a postgraduate degree from a recognized institution in: economics, management, commerce, development studies, financial analysis, law, engineering, or development administration
- AND have at least 15 years of management experience in: infrastructure construction, project construction and development, public-private partnership, investment promotion, development administration, banking, the financial sector, industrial management, or the international financial sector or organizations

This is demanding. A postgraduate degree plus 15 years of management experience in relevant fields. The person appointed must genuinely have both academic and practical qualifications at a high level.

The Act also adds a significant provision in the first proviso: for the first appointment only, a qualified foreign national may be appointed. This is a striking exception to what might otherwise be assumed to be a requirement for Nepali nationality. The rationale is presumably that for the inaugural appointment, Nepal may not have a sufficient pool of candidates who meet the full requirements with the combination of international exposure, financial expertise, and practical experience needed to set up a genuinely sophisticated development finance institution from scratch. The provision signals that quality of the first appointment is paramount, even at the cost of national preference.

This provision will be controversial in some quarters — there are always political sensitivities about foreigners leading major national institutions. But the pragmatic recognition that Nepal's human capital in this very specific area may be limited is honest and sensible.

## Part 6: CEO Disqualifications

Section 19, Sub-section 4 lists those who cannot be CEO:

- Persons convicted of corruption, theft, human trafficking, money laundering, rape, fraud, or similar morally reprehensible criminal offenses
- Persons currently on the financial regulatory blacklist
- Persons who have obtained permanent residency, green card, or any form of permanent residence permission in any foreign country
- Persons under 45 years of age
- Persons over 65 years of age

Several of these require comment.

The criminal conviction disqualification is standard and appropriate. Anyone with a conviction for financial crimes or moral turpitude should not run a major financial institution.

The blacklist disqualification prevents persons who have defaulted on financial obligations from running an institution that will lend to others.

The **permanent residence in a foreign country** disqualification is unusual and has been a subject of debate in Nepal's policy circles more broadly. The concern is that someone who has obtained permanent residency or citizenship in another country has a lesser commitment to Nepal and could, in the worst case, be vulnerable to foreign interests or could easily decamp with assets if things go wrong. The logic has some validity, but it is also a broad exclusion that could eliminate some genuinely qualified candidates — particularly members of the Nepali diaspora who have built expertise abroad and might otherwise return to serve.

The **minimum age of 45** ensures the CEO has had time to accumulate the experience the role requires. The maximum age of 65 is a practical limit preventing very elderly appointments.

## Part 7: The CEO's Term and Renewal

The CEO's term is four years, renewable once. This means a maximum tenure of eight years.

Four-year terms are standard for senior financial institution executives globally. A four-year term is long enough for a CEO to implement strategy, see projects through from identification to investment to early performance monitoring, and build institutional culture. It is short enough to provide a natural mechanism for change if performance is poor or circumstances change.

The single renewal provision (maximum eight years) prevents the entrenchment of a long-serving CEO who accumulates political relationships and informal power over time. A CEO who knows their maximum tenure is eight years has incentives to build a strong institution that outlasts them, rather than to make themselves personally indispensable.

The post-service rule (Section 19, Sub-section 12) is particularly important: for three years after their term ends, the CEO cannot be employed by or involved in any project that received the fund's investment approval during their tenure. The only exception is projects implemented by entities that are fully or partially government-owned or government-controlled.

This cooling-off period prevents the classic "revolving door" problem: a CEO who approves investment in a project and then takes a senior position with that project's company immediately after leaving the fund. Such arrangements create perverse incentives — a CEO might approve investments with an eye to future employment opportunities rather than purely on merit. Three years is a meaningful cooling period, though some would argue five years is more robust for a position of this importance.

## Part 8: Conditions for CEO Removal

The CEO cannot be removed arbitrarily. Section 19, Sub-section 10 specifies the conditions under which the CEO's position can become vacant:

- If a disqualifying condition arises (described above)
- If the Cabinet removes them on the Board's recommendation due to unsatisfactory performance, poor conduct, or failure of the performance contract (Sub-section 4 of Section 21)
- At the end of their term
- If their resignation through the Board is accepted by the Cabinet
- Death

The requirement for a Board recommendation before removal is an important protection against arbitrary political dismissal. The government cannot simply fire the CEO because a new Finance Minister dislikes them — the Board must recommend removal, and removal must be on specified grounds. This protection is incomplete — the Finance Ministry Secretary chairs the board and could in theory use their position to facilitate a politically motivated recommendation — but it is better than having no procedural requirement at all.

Sub-section 11 adds that before removing a CEO, the government must give them a reasonable opportunity to present a defense. This basic procedural fairness requirement — the right to be heard before being removed — is a minimum standard of natural justice.

## Part 9: The Annual Performance Contract

Section 21 is one of the most operationally important provisions in the Act. It requires that within 30 days of appointment, the CEO must sign an annual performance contract with the Board.

This performance contract must specify:

- A detailed description of the work the CEO must complete within one year, and their responsibilities
- The policy and programmes of the government regarding infrastructure projects, the budget, the strategic plan, the proposals submitted by the Board, and the implementation roadmap including milestones and timelines
- Performance indicators that can be used to measure the CEO's performance, and the expected level of achievement
- The quality of performance, accountability for underachievement, and conditions for failure to achieve targets

The performance contract is evaluated annually by an independent evaluator appointed by the Board. The independent evaluator assesses whether the CEO's performance met the contract's targets, on time and at the required quality level. If performance is found to be poor, the Board can recommend to the Cabinet that the CEO be removed.

This performance contract system is a significant institutional innovation for Nepal. Most senior public sector appointments in Nepal have historically had vague, unmeasurable performance expectations. The explicit requirement for a written contract with specific, measurable targets, evaluated by an independent assessor, creates genuine accountability. It also creates a record — if the CEO claims success while the record shows failure, the contract provides the evidence to test that claim.

The quality of this system depends, as always, on implementation. Performance targets that are set too vaguely, or that can be met by adjusting the methodology of measurement rather than by genuine performance improvement, undermine the contract's value. And if the independent evaluator is not genuinely independent — if they are chosen in ways that make them susceptible to pressure from the CEO or the government — the evaluation will not be credible.

But the framework is sound. It is the kind of accountability mechanism that the best-run development finance institutions use, and its inclusion in the Act reflects genuine learning from international practice.

## Part 10: What This All Means for the Institution's Character

The CEO provisions together create a picture of the kind of institution the Act's drafters envisioned: one led by a highly qualified, experienced professional selected through a merit-based process, operating on a fixed term with clear performance accountability, protected from arbitrary political dismissal, and subject to a cooling-off period that limits the conflict-of-interest risk of post-tenure employment.

This vision is ambitious and, frankly, more sophisticated than what most Nepali public institutions have historically had. Whether the vision is realized in practice depends on:

- Whether the selection committee (Civil Service Commission Chair, NRB Governor, IBN CEO) conducts a genuinely rigorous, merit-based process
- Whether the Cabinet chooses among the three recommendations on merit rather than political connection
- Whether the performance contract targets are set at a level that genuinely measures performance rather than providing an easy cover for underperformance
- Whether the independent evaluator is genuinely independent and rigorous
- Whether the removal protections are actually enforced or are circumvented through informal political pressure

The history of Nepali public institutions provides grounds for caution. The law of the land for public service commissions, central bank governance, and similar institutions contains similar high-minded provisions that have sometimes been honored and sometimes circumvented when political interests were strong enough. The critical question — which can only be answered by watching how the institution operates over time — is whether this fund will be different.
