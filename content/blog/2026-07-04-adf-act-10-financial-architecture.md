## Part 1: The Financial Structure as the Foundation of Sustainability

A development finance institution that runs out of money cannot fulfill its mandate. This obvious statement points to a less obvious challenge: how does a DFI manage its finances so that it remains solvent, continues to grow, and generates sufficient capital to keep investing in development projects over years and decades?

The financial architecture of the Alternative Development Finance Fund — how its money is organized, how reserves are maintained, how surpluses are distributed — is specified in Sections 31 through 34 of the Act. These provisions are technical but critically important. A fund that distributes all its profits as dividends will not have capital for future investments. A fund that maintains excessive reserves will generate poor returns for shareholders. A fund that does not separate its different financial instrument pools will have no way to track the performance of specific programs.

This article examines the Act's financial architecture in plain English.

## Part 2: What Goes Into the Main Fund

Section 31 specifies the sources of income for the fund:

**Share sale proceeds (from Section 8):** The money raised when the fund first issues shares to the government, retirement funds, and insurance companies. This is the initial capitalization — the paid-up capital of Rs. 25 billion. This is the foundation of everything else.

**Proceeds from alternative development finance mobilization (from Section 3):** Income generated by the various financial instruments the fund deploys — interest received on loans, fees received for guarantees, management fees from managing other funds, fees for advisory services, and so on. As the fund deploys its capital and builds a portfolio of investments, this becomes the primary ongoing revenue stream.

**Amounts received from the Nepal government for project execution or institutional development:** The government may provide additional funds over time — for specific projects the government wants the fund to support, or for institutional strengthening of the fund itself. This provision allows the government to supplement the fund's capital when needed without requiring new legislation.

**Grants or loans received from international financial institutions:** Multilateral development banks, bilateral development agencies, and other international institutions may provide grants or concessional loans to support the fund's operations or programs.

**Interest or dividends received from the fund's investments:** Returns on the fund's portfolio — interest received on loans, dividends received from equity investments.

**Service charges and consulting fees:** The fund charges fees for providing guarantees, managing other funds (in its fund-of-funds and fund-manager roles), and providing advisory services to public entities under Section 9(ढ).

**Service charges for guarantees and financial intermediation:** Fees for acting as a guarantee provider or financial intermediary for infrastructure projects.

All of these income streams flow into the fund. The fund must maintain these balances in bank accounts at commercial banks or infrastructure development banks designated by the board — specifically licensed Class A commercial banks or infrastructure development banks authorized by Nepal Rastra Bank. The fund must maintain separate accounts in Nepali rupees and foreign currencies.

## Part 3: The Sub-Fund Architecture

Section 32 is one of the most important financial management provisions. It requires that for each alternative development finance instrument the fund operates, the receipts and payments must be maintained in separate accounts — separate sub-funds.

The rationale for this is sound and important. The Alternative Development Finance Fund is not a single, undifferentiated pool of money. It is an umbrella institution that may simultaneously be:

- Issuing infrastructure bonds and managing the proceeds
- Running an equity fund for hydropower projects
- Managing a guarantee fund
- Operating a remittance investment fund
- Acting as a fund-of-funds

Each of these programs has different financial characteristics:
- Different investor bases (bond investors vs. equity investors vs. government guarantee providers)
- Different risk profiles (loans carry credit risk; equity carries market risk; guarantees carry contingent risk)
- Different time horizons (a 20-year infrastructure bond vs. a 7-year equity fund)
- Different return expectations (bond investors want low, steady returns; equity investors want higher, more variable returns)
- Different regulatory requirements

If all of these were pooled together in a single accounting system, it would be impossible to:
- Track the performance of each individual program
- Demonstrate to investors in a specific program that their money is being used appropriately
- Manage the distinct risks of each program
- Satisfy the reporting and accountability requirements for each program separately
- Prevent cross-subsidization (where losses in one program are quietly covered by surpluses in another)

The requirement for separate accounts — and the management of each sub-fund with a fixed term appropriate to its instruments and purpose — creates financial transparency and program-level accountability that a pooled system could not provide.

The sub-funds must maintain their balances in the same manner as the main fund's accounts (designated commercial banks, with separate foreign and domestic currency accounts).

Section 32, Sub-section 4 adds that beyond the mandatory sub-funds tied to specific instruments, the fund can establish additional specialized funds as needed for its business operations. This flexibility allows the fund to create new programs in response to emerging opportunities without requiring legislative amendment.

## Part 4: The Reserve Fund Requirements

Section 33 creates two mandatory reserve funds that the fund must maintain from its net profits:

### The General Reserve Fund (Sadharana Jageda Kosh)

The fund must set aside a minimum of 20% of net annual profit into the General Reserve Fund until the reserve reaches twice the paid-up capital amount. After that threshold is reached, the minimum contribution drops to 10% of net annual profit.

Let us put this in concrete terms. If the fund has paid-up capital of Rs. 25 billion, it must maintain a General Reserve Fund of at least Rs. 50 billion (twice the paid-up capital). Until this level is reached, at least 20% of each year's net profit goes into this reserve. Once the Rs. 50 billion level is reached, at least 10% of net annual profit must continue to flow into the reserve.

The General Reserve Fund is a cushion against future losses. If the fund has several years of poor investment performance — some loans default, some equity investments lose value — the reserve absorbs those losses rather than eroding the paid-up capital. This is the same principle behind a commercial bank's capital adequacy requirements: maintaining sufficient capital to absorb unexpected losses.

### The Free Reserve Fund (Svatantra Jageda Kosh)

After the General Reserve deduction, the fund may set aside additional amounts into a Free Reserve Fund. The specific amount is left to the fund's discretion (and is one of the provisions delegated to subordinate regulations).

The Free Reserve Fund is more flexible than the General Reserve. It can be used for:
- Issuing bonus shares to existing shareholders (increasing their shareholding without requiring additional cash investment)
- Other purposes as prescribed in the regulations

Bonus shares are a mechanism for rewarding shareholders with additional ownership without paying cash dividends. When the fund issues bonus shares to shareholders from its free reserves, the shareholders' proportional ownership in the fund increases, and the free reserves decrease correspondingly. This is a capital allocation decision — the fund is saying "instead of paying you cash, we are converting our reserves into additional equity that we issue to you."

## Part 5: Dividend Distribution — Who Gets What and When

Section 34 governs dividend distribution. Dividends are cash payments made to shareholders from the fund's profits. The provision states:

After the fund's net profits are used to:
1. Cover any losses from the previous year
2. Make the required contributions to the General Reserve Fund and Free Reserve Fund (as specified in Section 33)

...the remaining profits can be distributed as dividends to shareholders.

This structure creates a clear priority for the use of profits:
- First, losses must be made up
- Second, reserves must be topped up
- Third, dividends can be paid

This is standard responsible financial management. A fund that paid dividends without first covering losses and reserves would be deteriorating its financial health in order to provide short-term cash to shareholders — a form of financial imprudence that the Act explicitly prevents.

### Why Dividend Policy Matters for Development Finance

Dividend policy is a point of tension in any development finance institution. Shareholders — the government, the pension funds, the insurance companies — have an interest in receiving returns on their investment. The pension funds in particular, as managers of retirement savings, have a fiduciary duty to their beneficiaries to earn adequate returns. They are not charities; they need their investment in the fund to generate returns.

At the same time, the fund needs to retain capital for reinvestment. A development finance institution that pays out all its profits as dividends will not grow its balance sheet over time and will not be able to expand its investment activities. The trade-off between distributions to shareholders and reinvestment for growth is a fundamental financial management question.

The Act's answer — reserves first, then dividends — is conservative but appropriate for an institution in its early years. As the fund matures, builds track record, and accumulates reserves, the balance may shift toward higher dividend payouts. But in the early years, building the financial strength of the institution should take priority over maximizing shareholder distributions.

## Part 6: What the Financial Architecture Means for Long-Term Sustainability

Taken together, the Act's financial management provisions create an institution designed for long-term sustainability rather than short-term financial extraction.

The separate sub-fund structure ensures program-level transparency and accountability. The reserve requirements build a financial cushion against future losses. The dividend priority structure prevents financial imprudence. The mandate to maintain bank accounts at designated institutions with separate currency accounts ensures proper financial management.

These are positive features. But they are also features that depend on rigorous implementation. The history of DFI financial management globally includes many cases where:

- Reserve requirements were technically met on paper but the reserves were held in low-quality assets (like loans to related parties)
- Separate fund accounts existed on paper but were practically commingled
- Dividend distributions were manipulated by politically connected shareholders at the expense of the institution's financial health
- "Net profit" was defined in ways that hid losses and allowed excess distribution

The Act creates the right framework. Whether that framework is implemented faithfully depends on the quality of the internal financial management, the rigor of internal and external audit, and the willingness of oversight bodies to enforce compliance.

## Part 7: The Connection Between Financial Health and Mission Achievement

There is a deeper point worth making here. Development finance institutions sometimes fall into a trap of thinking that financial rigor and development mission are in tension — that being "too commercial" or "too focused on returns" compromises the development mission.

This view is wrong, and understanding why it is wrong is important.

A financially healthy DFI is better at its development mission than a financially weak one for several reasons:

**Capital preservation enables compounding.** If the fund earns a modest return on its investments and reinvests those earnings, its capital base grows over time. A Rs. 25 billion fund that earns 8% per year and reinvests half its earnings will have a much larger investment capacity in ten years than one that chronically underperforms or distributes all earnings.

**Financial strength enables risk-taking.** An institution with a strong capital base and healthy reserves can afford to take on more risk — investing in earlier-stage projects, taking first-loss positions in blended finance structures, guaranteeing more challenging projects. An institution with thin reserves must be conservative to protect its solvency, which means it will miss high-impact, higher-risk investments.

**Financial credibility attracts co-investment.** When the fund issues bonds or seeks co-investors, its financial health is the primary factor in investor confidence. A fund with strong financials, clean audit reports, and a track record of meeting its obligations will attract investment at better terms than a financially fragile institution. Better terms mean lower costs for the projects it finances.

**Financial discipline prevents capture.** An institution under financial stress is more vulnerable to political pressure — desperate for capital, more likely to accept politically motivated investments that come with implicit government support, more likely to tolerate governance shortcuts to secure financing. Financial strength reduces vulnerability.

The fund's financial architecture is not just good accounting practice. It is a foundation for mission effectiveness.
