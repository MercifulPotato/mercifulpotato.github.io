## The master glossary

Every important term from the series, defined in plain English. These are the words that let you understand any budget.

**Nepal's economy and money**

*Gross Domestic Product (GDP)* — the total value of everything a country produces in a year; the measure of the size of the economy, against which budget figures should be scaled. Nepal's is on the order of NPR 6 trillion (very roughly USD 40–46 billion).

*Remittance* — money sent home by people working abroad; for Nepal, a sum on the order of USD 9–11 billion a year, around a quarter or more of the economy, which drives consumption, imports, and (indirectly) much of the government's tax revenue.

*Nepalese rupee (NPR)* — Nepal's currency, *pegged* to the Indian rupee at a fixed rate of 1 Indian rupee = 1.60 Nepalese rupees, so its value against the US dollar moves only when the Indian rupee's does.

*Peg* — a fixed, officially maintained exchange rate between two currencies (here, the Nepalese and Indian rupees).

*Inflation* — the rate at which prices rise and money loses value over time. This budget targets a ceiling of 6 percent.

*Nominal versus real* — a *nominal* figure is in the money of the day, unadjusted; a *real* figure is adjusted for inflation, showing the actual quantity of resources commanded. Records and increases should be judged in real terms.

**Time and the budget cycle**

*Bikram Sambat (BS)* — Nepal's official calendar, roughly 56–57 years ahead of the Gregorian; the budget year 2083/84 BS is 2026/27 in Gregorian years.

*Fiscal year* — the twelve-month period a government uses for budgeting; Nepal's runs from around 16 July to around 16 July (the BS month of Shrawan to the end of Ashadh).

*Estimate, revised estimate, actual* — the *estimate* is the original plan set at the year's start; the *revised estimate* is the updated (usually lower) figure set partway through the year as reality asserts itself; the *actual* is what truly happened, confirmed afterward. Percentage comparisons depend critically on which is used.

**The structure of the budget**

*Budget* — a government's plan for raising and spending money over a year; simultaneously a *plan*, a *law*, and a *political statement*.

*Recurrent (current) expenditure* — money spent running the state — salaries, operations, interest, transfers — which leaves no durable asset; about 59.8 percent of this budget.

*Capital expenditure* — money spent building durable assets — roads, power, buildings; about 20.3 percent of this budget.

*Financing (financial management)* — money for repaying loan principal, on-lending, and investment; neither running the state nor building assets; about 19.9 percent of this budget.

*Appropriation Bill / Act* — the law authorising the government to spend; its detailed breakdown is the *Red Book*.

*Finance Bill / Act* — the law giving legal force to tax changes; called the Finance Bill 2083 until passed, then the Finance Act 2083.

*Red Book* — the volume of detailed expenditure estimates; where the binding spending figures live.

**Revenue, deficit, and debt**

*Revenue* — the government's own income, chiefly from taxes; this budget targets NPR 1,405.31 billion.

*Tax versus non-tax revenue* — *tax* revenue is compulsory levies (the great bulk); *non-tax* is fees, dividends, royalties, and the like.

*Deficit* — the gap between spending and revenue-plus-grants, which must be borrowed; this budget's is about NPR 657 billion. A *flow* (this year's borrowing).

*Public debt* — the accumulated stock of past borrowing not yet repaid; a *stock*, on the order of 40–45 percent of GDP for Nepal.

*Grant* — money given that need not be repaid (a gift).

*Loan* — borrowed money that must be repaid with interest.

*Concessional loan* — a loan on terms far better than commercial (very low interest, long maturity); much of Nepal's foreign debt.

*Crowding out* — when heavy government borrowing from the domestic pool leaves less credit, at higher rates, for the private sector.

*Currency risk* — the danger that a foreign-currency loan costs more to repay in rupees if the rupee weakens.

*Rolling over (debt)* — borrowing afresh to repay maturing debt; why gross borrowing exceeds the net increase in debt.

**Taxes**

*Income tax* — tax on what people earn and companies profit; this budget doubled the personal exemption to NPR 1,000,000 and cut the top rate from 39 to 29 percent.

*Slab (bracket, band)* — a portion of income taxed at a particular rate, with rates rising for higher slabs.

*Marginal rate versus effective rate* — the *marginal* rate is the rate on your topmost slice of income (the highest slab you reach); the *effective* rate is the share of your *whole* income actually paid in tax, which is lower because lower slabs are taxed less. Confusing the two overestimates what high earners pay.

*Progressive versus regressive* — a *progressive* tax takes a larger share from those who earn more; a *regressive* tax takes a larger share from those who earn less. Income tax is progressive; consumption taxes are regressive.

*Consumption tax* — a tax on what you spend, built into prices; the largest source of Nepal's revenue, and regressive.

*Value-added tax (VAT)* — Nepal's main consumption tax, standard rate 13 percent, much of it collected at the border on imports.

*Customs duty (tariff)* — tax on imported goods, serving both revenue and protection of domestic industry; this budget cut the tiers from 11 to 7 and reduced duty on 273 raw materials.

*Excise duty* — a selective tax on particular goods (alcohol, tobacco, vehicles); this budget abolished it on 360 goods.

*Corporate tax* — tax on company profits; 25 percent standard, 30 percent for banks, insurance, telecom, tobacco, and alcohol.

*Green Tax* — a new levy consolidating the former infrastructure development tax and road maintenance fee, collected at customs; its "green" substance is debatable.

**Federalism**

*Federalism* — the division of governmental power among multiple tiers; Nepal has three: federal, 7 provinces, 753 local governments.

*Fiscal equalization grant* — unconditional money transferred to lower tiers to spend on their own priorities and to reduce regional disparities.

*Conditional grant* — money transferred for a specific, centrally-directed purpose (tied money); in this budget, conditional grants to local governments far exceed unconditional ones.

*Revenue sharing* — the rule-based division of certain taxes and royalties among the tiers.

**Judging the budget**

*Execution rate (absorption)* — the share of allocated money actually spent; chronically low for capital spending in Nepal. The truest measure of whether a budget was real.

*Execution gap* — the chronic shortfall of actual spending and revenue against the plan.

*Announced, enacted, implemented* — the three stages every budget provision must travel: *announced* in the speech (an intention), *enacted* into law (an authority), *implemented* in reality (an outcome). They are not the same.

*Inputs versus outcomes* — *inputs* are what is spent and built (rupees, hospitals, enrolment); *outcomes* are what is achieved (health, learning, served people). Inputs do not guarantee outcomes.

## The citizen's checklist: how to track the promises

The budget is a set of promises made in mid-2026. Here is how to find out, for yourself, over the coming year, which of them become real — the practical application of reading like an auditor.

**Where to find the authoritative information.** The *Ministry of Finance* (mof.gov.np) publishes the budget speech, the Red Book of detailed allocations, and mid-term review documents — the source for what was promised and allocated. The *Financial Comptroller General Office* (fcgo.gov.np) publishes actual-expenditure and revenue data — the source for what was actually spent and collected, and thus for the all-important *execution rates*. The *Office of the Auditor General* (oagnep.gov.np) publishes annual audit reports — the independent verdict on how money was actually used, including irregularities. *Nepal Rastra Bank* (nrb.org.np) publishes data on the economy, inflation, remittances, imports, debt, and the exchange rate. The *Inland Revenue Department* (ird.gov.np) publishes the tax circulars with the operational detail of tax changes. And reputable independent reporting and analysis fill in what the official sources do not.

**How to track a specific promise.** For any promise that matters to you — a hospital, a road, a programme, a coverage target — work through the stages: First, confirm it is actually *enacted* — find its specific allocation in the Red Book, not just its mention in the speech. Second, distinguish *input from outcome* — identify what would count as the promise actually delivered (functioning hospitals, not just buildings; people actually treated, not just enrolled). Third, watch the *execution* — as the year progresses, check whether the allocated money is actually being spent, from the Financial Comptroller General Office's data. Fourth, check for *re-announcement* — has this promise appeared in previous budgets, and what happened to it? Fifth, judge by the *outcome over time* — over the coming year and years, track what was actually built, what actually functions, what actually reached people, using the official data, the audit findings, and independent reporting. This is how you audit a promise: follow it from announcement through allocation through spending to outcome, using public evidence, and judge by what actually happens.

**The questions to ask of the whole budget, at year's end.** When the fiscal year ends in mid-2027, ask: What was the *execution rate*, especially for *capital* spending — how much of the building budget was actually spent? Did *revenue* meet its target, or fall short? Was the budget *revised down* at mid-year, and by how much? What did the *Auditor General* find about how the money was used? Did the *growth* assumption (7 percent) hold, or did the economy grow closer to its recent ~3.85 percent? And, promise by promise, what was actually delivered against what was announced? These questions, answered from the public data, are how you judge whether this budget was real — and they are within reach of any citizen willing to verify rather than trust.

## The twelve-month watch calendar

What to watch over the year following this budget, to see whether it becomes real. (Dates and events should be confirmed as they approach; this is a guide to the rhythm of the budget year.)

*The start of the fiscal year (around mid-July 2026)* — the budget takes effect; watch whether spending, especially capital spending, actually begins, or whether the usual slow start sets in. The tax changes take effect; watch for the Inland Revenue Department's circulars giving the operational detail of the income-tax slabs and other changes.

*Monetary policy (typically mid-to-late July, after the budget)* — Nepal Rastra Bank issues its monetary policy for the year, which sets the framework for credit, interest rates, and the financial sector that the budget's plans depend on; watch whether it aligns with or strains against the budget's assumptions.

*The early and middle months* — watch the pace of capital spending and revenue collection through the official data; the chronic pattern is slow capital spending and the risk of revenue shortfall, which would foreshadow a mid-year revision.

*The mid-year review (around the middle of the fiscal year)* — the government reviews and often *revises* the budget; watch whether it is revised *down* (the usual pattern as optimistic assumptions fail), and by how much, which reveals how realistic the original plan was.

*Throughout the year* — watch the progress of the flagship promises: the hospitals, the highways, the health-insurance coverage, the AI compute centre, the reforms — tracking actual delivery against announcement. Watch for re-announcements of stalled projects. Watch the exchange rate and the remittance and import data, which drive revenue. And watch for the privatisation moves (such as the Nepal Telecom share sale) and how they are actually conducted.

*The end of the fiscal year (around mid-July 2027)* — the moment of truth: the actual outturn. Watch the final execution rates (how much was actually spent, especially of capital), the actual revenue against target, and the actual delivery of promises. This is when the budget's reality, as opposed to its announcement, becomes fully visible.

*Afterward* — the *Auditor General's report* on how the money was actually used, the independent verdict that reveals irregularities and the true quality of the spending.

## The balanced verdict

We have weighed every part of this budget on its merits. Here, drawn together, is the balanced final assessment — the genuinely good, the genuinely risky, and the things to stay skeptical about — offered not as a partisan judgement but as a fair summary of what the series found.

**The genuinely good.** This budget contains real merits that deserve recognition. The *income-tax relief* for the salaried middle class, through the doubled exemption, is genuine relief for people squeezed by rising prices. The *customs simplification* (11 tiers to 7) is a real, sensible administrative reform. The *formalisation measures* — the digital-payment VAT refund, the VAT-bill lottery — are clever, well-designed attempts to bring the informal economy into the tax net, the most credible part of the answer to collecting more while cutting rates. The *IT-export and sweat-equity incentives* are well-targeted at a genuine, geography-suited opportunity. The *legal reforms* to company, insolvency, and venture-capital law address real gaps in the business environment. The *export incentives* target a genuine national weakness. Several *social commitments* — the doubled nutrition allowance for marginalised children, free childhood-cancer treatment, support for the disabled and for returnee migrants — are humane and worthy. The *energy focus* plays to Nepal's greatest economic strength. And the *tourism* promotion plays to a real asset. The overall *direction* — toward a more modern, open, enterprise-friendly Nepal that invests in its people and its strengths — is, in many respects, sound, and the involvement of serious economic expertise shows in the more sophisticated measures.

**The genuinely risky.** Other parts carry real risks that deserve caution. The *income-tax cut*, while relieving the middle, also delivers its largest absolute benefit to high earners (through the top-rate cut) and costs revenue in a deficit budget, possibly shifting the burden toward the regressive consumption taxes that fall hardest on the poor. The *revenue target*, set above the recent record while cutting rates, is optimistic and may fall short. The *deficit and rising debt*, much of it not translated into productive assets, are a concern in trajectory if not yet in level. The *recurrent ratchet* — the roughly 21 percent pay rise — permanently raises the running costs in a budget already too heavy on recurrent and too light on capital. The *electricity tax* contradicts the electrification strategy. And the chronic *execution gap* threatens to leave the capital spending and the ambitious promises substantially undelivered, as it has for every recent government.

**The things to stay skeptical about.** And some parts warrant outright skepticism. The *"sovereign AI compute centre"* is, on the evidence of its tiny funding and formidable constraints, largely a buzzword — a shiny object sized for headlines, not a serious funded commitment. The *agricultural-investment grant*, with its NPR 2 crore floor, excludes the smallholders it is presented as helping and channels public money to the prosperous. The *Green Tax* may be largely a rebranding of existing levies, its environmental substance debatable. The large *opaque "miscellaneous" allocation* (about NPR 90.42 billion) undermines the transparency that is a budget's purpose. The *7 percent growth target*, against 3.85 percent reality, is a credibility gap at the foundation. And the many *announced reforms and promises* — the hospitals, the one-stop investment system, the health coverage — face the chronic Nepali pattern of announcement outrunning implementation, and should be credited only as they are actually delivered.

**The overall judgement.** The fairest overall judgement is *conditional*, and it rests on the distinction this series has insisted upon throughout: this is a budget with genuinely good intentions and several genuinely sound measures, written by a new government with real expertise and a real mandate, *but* it shares the chronic weaknesses of its predecessors — the execution gap, the optimistic assumptions, the opacity, the gap between ambitious announcement and likely delivery — and it contains its share of buzzwords, regressive tilts, and promises that may not be kept. Whether it proves a good budget depends entirely on *execution*: on whether this government can do what its predecessors could not — actually spend the capital, actually collect the revenue, actually deliver the promises, actually narrow the gap between plan and achievement. The clean slate offers a real chance; the chronic pattern and the deep constraints offer no assurance. And so the verdict is not a verdict at all, but a *charge to watch*: to judge this budget not by the hope of its mandate or the goodness of its intentions, but by what it actually delivers over the coming year, measured against what it promised, using the public evidence and the tools this series has given you. The budget made the promises in mid-2026. Only the year that follows — and your own auditing of it — will reveal which were real.

## The spot-the-spin cheat sheet

The complete toolkit, in one screen, for cutting through any budget's framing. When you encounter the claim, ask the question.

*"Largest budget in history"* → Larger in real terms after inflation? Will it actually be spent?
*A percentage increase* → Compared to which base — original, revised, or actual?
*A defence citing the election win* → Is this about authority, or about whether the numbers add up?
*A promised programme* → Announced, enacted, or actually implemented?
*"Investment" / "development"* → Will it leave a durable, productive asset?
*A revenue target* → On what growth and import assumptions? What is the recent record?
*A modest-looking deficit* → Reconstruct it: spending minus revenue minus grants.
*"Empowering local government"* → How much of the money is unconditional versus tied?
*"Tax relief"* → Who actually benefits, and who actually pays?
*"Simplification" / "green" / "welfare"* → What is the distributional effect? What is the actual substance behind the label?
*Announced reforms* → Tracked to actual implementation? Are the deep constraints addressed?
*Energy capacity built* → Is the power actually delivered, or stranded for lack of transmission?
*An infrastructure achievement* → Completed, functioning, and durable? Announced before?
*Spending on health or education* → What are the outcomes, not just the inputs?
*A benefit for the vulnerable* → Is there a delivery mechanism? Does it actually reach the poorest?
*A subsidy for a broad group* → What are the eligibility conditions, and who do they exclude?
*A visionary announcement* → Is it funded commensurately? Are the constraints addressed? Sized for impact or headlines?

And beneath all of them, the one question that defeats every deception: **not what does the budget say, but what is actually true and what actually happens?** Be the auditor, not the audience.

And you now have everything you need — the understanding, the numbers, the sources, the calendar, the toolkit, and above all the discipline — to read that data for yourself, to hold this budget to account on its delivery rather than its announcement, and to do the same for every budget that follows. The series ends here. The watching begins now. Keep this guide; use it; and never again be the audience for a budget when you can be its auditor.

## A Note on Numbers and Assumptions

All US dollar figures in this guide use our single assumed rate of **1 US dollar = 150 Nepalese rupees**, an approximation close to the Nepal Rastra Bank reference rate in mid-2026; the Nepalese rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee at a fixed 1.60 rupees per Indian rupee, so its value against the dollar moves only when the Indian rupee's does, and it drifts daily. Treat every dollar figure as intuition, not accounting.

All rupee figures are nominal — in the money of the day, not adjusted for inflation — unless explicitly stated otherwise. Every figure in this guide is drawn from Nepal's FY 2083/84 budget and the analysis of the preceding nineteen posts, and should be confirmed against the official Ministry of Finance documents, the Finance Act 2083, and the supporting sources; some figures reported in secondary sources are rounded differently from the official Red Book, and items such as the previous year's revised estimate, the exact ministry allocations, the income-tax slab table, and the precise definitions of sector versus ministry should be taken from the authoritative sources. Where this series has flagged a figure as uncertain or to be verified, that caution carries into this guide.

And the assumption that has anchored all twenty posts, offered here as the final and most important word: a figure that has been *announced* in a budget is not the same as one that has been *enacted* into law, which is not the same as money actually *spent* and turned into something real. Judge a budget — this one, and every one — by what it actually delivers, not by what it announces or how it frames itself. Be the auditor, not the audience. That discipline, applied with the toolkit and the public evidence this guide provides, is the whole of holding a budget to account — and it is now yours.
