Sometime around 2007 or 2008, something quietly shifted in Nepal's national accounts. For the first time in recorded history, remittances — the money sent home by Nepali workers employed abroad — exceeded the combined value of Nepal's merchandise exports. The country that had once survived economically on agriculture, tourism, and foreign aid discovered that its most reliable export was no longer a physical product or a destination; it was people. Specifically, it was the labor of millions of Nepali men and women who had traveled to the Persian Gulf, Malaysia, Japan, South Korea, and dozens of other countries to work in construction, manufacturing, domestic service, and a wide range of other sectors, and who were sending a substantial fraction of their earnings back to families in Nepal.

By the mid-2020s, Nepal had become one of the most remittance-dependent economies on Earth, in a way that made economists simultaneously concerned and impressed. Concerned because an economy that depends on exporting its working-age population to finance its consumption is not, on the face of it, pursuing a development model that builds domestic productive capacity. Impressed because the scale and consistency of remittance inflows had provided a degree of macroeconomic stability — keeping foreign reserves adequate, funding import demand, supporting household incomes — that would not otherwise have been possible. The remittance system was doing something that Nepal's formal economy was failing to do: reliably providing foreign exchange and income at scale.

**The Scale and Structure of Nepali Migration**

The numbers are large enough that they require some context to process properly. Nepal's total population is approximately 30 million people. The best estimates suggest that between 4 and 5 million Nepali citizens are working abroad at any given time — meaning somewhere between 13 and 17 percent of the entire population is overseas for work. The working-age population is perhaps 15 to 18 million people, which means that something approaching a quarter to a third of Nepal's working-age citizens are employed outside the country. No other major South or Southeast Asian country approaches this proportion.

Where do Nepali workers go? The destinations fall into several tiers. The largest single destination cluster, absorbing approximately 1.5 to 2 million workers at peak, is the Persian Gulf states — Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman. These countries are among the world's wealthiest per capita, their ruling families have pursued infrastructure and construction programs of extraordinary ambition, and they have needed a massive imported labor force to realize those ambitions. Nepali workers have filled a significant fraction of this demand, working primarily in construction, security, hospitality, and domestic service. The conditions in Gulf employment have been a persistent source of concern for human rights organizations, with the kafala system — a labor arrangement that ties a worker's legal status to their employer — creating vulnerabilities to exploitation that have been widely documented. The deaths of Nepali workers in Qatar during the World Cup stadium construction projects were the most internationally visible manifestation of conditions that have existed across Gulf labor markets for decades.

Malaysia is the second major destination, with something between 300,000 and 500,000 Nepali workers at various points, employed primarily in manufacturing and construction. Japan and South Korea have absorbed growing numbers through official skills-based programs. Other significant destinations include India — where migration is informal, unrestricted by the open border, and likely underestimated in official statistics — the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, and the United States for higher-skilled migrants, and a scattering of other countries for various specialized occupations including the Gurkha military service that has historically placed Nepali soldiers in the British and Indian armies.

The remittance flows from these workers back to Nepal are substantial by any measure. Total formal remittances are estimated at roughly 8 to 9 billion US dollars per year in recent years, representing approximately 23 to 27 percent of Nepal's GDP. In practical terms, this means that for every four rupees of economic activity in Nepal, one rupee is funded by money sent home from abroad. The informal channels — cash carried by travelers, transfers through the hawala network, payments in kind — add an additional and essentially unmeasurable amount on top of the formal figures. Total remittances including informal flows are probably somewhere between 25 and 35 percent of GDP.

**How the Money Moves**

Understanding how remittance money actually travels from a construction site in Doha or a factory floor in Kuala Lumpur to a village in Palpa or Jumla requires understanding a financial system that operates in parallel to, and sometimes in tension with, the formal banking system.

The formal channel for remittances is the network of licensed money transfer operators — Western Union, IME, Prabhu Money Transfer, and dozens of other licensed operators — that partner with banks and financial institutions on both ends of the transfer. A Nepali worker in Qatar goes to a money transfer office, pays in riyals, the operator's system credits a corresponding amount in rupees (minus a fee and an exchange rate margin) to an account or a payable-to-cash arrangement in Nepal, and the worker's family collects at a partner bank or agent location. This system is well-developed in Nepal and relatively affordable for low-value transfers, with fees having dropped significantly over the past decade as competition among operators increased and as central banks pushed for lower-cost remittance corridors.

The informal channel — the hawala system — operates on a network of trust between financial intermediaries in origin and destination countries. A worker gives money to a hawaladar in Qatar, who communicates via phone to a partner hawaladar in Nepal, who pays out an equivalent amount in rupees from funds already held in Nepal. Settlement between the two hawaladars happens through various mechanisms — import/export invoicing, physical cash transport, or accumulated balances — on a periodic basis. Hawala is faster, often cheaper, more flexible, and completely unregulated. Its persistence alongside the formal system reflects both the gaps in formal system coverage — particularly for workers in informal employment who may not have documentation adequate for formal financial services — and the efficiency advantages that a well-run trust network can achieve over a regulated institution with compliance costs.

For the purposes of the Alternative Development Finance Fund, what matters is that the total pool of remittance flows is enormous, that most of it flows into consumption rather than investment, that a significant fraction currently bypasses the formal financial system entirely, and that this represents, in principle, a large and latent pool of investable capital if the right instruments and incentives can be created.

**Why Remittances Flow to Consumption, Not Investment**

The pattern of remittance use in Nepal is well-documented and follows a pattern common to remittance-dependent economies globally. The most rigorous household surveys consistently find that the majority of remittance income is used for current consumption — food, clothing, medical expenses, education fees, household goods — and for debt repayment, with a significant fraction of migrant families having borrowed to fund the migration itself, including paying fees to recruitment agencies. A smaller fraction goes into housing construction and improvement, which is investment in the sense that it creates a durable asset but does not produce an economic return. An even smaller fraction goes into productive investment — business formation, agricultural equipment, formal financial savings.

This is not irrational behavior by the families receiving the money. Migration is expensive and risky. Workers take on debt to pay recruitment fees and travel costs, often at high interest rates from informal lenders. The first priority of remittance income is therefore debt service. Once debts are cleared, the family's immediate consumption needs — often significantly deferred during the period when they were in debt — take priority. Housing investment follows, particularly in rural Nepal where the traditional housing stock is often inadequate and where owning a modern house is a significant social marker. Formal financial savings and business investment come later, if at all, and for many families the uncertainty about whether the migration income will continue — contracts typically run for two to three years, renewal is uncertain — makes long-term investment seem risky compared to immediate consumption.

The recruitment fee problem deserves specific mention because it shapes the financial situation of migrants from the first moment of departure. Nepal has legally established limits on the fees that recruitment agents can charge to workers seeking employment in certain destination countries, but enforcement is imperfect, and in practice many workers pay fees far in excess of legal limits. A worker who has borrowed 200,000 to 500,000 Nepali rupees to pay a recruitment agency and fund travel to Qatar enters the migration with a debt equivalent to a year's or more worth of savings at their destination wage. The first year or more of remittances therefore flows directly into debt repayment to rural informal lenders, with no benefit to any formal financial institution or any productive investment.

**Precedents: India, Israel, and Ethiopia**

The remittance fund mechanism authorized in the Alternative Development Finance Fund legislation is not an invention. It draws on precedents from three countries that have successfully used diaspora financing instruments to mobilize capital at scale for domestic investment.

India's experience is the most directly relevant and the most extensively studied. India has issued three series of non-resident Indian bonds — the India Development Bonds in 1991, the Resurgent India Bonds in 1998, and the India Millennium Deposits in 2000 — each during periods of foreign exchange crisis when India needed to rebuild its reserves. Each bond issue raised between 4 and 5 billion US dollars in a matter of months, far exceeding government expectations. The bonds were sold to Non-Resident Indians and Persons of Indian Origin — the Indian diaspora — at interest rates slightly above comparable international instruments and with foreign currency denomination, meaning the investor was protected against rupee depreciation. The State Bank of India served as the primary distribution channel. The success of these instruments demonstrated that diaspora investors, given a credible issuer, a reasonable return premium reflecting their affinity for the home country, and a foreign-currency option that removes exchange rate risk, will participate in large numbers even though they have no legal obligation to do so and have many alternative investment options in their countries of residence.

India subsequently developed the more permanent Non-Resident Indian deposit scheme — now comprising several categories including NRE (Non-Resident External) and NRO (Non-Resident Ordinary) accounts at Indian banks — that have consistently mobilized tens of billions of dollars of diaspora savings into the Indian banking system. The lesson is that diaspora financing at scale requires not a one-off bond issue but a permanent infrastructure of products, regulations, distribution channels, and trust-building that makes diaspora investment routine rather than exceptional.

Israel's experience with Diaspora bonds — issued by the State of Israel through the Development Corporation for Israel since 1951 — is the longest continuous example in the world. Israel has raised over 40 billion dollars from the Jewish diaspora since 1951 through instruments sold primarily in the United States but also in other countries with large Jewish communities. The bonds have been consistently below-market in terms of yield — they offer lower returns than comparable sovereign bonds in the market — and have sold successfully for seven decades because they carry a non-financial return in the form of a connection to Israel's development and a form of identity expression for diaspora investors. The Israel precedent suggests that for communities with strong emotional ties to the home country, diaspora bonds can succeed even without market-competitive returns.

Ethiopia's Diaspora Bond program, launched in 2008 and revised several times since, is the most directly relevant developing-country precedent for Nepal. The Ethiopian Diaspora Bond was issued to finance specific infrastructure projects — initially including the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam — at interest rates below international market rates but above Ethiopian domestic rates. The bond program had mixed success: it raised meaningful amounts, demonstrated viability of the instrument in a low-income country context, but fell short of the most ambitious projections, in part because the foreign exchange management regime and concerns about Ethiopian sovereign risk dampened investor appetite. The Ethiopia precedent is instructive in both its successes and its limitations: diaspora bonds can work in a country at Nepal's development level, but they require careful attention to currency risk management, credible commitment mechanisms, and transparent communication about how the funds will be used.

**The Fund's Remittance Mechanism**

The Alternative Development Finance Fund Act authorizes the Fund to raise capital through what the legislation calls a "remittance fund" — an instrument through which Nepali workers abroad can invest a portion of their earnings directly into the Fund, which will then deploy those funds in infrastructure projects. The legislation is somewhat general on the precise mechanics, leaving implementation design to the Fund's management, but the broad structure involves the Fund issuing rupee-denominated bonds or investment instruments to non-resident Nepali workers, channeling that capital into the Fund's investment portfolio, and distributing returns — interest or dividends — back to the investors over the instrument's term.

The key design questions are those that all diaspora financing instruments must address. First, currency risk: a worker sending money from Qatar is earning riyals, converting to rupees for investment in Nepal, and hoping to receive rupee returns that, when converted back to riyals or dollars at the end of the investment period, represent a positive real return. If the rupee depreciates significantly against the riyal during the investment period — as Nepali currency has historically tended to do against major currencies over long periods — the investor may find that their rupee return does not compensate for the exchange rate loss. Addressing this requires either offering foreign-currency denomination (which shifts the exchange rate risk to the Fund), offering a return premium large enough to compensate for expected depreciation, or providing some form of exchange rate guarantee.

Second, distribution infrastructure: reaching Nepali workers in Qatar, Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, and dozens of other countries requires a distribution mechanism — financial intermediaries in those countries who can sell the instrument, accept payment, and handle the account management for potentially millions of small-value investors. This is not trivial. The current remittance infrastructure is well-developed for one-way flows of relatively small transfers, but investment product distribution to migrant workers requires different infrastructure, including account management, performance reporting, and eventually redemption processing.

Third, trust and credibility: a Nepali worker with modest savings decides to invest them in a Fund instrument rather than keeping the money in savings at their destination-country bank, or sending it home for a family member to manage, based on their trust in the Fund's honesty and operational competence. Building that trust requires a track record, transparent reporting on how invested funds have performed, and a regulatory structure that provides recourse if something goes wrong. The Fund, being new, will need to build this trust incrementally, probably starting with smaller pilots before attempting to mobilize funds at the scale that the theoretical diaspora pool would suggest.

The potential pool is genuinely large. If five million Nepali workers abroad each invested the equivalent of 100,000 rupees — a relatively modest sum for someone with two or three years of Gulf earnings — the total capital mobilized would be 500 billion rupees, or roughly twenty times the Fund's initial authorized capital. This is an upper bound that makes strong assumptions about participation rates, but even at a one percent participation rate the potential mobilization would be significant. The structural opportunity is real. The execution challenge is substantial. The Fund's success in actually mobilizing this capital will be one of the most watched and most consequential tests of its capabilities.
