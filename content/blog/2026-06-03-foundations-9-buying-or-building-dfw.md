# Part 9 of 10 — Buying or Building in DFW: Everything You Need to Know About Expansive Clay and Foundations in Dallas-Fort Worth

Dallas-Fort Worth is one of the most complex residential foundation environments in the United States. Not because the loads are unusual, not because the seismic hazard is high, not because of frost or floods — but because of the clay. Specifically, because of smectite, the mineral that makes North Texas soil expand when wet and shrink when dry, and that has been causing foundation problems in the region as long as concrete slabs have been poured on top of it.

This article is for three audiences: the person buying a home in DFW who wants to understand the foundation inspection report, the person planning to build a new home who wants to understand what they are paying for when the engineer specifies a post-tensioned slab, and the homeowner who wants to know how to maintain their existing slab foundation and extend its life. All three groups are dealing with the same underlying geological reality, and understanding it changes how you make decisions.

## Part 1: The Geology — Why DFW Clay Is Different

### The Austin Chalk and Taylor Marl

The bedrock of the Dallas-Fort Worth Metroplex and much of north-central Texas consists primarily of two Upper Cretaceous formations:

**The Austin Chalk** is a white to light gray chalky limestone and marl deposited approximately 90 to 83 million years ago in a shallow inland sea. It outcrops across a northeast-trending band through the center of the DFW Metroplex — roughly from Waxahachie northeast through Dallas, toward the Oklahoma border. It is relatively hard, fractures in joints, and weathers to a thin residual soil.

**The Taylor Marl** (also called the Taylor Group or Taylor Clay) is a dark gray to black chalky marl and clay deposited slightly later in the same Cretaceous sea. It underlies much of the western portion of the Metroplex (Fort Worth, Arlington, Grand Prairie) and outcrops in areas where the Austin Chalk is absent or has been eroded.

Both units weather at the surface to produce highly plastic, dark clay soils — the famous black cotton soils of North Texas — that are predominantly montmorillonite (smectite) group clay minerals.

### Smectite — The Expanding Clay Mineral

Clay minerals are sheet silicates — microscopic plate-like crystals with enormous surface area relative to their volume. The different clay mineral groups have different crystal structures, and this determines their engineering behavior.

**Smectite (montmorillonite)**: A 2:1 clay mineral with a crystal structure that permits water molecules and cations to enter between the crystal layers, expanding the crystal lattice itself. This interlayer expansion is what makes smectite so dangerous for foundations. A smectite-rich clay can:

- Expand in volume by 25% to 50% as it wets from dry to saturated
- Exert swelling pressures of 10,000 to 40,000 psf (70 to 280 kPa) against any confining structure
- Shrink dramatically as it dries, producing deep desiccation cracks (called "dessication cracks") that extend vertically down 3 to 8+ feet

**For comparison**: Kaolinite, the dominant clay mineral in the Southeast United States (including Hampton Roads, Virginia), has essentially no interlayer expansion capacity. A kaolinite clay may have a plasticity index (PI) of 8 to 15. A Dallas smectite clay has a PI of 25 to 60 — the same volume of clay mineral holds dramatically more water and swells far more.

The Atterberg limits for typical DFW black clay:
- Liquid Limit (LL): 60 to 100
- Plastic Limit (PL): 25 to 35
- Plasticity Index (PI): 35 to 65

These values place DFW soils firmly in the "high" to "very high" plasticity category on the Casagrande plasticity chart — the CH classification. Compare to Houston's Beaumont Clay (similar PI), Atlanta's residual soils (PI 10 to 20), or Raleigh's Piedmont soils (PI 8 to 15).

### The Active Zone

Not all of this clay is active. The term **active zone** refers to the depth to which soil moisture fluctuates seasonally — the zone where wet seasons raise moisture content and dry seasons lower it. Below the active zone, soil moisture is essentially constant year-round because it is beyond the influence of surface evaporation, plant root extraction, and rainfall infiltration.

In North Texas, the active zone is typically 5 to 15 feet deep, with significant local variation depending on:
- Vegetation: Trees with deep roots (oak, pecan, elm) extend root drying significantly deeper — active zones of 10 to 20 feet under large trees are documented
- Drainage: Well-drained sites dry out more; poor drainage creates perennially wet zones
- Irrigation: Landscaping irrigation during dry summers maintains moisture and reduces amplitude of moisture variation
- Impervious cover: Concrete and asphalt slow evaporation and runoff, moderating moisture changes

Below the active zone, the clay maintains a relatively constant moisture content (near its "shrinkage limit" in dry years) and a relatively constant volume. Foundation elements that extend below the active zone — drilled piers — are therefore stable. Foundation elements that are entirely within the active zone — conventional shallow footings — move with the soil.

### Seasonal Movement Pattern

The classic DFW slab movement pattern follows the annual moisture cycle:

**Spring (February–May)**: Rain increases soil moisture, clay swells. The slab heaves upward, particularly at the edges (which have greater moisture variation than the center, which is protected from direct rain by the slab above). Doors that fit perfectly in winter may stick in spring as frames rack slightly from differential heave.

**Summer (June–September)**: Drought and evapotranspiration dry the soil. Clay shrinks. The slab drops, particularly at the edges. Cracks in the soil appear adjacent to the foundation as the clay pulls away from the slab edge.

**Fall (October–November)**: Rains begin again; soil re-wets; cycle repeats.

**Center Lift vs. Edge Drop**: When the center of the slab is drier than the edges (less common, usually from interior plumbing leaks or tree roots drawing moisture from the center), the center drops and the edges stay elevated — "center lift" failure. More commonly, the edges dry and drop relative to the center — "edge drop" — which is the opposite and more typical pattern.

The cumulative effect of thousands of these seasonal cycles, plus long-term drying trends (drought periods), plus localized moisture variations from landscaping, plumbing leaks, trees, and drainage — is why DFW foundation movement is essentially universal and why nearly every home in the region will show some foundation movement over its lifetime.

## Part 2: Post-Tensioned Slabs — How They Work and Why They Dominate DFW

### The Problem with Conventional Slabs on Expansive Clay

A conventional reinforced concrete slab — rebar in a grid, no prestress — relies on the concrete's tensile strength (from the rebar) to resist bending. But bending in a flat slab requires the slab to deflect first, and the forces from expansive clay (both swelling and shrinking) far exceed the capacity of conventional reinforcement to prevent deflection.

A typical 5-inch reinforced concrete slab with #4 rebar at 12 inches will crack under differential movement of 0.5 to 1.0 inch across a 20-foot span. In DFW, seasonal differential movement of 1 to 3 inches across a typical house is common. The math is straightforward: conventional slabs crack.

The crack pattern from edge drop: diagonal cracks from corners toward the center, and longitudinal cracks along the walls. The crack pattern from center lift: longitudinal cracks along the center of the slab where tension develops on top.

### Post-Tensioning — The Solution

As described in Article 3, post-tensioning actively compresses the slab before any movement occurs. For DFW specifically, the relevant design standard is the **Post-Tensioning Institute's PTI DC80.3** — "Design of Post-Tensioned Slabs-on-Ground," which has a specific provisions for expansive soil.

PTI DC80.3 was developed specifically to address residential slab design on expansive clay. It uses a soil model (the PTI soil parameters: **ym** — maximum soil movement at the slab edge, **em** — edge moisture variation distance) derived from soil testing and regional climate data.

**How the PTI design works**:

1. **Geotechnical testing**: Soil borings and laboratory tests determine the key parameters:
   - Expansion Index (EI) per ASTM D4829
   - Swell test results (percent swell and swell pressure under various surcharge conditions)
   - Active zone depth estimate
   - PTI parameters ym and em

2. **Climate zone**: DFW falls in PTI Climate Zone 2 (hot and dry), which specifies design moisture variation parameters.

3. **Slab design**: Given the soil parameters and climate zone, PTI DC80.3 provides a calculation procedure that determines:
   - Required slab thickness (typically 4 to 5 inches for residential)
   - Required tendon layout — the density and pattern of post-tensioning tendons
   - Required concrete strength (minimum 3,000 psi; typically 3,500 to 4,000 psi in DFW)
   - Required perimeter and interior stiffening beams (thickened ribs that increase slab rigidity)

4. **Slab action**: The slab is designed to span across zones of differential soil movement without cracking. Under "edge drop" conditions, the slab acts as a beam bridging the unsupported perimeter. Under "center lift" conditions, the slab acts as a reverse beam with the soil heaved beneath the center. In both cases, the post-tensioning compression prevents cracking.

**Typical DFW post-tensioned slab specifications**:
- Slab thickness: 4 to 5 inches (thinner than a conventional slab due to the post-tension stiffness)
- Tendons: 0.5-inch diameter, 270 ksi, 7-wire low-relaxation strand, at approximately 4 to 6 feet on center each way (final spacing depends on PTI design)
- Initial prestress force: approximately 33,000 lbs per tendon after stressing
- Perimeter beam: 12 to 18 inches deep × 12 inches wide, with additional rebar; runs along the full perimeter
- Interior stiffening beams (ribs): 12 to 18 inches deep × 12 inches wide at approximately 15 to 20 feet on center, in both directions; creates a ribbed slab on expansive soil
- Concrete: 3,500 to 4,000 psi, placed and cured carefully (DFW summer heat requires special curing precautions)

**Post-tensioning installation on site**:

1. Fine-graded subgrade, topsoil removed, vapor retarder placed
2. Interior sand cushion (3 to 4 inches of clean sand) is placed between the vapor retarder and the slab bottom — this sand layer allows the slab to slide slightly relative to the soil when the soil moves, preventing direct drag forces on the slab
3. Perimeter forms set; thickened beam excavations made
4. Rebar placed in beam zones; tendon chairs positioned; tendons laid across the slab in the specified pattern
5. Concrete placed; forms stripped after 12 to 24 hours (concrete strength sufficient for early form removal)
6. Stressing: 3 to 7 days after pour (when concrete reaches approximately 2,500 psi minimum), a hydraulic jack is applied to each tendon end and the tendon is pulled to the design force; the wedge anchor locks it off
7. Tail ends (dead ends and live ends) are covered with patch concrete after stressing

The stressing operation is visible at the slab edge as small pockets where the tendon ends protrude — these are ground flush and patched after stressing.

### The Sand Layer — A Critical DFW Detail

The sand "slip layer" between the vapor retarder and the concrete slab bottom is a DFW-specific standard practice that reduces soil-slab friction. When the expansive clay moves laterally (which it does as it swells and shrinks, not just vertically), friction between the soil and slab bottom drags the slab. The sand layer reduces this friction coefficient from approximately 0.7 (concrete on clay) to approximately 0.1 to 0.2 (concrete on sand). This reduces the drag force and helps the slab behave as the free-floating plate it is designed to be.

## Part 3: Drilled Piers in DFW — When They Are Used and What They Accomplish

As discussed in Article 4, drilled concrete piers that extend below the active zone are an alternative to the post-tensioned slab for DFW residential construction.

### When Piers Are Specified Instead of (or in Addition to) a PT Slab

**Higher-load structures**: Multi-story construction, heavy masonry or stone cladding, unusually large spans — all create column and wall loads that are too high for a slab to carry without significant bearing stress on expansive clay. Piers bypass the clay entirely, bearing on the more stable rock or dense calcareous material below.

**Severe soil conditions**: Sites where the active zone is unusually deep, where the swell pressure is very high, or where the site has a history of severe movement — engineers may specify piers for all structural loads, with the PT slab serving primarily as a floor and enclosure element (essentially spanning between piers rather than bearing on the soil).

**Slope conditions**: On sloped sites, the differential elevation creates very different moisture conditions at uphill and downhill edges of the foundation. Piers provide consistent depth of bearing regardless of grade.

**Commercial construction**: Virtually all commercial construction in DFW uses drilled piers for structural loads, with slab-on-grade for the floor.

### Pier Design for DFW

The key design parameter for DFW drilled piers is **the depth below the active zone**. Piers that bottom out within the active zone are worse than useless — the expansive clay adheres to the pier sides (adfreeze in frost terms; the DFW equivalent is soil-pier adhesion during swelling) and can lift the pier as the upper clay swells.

**Uplift on piers**: When upper clay swells, it grips the pier and tries to push it upward. The force is not small — soil adhesion in swelling clay can develop 2,000 to 10,000 psf on the pier surface within the active zone. For a 12-inch diameter pier 10 feet through the active zone: uplift force = (π × 1 ft × 10 ft) × 5,000 psf = 157,000 lbs = 78 tons of uplift force!

The pier's weight alone (12-inch diameter, 20 feet total = 1.75 cubic yards of concrete = approximately 3.5 tons) cannot resist this. The pier must be anchored by:
- Skin friction below the active zone (in stable soil or rock) must exceed the uplift force from the active zone above
- The building itself (gravity load) presses down on the pier head, opposing uplift

This is why DFW piers must extend significantly below the active zone — the deeper "anchor" segment below the active zone must be long enough to develop skin friction exceeding the active-zone uplift.

**Typical DFW pier design**:
- Diameter: 10 to 14 inches (small residential piers); 18 to 24 inches for commercial
- Total depth: 12 to 20 feet (to reach below the active zone AND develop adequate anchor skin friction)
- Rebar: Minimum 3 to 4 bars of #4, full length; additional steel in the active zone upper portion to resist bending from lateral soil movement
- Void forms (cardboard tube void forms): Placed in the top 12 to 18 inches of the drilled hole before concrete — the cardboard form fills the active zone near the surface, leaving a gap between the expanding clay and the pier concrete so that the clay cannot exert upward adhesion at the very top where it is wettest and most active

The void form is one of the most important and most commonly omitted details in DFW pier construction. Without it, the top 12 to 18 inches of the pier is in direct contact with the most active clay.

### The Pier + Grade Beam System

When piers are used in DFW residential construction, they are typically capped with a reinforced concrete grade beam at grade that:
- Spans between piers (typically 8 to 12 feet on center)
- Carries the wall loads from above
- Is suspended above the ground by a gap of 1 to 2 inches (provided by cardboard void forms placed under the beam during construction, which then compress and rot away)

The void under the grade beam allows the expansive clay to heave beneath the beam without lifting it. The piers anchor the beam from below while the grade beam distributes loads from the walls to the piers.

## Part 4: The Home Buyer's Foundation Checklist for DFW

If you are buying a house in the Dallas-Fort Worth Metroplex, foundation condition is the single most important item on your inspection checklist. Here is how to approach it.

### Before the Inspection

**Research the lot**: Ask for any prior foundation inspection reports or engineering letters from the seller or the MLS disclosures. In Texas, the Seller's Disclosure Notice requires disclosure of known foundation problems and prior repairs. This disclosure is mandatory but not always complete — treat it as a starting point, not a final answer.

**Research the soil**: Check the USDA Web Soil Survey (websoilsurvey.sc.egov.usda.gov) for the property address. Find the soil type and its shrink-swell potential rating. High-shrink-swell soils are essentially universal in DFW but the specific rating tells you how extreme.

**Look at the site**: Drive by the property before the inspection. Note:
- Are there large trees (oak, pecan, elm, hackberry) close to the house (within 20 to 30 feet)? These create significant soil moisture extraction.
- Is there obvious slope? Sloped sites have higher differential moisture risk.
- Any visible gaps between the foundation and the brick veneer at the corners? This is called "brick frieze board gap" and is a common indicator of foundation movement.
- Does the driveway approach or patio have significant cracking or tilting? Grade changes around the house can direct water toward or away from the foundation.

### The Foundation Inspection Itself

**Standard residential foundation inspection in Texas**: A licensed professional engineer (PE) or a certified foundation inspector conducts a visual inspection plus differential elevation survey.

**Differential elevation survey (the most important tool)**: A Zip-Level (hydrostatic level), Leica builder's level, or laser level is used to measure the elevation of the floor slab at a grid of points across the entire house — typically every 4 to 6 feet. The raw data is plotted to show the "floor plane" — the variation in slab elevation across the building.

Interpreting the results:
- **Total differential**: The difference between the highest and lowest reading across the slab. A perfectly flat slab would show 0 inches. Most DFW slabs show some differential.
- **Acceptable range**: There is no single code-mandated limit, but industry guidance (from the Texas Section of ASCE's Guideline for the Evaluation of the Structural Serviceability of Foundations for Single Family Homes) suggests:
  - Up to 1.0 inch total differential: Minor to normal for the DFW environment
  - 1.0 to 1.5 inches: Moderate movement; warrants monitoring and maintenance attention
  - 1.5 to 2.0 inches: Significant movement; functional and cosmetic damage likely; repair evaluation warranted
  - Greater than 2.0 inches: Serious movement; immediate engineering evaluation recommended

**Visual indicators examined by the inspector**:

Cracks in drywall — not all drywall cracks indicate foundation problems (settling, seasonal humidity changes, and poor workmanship all cause drywall cracks), but certain patterns are diagnostic:
- Diagonal cracks from door/window corners: The most classic indicator of differential settlement or differential heave. Cracks run diagonally from the corner of openings toward the direction of relative movement.
- Stair-step cracks in brick: Cracks following mortar joints in a stair-step pattern indicate differential horizontal and vertical movement — a classic foundation movement indicator.
- Horizontal cracks in brick at consistent height: May indicate differential movement at that elevation, or poor construction.

Doors and windows out of square — test every door in the house. A door that requires significant force to open or close, or that drags on the floor or sticks at the top corner, is in a distorted frame. The distortion can result from foundation movement, but also from other causes (framing shrinkage, poor installation) — the inspector must determine which.

Gaps at exterior corners — look at the corner trim boards on the exterior. Gaps opening at corners indicate the corner is racking (two walls moving relative to each other), which is a foundation movement indicator.

**Crawl space inspection** (if applicable — most DFW homes are slab-on-grade): N/A for most DFW houses.

**What a foundation inspection does NOT tell you**: The inspection gives you the current state of the foundation. It cannot reliably tell you:
- Whether the movement has stabilized or is ongoing (that requires multiple inspections over time, or a period of monitoring)
- Whether movement will worsen in the next drought or wet cycle
- The root cause of specific cracks (many cracks have multiple possible causes)
- Whether an uncracked slab is about to crack

For serious evaluations, a structural engineer may recommend installing **benchmarks** — permanent measurement points set into the foundation at specific locations — so future readings can be compared to the current baseline.

### Foundation Disclosure and Repair Warranties in Texas

**Prior repair history**: If the home has had foundation repairs, you have a right to all documentation — the engineering report, the contractor's scope of work, the warranty documents. Prior repair does not automatically mean a problem; it often means the previous owner was proactive. What matters is whether the repair was engineered (did a PE design it?), whether it was properly done (do before-and-after elevation surveys show improvement?), and whether the warranty is transferable.

**Warranty transferability**: Most reputable foundation repair contractors in DFW offer transferable warranties (the warranty transfers to a new owner upon sale). Verify this before closing. A non-transferable warranty is worth nothing to you as the buyer.

## Part 5: Building New on DFW Expansive Clay

If you are building a new home in DFW, here is what to ask, understand, and verify.

### Geotechnical Investigation

A geotechnical investigation is not optional for building on DFW expansive clay. Minimum requirements:
- Two soil borings to 20 feet (minimum), extending below the expected active zone and into the bearing stratum
- SPT testing at 1.5-meter intervals
- Atterberg limits and expansion tests (ASTM D4829 Expansion Index) on representative clay samples
- Determination of PTI soil parameters (ym and em) for slab-on-expansive-soil design
- Active zone depth estimate

Cost: $1,500 to $3,500 for a residential investigation in DFW. Your builder may have a standard geotech firm they use; you should verify the report is adequate.

### Foundation Design Options

**Option 1: Post-Tensioned Slab per PTI DC80.3** (by far the most common for DFW residential):
- Requires PTI design by a licensed PE
- Design should be specific to the site geotech — not a generic template
- Specifications should include: tendon spacing and pattern, perimeter beam and interior rib dimensions, concrete mix design, sand layer specification, vapor retarder specification

What to check: Does the builder use a site-specific PTI design from a licensed engineer, or a generic "standard DFW slab"? The difference matters on high-PI soils.

**Option 2: Drilled Pier and Grade Beam System**:
- More expensive: typically $8,000 to $25,000 more than a comparable PT slab
- More conservative: less dependent on the PT slab performing perfectly; structural loads bypass the active clay entirely
- Better for high-movement sites, steep slopes, very high PI soils, and sites with large existing trees

**Option 3: Combination (Piers at Critical Locations + PT Slab)**:
- Piers under load-bearing walls and at corners; post-tensioned slab for the field area
- The slab spans to the piers; the piers carry the structural loads; the slab handles its own weight and light floor loads

### Construction Quality for PT Slabs in DFW

The post-tensioned slab is only as good as its construction. Key construction quality points:

**Sand layer**: Must be 3 to 4 inches of clean concrete sand (not bank run, not caliche, not crusher fines with excessive fines). The sand must be confined within the slab perimeter — not spilling out under the grade beam. Verify before pour.

**Vapor retarder**: 15-mil minimum directly beneath the sand layer. Lapped 12 inches, taped. Extended up the perimeter forms. Inspect before sand placement.

**Tendon placement**: Tendons must be at the specified elevation (mid-depth of slab for most spans; closer to slab bottom for longer spans). Supported on chairs, not laying on the sand. Tendons must not be kinked, damaged, or have the plastic sheathing torn.

**Concrete placement**: DFW summer heat requires a concrete temperature maximum (typically 90°F at delivery). Concrete trucks must not add water. Finishing must be rapid — in summer heat, concrete sets fast and surface crusting can trap bleed water. Begin curing immediately after finishing.

**Stressing**: Must be done by a trained crew; all tendons stressed to the design force (verified with a calibrated jack gauge); stressing records kept (force and elongation measured and documented for each tendon).

**Curing**: 7 days of wet curing or curing compound application. In DFW summer, this is critical — uncured concrete in 100°F sun loses moisture rapidly and develops surface shrinkage cracks.

## Part 6: Foundation Maintenance for DFW Homeowners

The most common question from DFW homeowners: "What can I do to keep my foundation from moving?" The answer is: quite a lot, actually. Foundation maintenance is real and effective.

### Principle: Uniform Moisture Under the Slab

The goal of all foundation maintenance is to **maintain uniform moisture content under the slab**, minimizing differential wetting and drying. The foundation moves when one part of the soil moisture field changes differently from another. Uniformity is stability.

### Watering the Foundation

During the long DFW summers (June through September), the soil near the slab perimeter can lose significant moisture, shrinking and pulling away from the slab edge. Maintaining soil moisture during dry periods is the single most effective foundation maintenance action a homeowner can take.

**How to water**:
- Use soaker hoses placed 12 to 18 inches from the foundation perimeter (not directly against the wall — you do not want to saturate the soil at the wall face, just maintain moisture in the soil a foot or two out)
- Water consistently: 1 to 2 times per week during dry periods, adjusting for rainfall
- Water deeply: soaker hoses at low flow for 1 to 3 hours each session; this penetrates 6 to 12 inches into the soil rather than just wetting the surface
- Keep the soil moist but not saturated — soil adjacent to the foundation should be dark and crumbly, not wet and sticky

Foundation watering systems (buried soaker hose loops connected to a hose bibb or dedicated valve) can automate this. Cost to install: $300 to $800.

**Seasonal adjustment**: In wet springs, reduce or eliminate watering. In dry summers, water consistently. The goal is a constant moisture condition, not maximum moisture.

### Drainage Away from the Foundation

Water pooling next to the foundation adds moisture; drought dries it out. Both extremes cause movement.

- **Maintain positive grade**: 6 inches of fall in the first 10 feet, away from the house. Re-grade annually if needed as soil settles.
- **Downspout extensions**: All downspouts should discharge at least 5 to 6 feet from the foundation. Underground PVC extensions to daylight are ideal.
- **No mulch against the foundation**: Mulch retains moisture and concentrates it at the foundation edge. Keep mulch 6 to 12 inches from the slab edge.
- **No low spots adjacent to the foundation**: Low spots collect water and create locally wet zones.

### Tree Management

Trees are the biggest long-term threat to DFW residential foundations. Tree roots extract enormous quantities of moisture from the soil — a large live oak can transpire 100 to 200 gallons of water per day in summer. This moisture extraction extends far beyond the tree canopy.

Rules of thumb:
- **Keep large trees at least one to two times their mature canopy radius from the foundation** (a mature oak with a 30-foot canopy radius should be at least 30 to 60 feet from the house)
- **Remove trees that are too close before they become large** — removing a small tree is cheap; removing a large tree is expensive and the soil moisture change after removal can be just as damaging as having the tree there (the soil rehydrates from the tree absence, swelling in the area that was formerly dry)
- **If a large tree must be removed near the foundation**: Do it in stages (trim progressively over 1 to 2 years) rather than all at once; this gives the soil moisture a chance to equilibrate gradually

### Plumbing Leak Detection

A broken water line under the slab is a significant foundation threat. The water dissolves and erodes fine particles from the soil, creates voids, and locally saturates expansive clay — causing center lift in the wet zone. Signs of a slab plumbing leak:
- Unexplained increase in water usage (check meter over 24 hours with all water off)
- Hot spots on the floor (a broken hot water line warms the slab)
- Mold or musty smell in a carpet area
- Visible wet area on slab in an area not exposed to rain

Electronic leak detection ($300 to $600) can locate most slab leaks without cutting. Fix leaks promptly — every week of leak is additional foundation risk.

### Annual and Seasonal Inspection

Walk around the perimeter of the house each spring and fall:
- Check for new cracks in brick, stucco, or caulk
- Check for gaps at window and door frames
- Open and close every interior door — note any that are newly sticking
- Look for gaps between the slab and the finish flooring near the exterior walls
- Check for cracks in driveway and patio adjacent to the house

Document observations with photos (timestamp each). A journal of seasonal observations — even just brief notes — gives a baseline that is invaluable for future comparison.

## Part 7: Foundation Repair in DFW — When, What, and How Much

### When Is Repair Warranted?

Not all foundation movement requires repair. The Texas Section ASCE guideline acknowledges that some degree of movement is expected and normal. Repair is warranted when:
- Differential movement exceeds 1.5 to 2.0 inches and is causing functional problems (sticking doors, cracked windows, visible structure distress)
- Movement is ongoing and increasing
- The cause is identified and addressable (specific drainage failure, large tree root impact, plumbing leak)
- The homeowner is selling and a buyer's inspector has identified a condition requiring remediation

### Foundation Repair Methods for DFW Slabs

**Mudjacking (slab leveling)**: Drilling holes through the slab and pumping a slurry (usually a cement/soil/water mix) beneath the slab to fill voids and lift settled areas. Fast, relatively cheap ($500 to $3,000 for a typical area). Appropriate for areas where the slab has settled due to void formation (soil washout, organic decay) beneath it. NOT appropriate for expansive clay movement — the slurry does not address the soil volume change that caused the movement.

**Polyurethane foam injection (PolyLift)**: Similar concept to mudjacking — holes drilled through slab, expanding polyurethane foam injected. Lighter than mudjack slurry; less disturbance to soil. Same limitations: appropriate for voids, not for expansive clay differential movement.

**Helical pier installation**: As described in Articles 4 and 10, hydraulic torque machines install helical piers through excavations adjacent to the exterior footings. Piers extend below the active zone; brackets connect to the existing footing; hydraulic jacks lift the footing back toward design elevation. This is the primary method for permanent repair of edge drop in DFW expansive clay.

Typical DFW helical pier repair:
- 6 to 12 piers around the perimeter of the affected area
- Each pier to 12 to 18 feet depth (below active zone)
- Torque correlation: final torque × K factor = installed capacity
- Lift operation: all piers pressurized simultaneously and monitored; slab lifted to target elevation
- Entire operation: 1 to 2 days; no concrete curing time
- Cost: $8,000 to $20,000 for a typical repair

**Steel push piers**: Hydraulically driven segmental steel pipe sections, pushed into the ground by reacting against the building weight until bearing on rock or very dense material. An alternative to helical piers for very deep bearing (30+ feet). Less common in DFW than helical because the DFW bearing stratum (Austin Chalk or Taylor Marl) is typically at 10 to 20 feet, accessible with helical.

**Internal pier drainage**: In some cases, the cause of center lift is a plumbing leak saturating the soil under the slab. After the leak is repaired, the soil may drain naturally; or internal drainage measures (a sump) can be installed to accelerate drainage.

### What Foundation Repair Does NOT Do

Foundation repair addresses the structural consequence (the slab is out of level) but does not permanently eliminate the cause (the expansive clay). After repair:
- The clay will continue to expand and contract seasonally
- Without ongoing maintenance (watering, drainage, tree management), the repaired areas may move again
- The best outcome is a repaired foundation that is then properly maintained

Reputable foundation repair contractors in DFW acknowledge this and include maintenance guidance with their work. Be skeptical of any contractor who implies the repair is "permanent" without any maintenance.

## Conclusion: Knowledge Is the Best Foundation Tool

Dallas-Fort Worth expansive clay is not a secret, and it is not a crisis. It is a known, well-documented engineering challenge with known, well-documented solutions. Post-tensioned slabs designed to PTI DC80.3, properly installed, maintained with consistent watering and drainage, are the appropriate foundation for the vast majority of DFW residential construction. They work — when done right.

The failure mode is not the clay. The failure mode is inadequate design (using a generic template instead of a site-specific PTI design), inadequate construction (adding water to the concrete, skipping the sand layer, not stressing all tendons), and inadequate maintenance (ignoring drainage, killing large trees suddenly, letting the soil dry out adjacent to the slab for years at a time).

Knowledge changes behavior. The homeowner who understands why they are running soaker hoses next to their foundation will actually do it. The buyer who understands what the elevation survey numbers mean will make a better-informed purchase decision. The builder who understands what PTI DC80.3 actually requires will not accept a generic template when a specific design is needed.

Tomorrow, in the final article of this series, we close with the most common real-world scenario in residential foundations: not new construction, but repair, renovation, and restoration of existing foundations that have experienced problems.
