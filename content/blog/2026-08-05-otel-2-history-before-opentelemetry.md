## Part 1: Nothing Starts From Nothing

Yesterday's post established the vocabulary this series depends on: monitoring, observability, and the three classic signals of logs, metrics, and traces. Today's post goes backward, because OpenTelemetry — the subject of the rest of this series — was not a bolt from the blue. It was the endpoint of roughly forty years of the software industry repeatedly running into the same problem, solving it in an incompatible way each time, and eventually growing tired enough of the resulting mess to demand something better.

Understanding this history is not a detour. It is essential context for a claim this series will make repeatedly and defend carefully: that OpenTelemetry's value comes less from any single clever technical idea and more from being the first attempt at this problem with broad enough buy-in, from enough different companies with competing interests, to actually become a shared standard rather than one more entry in a long list of incompatible tools. To understand why that achievement is significant, you need to see the graveyard of well-intentioned prior attempts it was built on top of, and the specific pain that made the industry ready to cooperate.

We will walk through this history in the order the three signal types actually matured in the real world, which is not the order we introduced them yesterday. Logs came first, by a wide margin. Metrics, as a distinct discipline with its own dedicated tools, came next, maturing across the 1990s and 2000s. Distributed tracing, the newest of the three in anything resembling its current form, did not really exist as a practical, shared idea until Google published an internal paper in 2010.

## Part 2: Printing to Paper — The Log Era

### The problem logs originally solved

In the earliest days of networked computing, a running program was usually just that: one program, running on one machine, operated by the people who wrote it or who sat close enough to them to ask questions. When something went wrong, the natural thing to do was exactly what a programmer today still does on their own laptop: insert a line that prints a message describing what the program is doing, run it again, and read the output. This habit is often called, half-affectionately, print debugging, and it remains genuinely useful for a programmer working alone on code that runs in one place. The trouble starts, as our restaurant kitchen analogy from yesterday should already suggest, the moment a single logical task is spread across more than one machine. If the "cook" and the "waiter" are different computers in different rooms, a note scribbled by the cook, in the cook's own notebook, does the waiter very little good.

### Eric Allman and the origin of syslog

The specific technical answer the industry converged on for a remarkably long time traces back to one person and one project. In the early 1980s, a programmer named Eric Allman, working at the University of California, Berkeley, wrote a piece of software called sendmail, which became one of the most widely used programs on the early internet for moving electronic mail between computers. As part of that project, Allman also created a companion piece of software called syslog: a small, simple system whose entire job was to let a program send a short text message, tagged with a severity level and the name of the program that sent it, to a central place where an administrator could read it later, whether or not that administrator happened to be sitting at the machine that generated the message.

This is worth pausing on, because it is the first appearance, in this series, of an idea that recurs constantly in observability: the separation of the software that generates a signal from the software that stores, displays, and lets a human make sense of that signal. Syslog's genuinely durable insight was structural, not technical: a program does not need to know or care how its diagnostic messages will eventually be read; it only needs to hand them off, in a simple agreed format, to something else whose job is to worry about that. This same separation of concerns — generate the data one way, consume it a completely different way, connect the two with an agreed format — is, in a far more elaborate form, exactly what OpenTelemetry is built around, as later posts in this series will show in detail.

Syslog spread far beyond sendmail. Other programs on the same and other Unix-like systems adopted the same basic message format, since it was simple and already implemented, and syslog became what the industry calls a de facto standard: something everyone used, without any single authoritative document defining precisely how it should behave, which meant that different implementations quietly disagreed with each other in small but occasionally maddening ways. It took until August 2001 for the Internet Engineering Task Force to formally document the format that had already been in wide use for two decades, in a document called RFC 3164, and until March 2009 for a genuinely modernized version, RFC 5424, to be published, adding more precise timestamps, a formal notion of a structured data section, and support for text encoded outside the older, English-centric character set. Here is a message in the older format, followed by the same conceptual message in the newer one, so the difference is visible directly rather than only described:

```
<34>Oct 11 22:14:15 mymachine su: 'su root' failed for lonvick on /dev/pts/8
```

```
<34>1 2026-08-05T03:14:15.003Z mymachine.example.com su 12345 ID47 - 'su root' failed for lonvick on /dev/pts/8
```

The number in angle brackets at the start of each line, in both formats, is not arbitrary. It is calculated by taking a code for the general category of software that produced the message — the syslog specification calls this the "facility," with fixed numeric codes for categories like mail systems, authentication systems, or generic user-level processes — multiplying it by eight, and adding a separate number from zero to seven representing how severe the message is, where zero means an emergency and seven means routine debugging detail. This severity scale, invented for syslog in the 1980s, is still the direct ancestor of the "log level" concept — debug, info, warning, error, critical — that essentially every programming language's logging library still uses today, including the ILogger interface built into modern .NET, which this series will use extensively starting in Part 9.

### What syslog could not do

Syslog was, and in many contexts remains, a genuinely good solution to the specific problem it targeted: getting a short, timestamped text message from wherever it was generated to a central place it could be read. It was never designed to answer a much harder question that would only become pressing later: given that a single customer transaction now touches a dozen separate programs running on a dozen separate machines, which of the many, many log lines produced during that transaction actually belong together? Syslog has no built-in concept of a transaction, a request, or a journey. Each line stands alone. Reconstructing the story of one specific customer's slow checkout from a sea of individually correct but individually context-free syslog lines, scattered across a dozen machines, each with only a local timestamp for a hint, is exactly the kind of forensic, error-prone exercise that the later chapters of this series exist to make unnecessary.

## Part 3: Counting Things — The Metrics Era

### From checking to collecting

If logs answer "what happened," the discipline that grew up around metrics exists to answer a different question: "how much, and how often, and is it changing?" This discipline developed somewhat separately from logging, largely because the earliest tools in this space were not really about metrics as a rich, general-purpose signal at all — they were about a much narrower practice called monitoring in the strict, literal sense: periodically checking whether something was in an acceptable state, and raising an alarm if it was not.

The most influential early tool in this narrower tradition was Nagios, first released in 1999. Nagios worked, and in many of its descendants still works, by running small scripts called checks against hosts and services on a schedule, each check returning a simple verdict — is this thing okay, or is it not — which Nagios would use to decide whether to send an alert, typically by email. This was, and remains, a genuinely useful pattern for a specific and narrower job: knowing quickly when a server has stopped responding, or a disk has filled up, or a service that should be running has stopped. It was not designed to preserve a rich history of exactly how a number changed over time; a Nagios check by itself decides "good" or "bad" in the moment and largely discards everything else.

### Graphite and the shift toward raw numbers

A meaningfully different idea emerged from Orbitz, the travel-booking company, in the latter part of the 2000s, in a tool called Graphite. Where Nagios's model was "check the world against a rule and decide good or bad," Graphite's model was closer to "just record the number, without judgment, and let a human decide later whether it looks good or bad by examining the shape of the resulting graph over time." This may sound like a small philosophical difference, but practitioners who lived through the transition describe it as a genuinely significant shift in how operations teams thought about their systems: instead of a wall of pass/fail lights, you got a library of numeric histories you could actually plot, compare, and reason about using ordinary statistical intuition, because the data itself was simply preserved rather than being reduced immediately to a single verdict.

### StatsD and making metrics cheap to emit

Graphite solved the problem of storing and graphing numbers over time, but it left open a separate, very practical question: how does application code, running live in production and trying to serve real user requests as quickly as possible, actually get a number into Graphite without slowing itself down in the process? In 2011, engineers at Etsy, the online marketplace, published and open-sourced a small tool called StatsD to address exactly this. StatsD's job was narrow and deliberately simple: an application would fire off a tiny, extremely lightweight message — commonly sent over a network protocol called UDP, chosen specifically because it does not wait for a confirmation of delivery, trading a small amount of reliability for speed — saying, in effect, "this event just happened, and here is one number about it." StatsD would then be responsible for collecting many of these tiny messages, aggregating them over a short window, and forwarding a summarized result on to something like Graphite. This pattern — fire off a minimal message from application code and let something else worry about aggregation and storage — is a direct conceptual ancestor of how OpenTelemetry's own metrics instruments, covered in full in Part 8 of this series, are designed to behave.

### Prometheus and the multidimensional turn

The final major development in this pre-OpenTelemetry metrics lineage happened at SoundCloud, the music-sharing company, starting around 2012. SoundCloud was in the middle of moving from a smaller number of larger applications toward what the industry now calls a microservices architecture — many small, independently running services, echoing exactly the distributed-system picture from yesterday's post — and found that their existing StatsD-and-Graphite setup, built for a somewhat simpler world, was struggling under the sheer number of independently changing service instances involved. Two SoundCloud engineers, working initially as something close to a side project and explicitly inspired by an internal Google monitoring system called Borgmon that neither had worked on directly but knew of by reputation, built a new tool called Prometheus.

Prometheus's genuinely new contribution, compared to everything before it in this history, was treating a metric's identifying information as a set of explicit key-value labels rather than as one long, dot-separated name. Where an older Graphite-style setup might represent "500 errors on the POST method for the /tracks endpoint" by encoding all of that into a single sprawling metric name, Prometheus represented the same fact as a metric name plus a small set of separate, explicitly labeled dimensions: which method, which endpoint, which response code, which specific server instance. This made it dramatically easier to slice the same underlying data along different dimensions after the fact — show me this broken down by endpoint, now show me the same thing broken down by instance instead — without needing to have anticipated every possible breakdown in advance when the metric was first named. This idea, of attaching a flexible, explicit set of key-value attributes to a signal rather than baking everything into a rigid name, reappears constantly throughout the rest of this series, and Part 8's detailed treatment of metrics instruments will make the connection to Prometheus's own data model explicit.

Prometheus was itself accepted as the second project ever hosted by the Cloud Native Computing Foundation, joining shortly after Kubernetes, the container-orchestration system that has become the CNCF's flagship project. That detail matters for a reason that will become important in Part 3 tomorrow: the CNCF, as an organization, was already deeply familiar with hosting neutral, vendor-independent infrastructure standards by the time OpenTelemetry came looking for a home in 2019. It had already done this once, successfully, with Prometheus.

## Part 4: Following the Journey — Distributed Tracing Is Born

### Google's internal problem

The third signal, distributed tracing, has the shortest history of the three, and its origin is unusually well documented, because the company that invented its modern form chose to publish a detailed technical paper about it rather than keeping it entirely internal. In April 2010, a team at Google led by Benjamin H. Sigelman published a paper describing a system called Dapper, which the paper itself calls "Google's production distributed systems tracing infrastructure." The paper explains, in terms that should sound very familiar after yesterday's restaurant-kitchen discussion, that Google's own services had grown into complex, large-scale distributed systems built from many separately developed pieces, sometimes in different programming languages, spanning many thousands of machines — and that engineers needed a way to understand the behavior of one specific request as it moved through that whole tangle, not just the health of any one piece in isolation.

Dapper's design introduced, in a form the rest of this series will build on directly starting in Part 6, the core idea of a trace as a tree of timed operations, later called spans, connected by parent-child relationships that mirror how one piece of work calls into another. Dapper also introduced, quite deliberately, the idea of sampling — recording only a small fraction of all requests in full detail, rather than every single one — as a way of keeping the overhead of tracing low enough to run constantly in production without noticeably slowing anything down. This same trade-off, examined in far more careful and skeptical detail, is the entire subject of Part 16 of this series.

Dapper itself was never released outside Google. What Google published instead was the idea, in enough technical detail that other engineers, at other companies, without access to Google's internal code, could go build something similar for themselves. That is exactly what happened next.

### Zipkin, at Twitter

Two years after the Dapper paper's publication, in 2012, engineers at Twitter open-sourced a tracing system called Zipkin, explicitly inspired by Dapper's design, aimed at helping Twitter's own engineers understand and tune the performance of Twitter's growing collection of internal services. Zipkin is generally credited as the first genuinely open-source distributed tracing system built along Dapper's lines, and it saw real, if initially modest, adoption outside Twitter over the following several years.

### Jaeger, at Uber

The next major entrant took a few more years to arrive. Uber began an internal tracing effort, which would eventually be named Jaeger — the German word for "hunter" — in February 2015. Uber's own account of the project, published later, explains that the company's rapid move toward a large and growing collection of microservices had produced exactly the same kind of diagnostic difficulty this series keeps returning to: individual services could each report that they, personally, were healthy, while a customer's overall experience was slow or broken for reasons no single service's own dashboard would reveal. Jaeger was publicly open-sourced by Uber in October 2016, explicitly built with native support for a still-new standardization effort called OpenTracing, which we introduce properly in the next section, and it was designed to be backward compatible with data already instrumented for Zipkin, easing adoption for teams that had already invested in the older tool.

## Part 5: The Tower of Babel Problem

By the middle of the 2010s, then, the software industry had a genuine abundance of tools: Nagios and its many descendants for check-based alerting; Graphite, StatsD, and Prometheus for metrics; Zipkin and Jaeger for tracing; and, running alongside all of this the entire time, a thriving commercial industry of proprietary Application Performance Monitoring vendors — companies like New Relic, AppDynamics, Dynatrace, Splunk, Datadog, and Microsoft's own Application Insights, each selling a polished, often genuinely excellent, end-to-end product that combined some mixture of logs, metrics, and traces behind a single vendor's dashboard.

It is worth being fair to that last category, because this series intends to be evenhanded rather than reflexively anti-vendor. These commercial APM products, in general, delivered real engineering value. They were often far easier to adopt than assembling open-source tools yourself, since a team could install one agent and get a working, attractive dashboard the same afternoon, backed by a company whose entire business depended on the tool actually working well and staying supported. Many engineering organizations, quite reasonably, chose that path, and were well served by it for years.

The genuine, structural problem was not that these tools existed. It was that essentially none of them spoke the same language as any of the others. If your application code called a proprietary tracing library's specific function to start a trace, and you later decided — for reasons of cost, features, a corporate acquisition, or simple dissatisfaction — that you wanted to switch to a different vendor's tracing product, you generally could not simply redirect the existing data somewhere new. You had to go back through your codebase, wherever that first vendor's specific calls had been made, and replace them with a different vendor's specific calls instead. For a small application, this might be an afternoon's mildly annoying work. For a large organization with hundreds of internal services, each independently built by different teams over years, each with its own scattered vendor-specific instrumentation calls, this became a project that could reasonably be measured in months of dedicated engineering effort — effort spent not building anything new for the business, but purely re-plumbing existing diagnostic wiring so it spoke to a different vendor's socket.

This specific, concrete cost has a name in the industry: switching cost, or more pointedly, vendor lock-in. It is worth being precise about what lock-in does and does not mean here, because the term gets thrown around loosely. It does not mean a vendor is secretly holding your data hostage against your will. It means, more mundanely and more powerfully, that the true cost of leaving a vendor is far higher than the cost of staying, for reasons that have nothing to do with whether the vendor's product is still the best available option — and a rational business, facing that math, will very often simply stay, even when a better or cheaper alternative exists, purely because the switching cost outweighs the benefit. A whole generation of software companies built defensible, profitable businesses partly on top of exactly this dynamic, and it is not an accusation of bad faith to say so plainly; it is simply how the economics of proprietary, incompatible tooling tend to work, in observability as in many other categories of enterprise software.

## Part 6: How to Spot the Lie — "Industry Standard"

This is the first appearance of the recurring feature this series will include in every one of the next twenty posts: a specific, repeatable test for detecting a specific kind of misleading claim, drawn from the exact material covered that day.

Today's material is well suited to introducing a durable, reusable test, because before real, neutral standards existed in this space, nearly every proprietary tool in this history described itself, in its own marketing, as something close to a standard — the standard log format, the standard way to monitor a system, the de facto choice. "De facto standard," in particular, is a genuinely useful and honest phrase when applied correctly, as we used it above for syslog: it means something that a great many people happen to use, without any formal, neutral body having declared it so. It is a much weaker claim than it sounds, and it is worth noticing how often it gets deployed to borrow the credibility of the word "standard" while quietly avoiding any of the obligations — open specification, multiple independent implementations, neutral governance — that a genuine standard actually carries.

Here, then, is a three-part test you can apply to any claim of this kind, and which we will explicitly reapply to OpenTelemetry itself in tomorrow's post, precisely because a standard that cannot survive being checked against its own advertised claims is not worth much. First: is there a written specification, publicly available, that exists independently of any one company's specific product? Second: are there multiple, genuinely independent implementations of that specification, built by different organizations, that can be checked against each other for compliance? Third: is the specification governed by a body that does not answer to any single vendor's commercial interests — meaning, concretely, that no single company can unilaterally change the rules to favor its own product? A claim of "industry standard" that fails all three of these checks is not necessarily a lie in the sense of a deliberate falsehood, but it is, at minimum, a much smaller and more fragile claim than the phrase implies, and syslog itself, for the first twenty years of its existence, is a perfectly good historical example of exactly that: enormously widely used, and not, in the strict sense, standardized at all until RFC 3164 caught up with reality in 2001.
