Every budget reveals what a society believes it owes to its weakest members. Beyond the roads it builds and the schools it funds, a budget contains a set of promises to people who cannot fully provide for themselves — the elderly who can no longer work, the disabled, the destitute, children, marginalised groups, workers without protection. This is the *safety net* — the system of payments and protections by which a society catches those who would otherwise fall — and it is one of the most morally significant and economically consequential parts of any budget.

This post examines Nepal's safety net as this budget shapes it: the allowances paid to the vulnerable, the doubled nutrition allowance for marginalised children, the support for workers and for migrants returning home, and the protections promised to the most disadvantaged. These are, like the health and education promises of the last post, often genuinely humane commitments. But the safety net raises hard questions that this post will confront honestly — questions about *who is caught and who falls through*, about the difference between a promise on paper and a payment that actually reaches a remote villager, and about the deep trade-off between protecting *everyone* in a category and protecting only those who actually need it, between generosity and sustainability. A safety net is only as good as the people it actually catches, and a budget can promise protection that never reaches the very people it names. By the end you will be able to read the budget's social promises and ask not just how generous they sound, but who actually receives them and whether they can actually be sustained.

## What is social protection?

Begin with the idea. **Social protection** — sometimes called social security or the social safety net — is the system of public support designed to protect people from poverty and destitution, particularly those who cannot earn enough to support themselves: the elderly, the disabled, the very poor, children in need, and others who are vulnerable. It takes various forms — regular cash payments (allowances), subsidised or free services, food support, and protections for workers — but its common purpose is to ensure that the most vulnerable members of society have at least a basic floor of support, that no one falls below a minimum standard of dignity and survival.

Why does a society provide social protection, as opposed to leaving everyone to fend for themselves? The reasons are partly moral and partly practical. The moral argument is straightforward: a decent society does not let its elderly starve, its disabled go without support, or its children grow up in destitution, simply because they cannot earn — there is a basic obligation to care for those who cannot care for themselves, an obligation most societies recognise. The practical argument is that social protection serves the whole society's interests too: it reduces poverty and its social costs, it provides stability and a sense that the system is fair (which matters for social cohesion — and recall that it was precisely a sense of unfairness and lack of opportunity that fed the protests bringing this government to power), and it can even support the economy by maintaining the consumption and capability of people who would otherwise be destitute. So social protection is both a moral obligation and a practical investment in a stable, fair society. The questions are never whether to have *any* safety net — almost everyone agrees a society should catch its most vulnerable — but how generous it should be, who it should cover, and, crucially, whether it actually reaches the people it is meant to catch.

## Nepal's existing allowances

Nepal has, over the years, built a system of social-security allowances — regular cash payments to certain categories of vulnerable people — and this system forms the backbone of its safety net. Understanding it is essential, because much of the budget's social spending flows through these allowances, and because their design illustrates the central trade-off we will examine.

The allowances go to several categories: the *elderly* (a senior-citizen allowance paid to people above a certain age, which is the largest of the allowances), *single women and widows*, *people with disabilities*, certain *marginalised and endangered ethnic communities*, and others. These are regular payments — a set amount per month — that provide a basic income floor to people in these categories. The senior-citizen allowance in particular has become an important and politically popular feature of Nepali social policy, providing the elderly with a modest but meaningful regular income in a country where many lack pensions or savings. The allowances represent a real commitment to the vulnerable and have genuinely reduced destitution among the elderly and others who receive them.

A crucial feature of these allowances, which sets up the central trade-off of this post, is that they are largely *categorical* and *universal within their category* — meaning they are paid to *everyone* in the eligible category (every person above the qualifying age, for instance) regardless of whether that particular person is rich or poor. The senior-citizen allowance goes to all elderly people who qualify by age, not only to poor elderly people; it is universal within the category of the elderly. This universality has important consequences, both good and bad, that we will examine when we discuss the targeting-versus-universality trade-off. For now, note the feature: Nepal's allowances are mostly paid to everyone in a category, not means-tested to reach only the poor. And note the fiscal consequence: because these allowances are universal within their categories, and because the categories (especially the elderly) are large and growing, the allowances represent a *large and growing recurrent commitment* — exactly the kind of sticky, permanent recurrent spending we discussed in the post on recurrent versus capital, which grows over time and is almost impossible to reduce (cutting an allowance the elderly have come to depend on is political suicide). The allowance system is generous and popular, but it is also a heavy and rising claim on the budget that hardens the recurrent share year after year.

## The budget's social-protection measures

This budget makes a number of social-protection commitments, and we examine the notable ones, applying the inputs-outcomes lens from the last post and watching for who is actually caught.

The headline social measure is the **doubling of the nutrition allowance for Dalit children and certain other vulnerable children to NPR 1,000 per month** (around USD 6.67). The Dalit communities are among the most marginalised and disadvantaged groups in Nepali society, subject to historical caste-based discrimination and disproportionate poverty; a nutrition allowance for their children, and for other vulnerable children, addresses childhood malnutrition and supports the most disadvantaged at the most critical stage of life. Doubling it to NPR 1,000 per month is a genuinely good and targeted commitment — it goes to a group that genuinely needs it, addresses a genuine need (child nutrition, which has lifelong consequences), and represents real support for the marginalised. The aspiration deserves praise. The questions, as always, are about delivery and reach: does the doubled allowance actually reach the Dalit and vulnerable children it names — including those in the most remote and marginalised communities, who are precisely the hardest to reach — or does it, like many such payments, fail to reach some of the very people it is meant to help, for reasons we will examine? And NPR 1,000 per month, while a doubling and while meaningful, is a modest sum (about 6.67 dollars) against the cost of adequately nourishing a child — so the question is also whether it is enough to actually achieve the nutritional outcome it aims at. Praise the targeted aspiration; watch whether it actually reaches the children and actually improves their nutrition.

The budget allocates funds for **women, children, and gender minorities** (a specific allocation on the order of NPR 2.27 billion, around USD 15 million) and contains commitments around *disability* (rehabilitation support, schools for children with autism), *children in difficult circumstances* (support for street children), and *senior citizens*. These address real needs among real vulnerable groups, and the inclusion of gender minorities and people with disabilities reflects a commitment to groups often neglected. Each deserves credit as an aspiration and scrutiny as to delivery and adequacy — whether the modest sums actually reach the intended groups and actually meet their needs.

The budget also addresses *labour* — the protection of workers — through the Ministry of Labour, Employment and Social Security (a relatively small allocation, on the order of NPR 3.62 billion, around USD 24 million, reflecting that labour is a smaller ministry though an important domain). The commitments here include matters such as a *labour registry* (recording workers, a step toward formalising and protecting them), *minimum wage* provisions (a legal floor on wages), *workplace safety*, the *payment of wages through banks* (which formalises wage payment, creates records, and can protect workers from being cheated), and the *social-security fund* (a contributory scheme providing workers with benefits like pensions and protections). These labour measures aim to extend protection to workers, many of whom in Nepal work in informal, unprotected conditions, and to build the institutions of a modern labour market. They are worthy aims, though, as with all the reforms we have examined, their effect depends on actual implementation and enforcement, which in the labour domain — where so much work is informal and beyond the reach of regulation — is especially challenging.

A particularly notable commitment, given everything we have learned about Nepal's dependence on migration, is support for *returnee migrants* — Nepalis who have worked abroad and are returning home. Recall that millions of Nepalis work abroad, and that their return — whether by choice, by the end of a contract, or by force of circumstances such as an economic downturn abroad — poses real challenges: returnees may struggle to find work at home, may have skills that do not fit the domestic economy, and may face difficulties reintegrating. A returnee-migrant programme — to support their reintegration, to help them use their skills and savings productively at home, perhaps to channel their experience into domestic enterprise — addresses a real and growing need, and connects to the deeper goal of reducing Nepal's dependence on out-migration by building opportunity at home. It is a forward-looking commitment, though again its value depends on whether it is actually implemented effectively and actually helps returnees reintegrate, rather than being a programme that exists on paper but does little in practice.

There is also a measure increasing the *travel allowance for female community health volunteers* (by 50 percent) — the women who form a vital, often under-recognised backbone of community health in Nepal, and supporting them is a small but worthy recognition of their work.

Taken together, these measures show a budget with real attention to social protection and to vulnerable groups — the marginalised, women and children, the disabled, workers, returnee migrants. The aspirations are, on the whole, humane and reasonable. As always, the questions are about who is actually reached, whether the support is adequate, whether it can be sustained, and whether it actually achieves its aims — and to those questions, especially the question of *who falls through the net*, we now turn.

## Who gets left out: the problem of reach

Here is the question that the warm language of social protection most often obscures, and that this post insists on confronting: when the budget promises support to vulnerable groups, *does the support actually reach them — all of them, including the hardest to reach*? Because a safety net is only as good as the people it actually catches, and the cruel reality of social protection in Nepal (and many developing countries) is that the people most in need are often precisely the people the net *fails* to catch.

Consider why the most vulnerable are often the hardest to reach. To receive an allowance, a person typically must be *identified and registered* — they must have the documentation that proves their eligibility (identity documents, proof of age, proof of disability, proof of belonging to an eligible group), they must be *enrolled* in the system, and they must have a *means of actually receiving the payment* (increasingly, a bank account, as payments move toward banking channels). Now consider who struggles with each of these requirements: the very poorest and most marginalised. The most destitute may *lack the documentation* — many of the poorest Nepalis, especially in remote areas and among marginalised communities, lack birth certificates, identity documents, or the papers needed to prove eligibility, and so cannot register for the allowances they are entitled to. They may be *unaware* of their entitlements or unable to navigate the bureaucratic process of claiming them. They may live in remote areas *far from the offices* where one registers and the points where one collects payment, making access difficult and costly. And as payments move to *banking channels*, those without bank accounts — disproportionately the poorest and most remote — may be excluded or face difficulties. So the requirements that the system imposes to administer the allowances — documentation, registration, banking — systematically *exclude* exactly the most vulnerable people the allowances are meant to help, because those people are the least able to meet the requirements. This is the cruel paradox of the safety net: the holes in the net are largest precisely where the most vulnerable people are, so the people who most need catching are the most likely to fall through.

This problem of *reach* is the central reason a budget's social promises must be judged not by their generosity on paper but by their actual delivery to the intended people. The doubled nutrition allowance for Dalit children is a genuinely good commitment — but if it fails to reach the Dalit children in the most remote and marginalised communities, who lack the documentation or the access to claim it, then it helps the relatively better-off and accessible among the eligible while missing the very poorest, exactly inverting its purpose. The allowances for the disabled require proving disability and registering, which the most isolated disabled people may be unable to do. So the question "who gets left out?" is not a marginal quibble but the central test of whether a safety net works: does it actually reach the most vulnerable, the hardest to reach, the people for whom it most matters — or does it, through the very requirements of its administration, systematically exclude them? A citizen evaluating the budget's social promises should ask, of each one: who actually receives this, and does it reach the most vulnerable members of the intended group, or only the more accessible and better-documented among them? Because a generous allowance that fails to reach the poorest is, for those poorest, no allowance at all — and they are precisely the people it was supposed to be for.

## The trade-off: universal versus targeted

We come now to one of the deepest and most genuinely difficult trade-offs in all of social policy, the one set up by the universal-within-category design of Nepal's allowances: the choice between *universal* and *targeted* social protection. This is a real dilemma with strong arguments on both sides, and a citizen should understand both rather than assuming one approach is obviously right.

A *universal* (or categorical) approach pays the allowance to *everyone* in a category — every elderly person, every child in a group — regardless of their wealth or need. Nepal's senior-citizen allowance, paid to all who qualify by age, is universal in this sense. A *targeted* (or means-tested) approach pays only to those who *actually need it* — only the poor elderly, only the poor children — assessing each person's means and giving the allowance only to those below some threshold of poverty.

The arguments for *universal* provision are strong. First, *it avoids exclusion errors*: because everyone in the category receives it automatically, no genuinely needy person is wrongly excluded by a flawed means-test, and — crucially given the reach problem we just discussed — universal provision does not require the poor to prove their poverty through documentation they may lack, so it is more likely to actually reach the vulnerable. Second, *it is administratively simpler and cheaper to run*: there is no costly, error-prone, intrusive process of assessing each person's means; eligibility is simple (your age, your category), so administration is easier and the resources are not consumed by the bureaucracy of means-testing. Third, *it carries no stigma*: because everyone in the category receives it, there is no shame in claiming it, unlike means-tested benefits that can stigmatise recipients as "poor." Fourth, *it builds broad political support*: because everyone benefits, the middle class and not just the poor have a stake in the programme, which makes it politically durable. These are powerful advantages, and they explain why universal categorical allowances are popular and resilient.

The arguments for *targeted* provision are equally real. First, *it is far cheaper for a given impact on poverty*, or, put another way, it concentrates the available money on those who actually need it rather than spreading it to the well-off who do not: a universal allowance gives money to rich and poor elderly alike, much of it to people who do not need it, whereas a targeted allowance gives the same total money in larger amounts to the poor alone, or the same impact at lower total cost. In a poor country with severe budget constraints — like Nepal, with its small per-person budget and its heavy recurrent burden — the argument that scarce resources should be concentrated on those who actually need them, not spread to the comfortable, is genuinely compelling. Second, *universal allowances are fiscally expensive and growing*, hardening the recurrent budget (as we discussed) by committing large sums to everyone in growing categories, including those who do not need it, which crowds out other spending — including, potentially, spending that could do more for the poor. So the targeting argument is essentially: in a resource-constrained country, why spend scarce money giving allowances to the well-off elderly when that money could either go further for the poor or fund other needs?

The honest verdict is that this is a *genuine dilemma with no costless answer*, and reasonable people weigh it differently. The universal approach reaches the vulnerable more reliably (no documentation barrier, no exclusion errors), is simpler and stigma-free and politically durable — but it is expensive and spends scarce money on people who do not need it. The targeted approach concentrates scarce resources on the needy and is cheaper for a given anti-poverty impact — but it requires means-testing that is administratively costly, error-prone, stigmatising, and, critically, *imposes exactly the documentation and assessment requirements that exclude the hardest-to-reach poor*, so that targeting in practice often fails to reach the very poorest it aims at, while universal provision, for all its waste, may actually reach them better. So there is a deep irony: targeting *sounds* more efficient and more pro-poor, but in a context of weak administration and poor documentation, *universal* provision may actually do better at reaching the genuinely vulnerable, because it does not require them to prove anything. There is no clean answer; the right choice depends on the specific context, on administrative capacity, on the budget constraint, and on values. What a citizen should understand is that the choice between universal and targeted social protection is a real trade-off — generosity and reach versus efficiency and concentration — and that neither approach is simply right, and that the seemingly-efficient targeted approach carries the hidden cost of excluding the hardest-to-reach. This budget, working largely within Nepal's existing universal-categorical system, faces this trade-off, and a citizen evaluating its social spending should weigh it with open eyes rather than assuming either that universal allowances are wastefully generous or that targeting is straightforwardly better.

## A worked example: the allowance that didn't arrive

Make the reach problem concrete with a composite example that captures the cruel paradox of the safety net.

Imagine an elderly woman in a remote mountain village — exactly the kind of person the senior-citizen allowance exists to help, poor, old, unable to work, with no pension or savings. She is, on paper, entitled to the allowance. But consider the obstacles between the entitlement and the money actually reaching her. She may lack the documentation to prove her age and identity — many of the oldest and poorest Nepalis, born in remote areas before reliable registration, lack birth certificates or identity documents, and obtaining them may require a difficult, costly journey to a distant government office and a bureaucratic process she cannot easily navigate. Even if registered, collecting the allowance may require traveling to a distant point, difficult for a frail elderly person in a remote area. And as payments shift to bank accounts, she may have no bank account and no easy way to open or access one from her village. So the allowance to which she is entitled — the generous, humane, well-intentioned allowance the budget funds — may simply *never reach her*, defeated by the documentation, the distance, and the banking requirements that the system imposes. Meanwhile, a better-off elderly person in an accessible town, with documents and a bank account, receives the same allowance without difficulty — so the universal allowance, in practice, reaches the accessible and documented while missing the most remote and marginalised, exactly the people who most need it.

Now imagine that, in an effort to be "efficient," the system were made *targeted* — means-tested, so that only the poor elderly receive it. This would, in principle, concentrate the money on people like our remote elderly woman. But in practice, the means-test would add *further* requirements — proving not just age and identity but poverty, through assessments and documentation — and these further requirements would be *even harder* for the most remote and marginalised to meet, so the targeting, intended to focus on the poor, might exclude the poorest even more thoroughly than the universal system did, while the somewhat-better-off poor who can navigate the process receive it. So neither approach cleanly solves her problem: the universal system reaches her in principle but the administrative barriers exclude her in practice; the targeted system aims at her in principle but its additional requirements exclude her even more in practice. Her case reveals that the deepest problem is not the *design* of the allowance (universal or targeted) but the *reach* — the failure of either system to actually penetrate to the most remote and marginalised, who lack the documentation, access, and banking that both systems require.

The lesson for a citizen is twofold. First, judge social protection by *reach* above all — by whether it actually gets to the most vulnerable, the hardest to reach, not by its generosity on paper or its design in principle. Second, recognise that the binding problem is often *administrative and infrastructural* — documentation, access, banking, the capacity to actually deliver payments to remote and marginalised people — rather than the headline design of the allowances, so that the most important social-protection reforms may be the unglamorous ones that improve *reach* (registering the undocumented, bringing payment access to the remote, building the delivery infrastructure) rather than the announceable ones that increase the *amount* of allowances that may never arrive. A budget that doubled an allowance but did nothing to improve its reach to the most marginalised would be increasing a payment that fails to reach the very people it names — generous on paper, hollow in the remote villages where the need is greatest. And a citizen who understands this asks, of every social promise, not "how generous is it?" but "does it actually reach the most vulnerable?" — the question that the warm language of caring for the marginalised is least eager to have asked.

## For the technically minded: allowances as data, and the cost of universality

For readers who work with numbers, here are the social-protection measures in schematic form, followed by a calculation showing the fiscal weight of universal allowances and the reach problem:

| Measure | Amount | USD (≈) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ministry of Women, Children & Social Welfare (total) | NPR 122.61 billion | 0.82 billion | Houses much social spending |
| Dalit/vulnerable child nutrition allowance | NPR 1,000/month (doubled) | ~6.67/month | Targeted to a needy group; watch reach |
| Women, children & gender minorities (specific) | NPR 2.27 billion | 15 million | Targeted support |
| Ministry of Labour, Employment & Social Security | NPR 3.62 billion | 24 million | Labour protections; small ministry |
| Senior-citizen and other allowances | (large, within social spending) | — | Universal within category; growing burden |

Now a calculation showing why universal allowances are fiscally heavy and how the reach problem undermines them:

```text
# The fiscal weight of a UNIVERSAL allowance: paid to everyone in a category.
monthly_allowance = 1000          # NPR per recipient per month (illustrative)
eligible_in_category = 3_000_000  # everyone in the category (illustrative)
annual_cost = monthly_allowance * 12 * eligible_in_category
# = NPR 36 billion (USD ~240 million) -- a large, PERMANENT, growing recurrent commitment.

# Targeting would pay only the poor -> fewer recipients -> lower cost OR larger payments:
poor_in_category = 1_000_000      # only those who actually need it (illustrative)
targeted_cost_same_amount = monthly_allowance * 12 * poor_in_category   # ~NPR 12bn
# Cheaper -- BUT targeting requires means-testing, which adds documentation requirements...

# The REACH problem: of those ENTITLED, only those who clear the barriers actually RECEIVE.
entitled        = eligible_in_category
with_documents  = 0.80 * entitled    # 20% lack the papers to register (illustrative)
with_access     = 0.85 * with_documents   # some cannot reach registration/payment points
with_banking    = 0.80 * with_access      # some have no bank account as payments digitise
actually_reached = with_banking           # ~54% of the entitled actually receive it
# The ~46% who fall through are disproportionately the POOREST and most REMOTE --
# exactly the people the allowance is most for. Targeting would add MORE barriers,
# excluding the poorest even more. Reach, not design, is the binding problem.
```

The calculation makes two points vivid. First, universal allowances are *fiscally heavy and permanent* — paying everyone in a large, growing category commits large recurrent sums forever, which is why they harden the recurrent budget. Targeting would be cheaper, but at the cost of means-testing's barriers. Second, and more importantly, the *reach* calculation shows that of those entitled, only a fraction actually receive the allowance, because the documentation, access, and banking requirements exclude the hardest-to-reach — and those excluded are disproportionately the poorest and most remote, the very people the allowance is for. Targeting, by adding the further barrier of proving poverty, would worsen this exclusion of the poorest even as it concentrates money on the poor in principle. A developer or analyst modelling social protection would model not just the headline allowance and the eligible population, but the *reach* — the fraction of the entitled who actually receive — because that, not the announced amount, determines who is actually helped. And a citizen, equipped with the same logic, knows that the binding problem is reach, that improving reach (documentation, access, delivery) may matter more than increasing amounts, and that both universal and targeted designs founder on the same administrative barriers that keep allowances from the poorest. The truth of a safety net is in who it actually catches, which the headline generosity conceals.

## How budgets deceive: announcing a programme without a delivery mechanism

The technique of deception for this post is one that the warm, humane language of social protection makes especially easy and especially common: **announcing a programme without a credible delivery mechanism** — proclaiming a generous benefit or protection for a vulnerable group, harvesting the moral credit, while the actual machinery to deliver it to the intended people is absent, inadequate, or unaddressed.

Here is why the deception is so natural in social protection. Promises to the vulnerable — to the marginalised, the elderly, the disabled, poor children — are *morally attractive and politically rewarding*: announcing a doubled allowance for Dalit children, support for the disabled, protection for workers, help for returning migrants, generates goodwill and the appearance of a caring government, and to question such a promise can seem heartless. So there is a strong temptation to *announce generous social commitments* — the amounts, the groups, the protections — and to bank the moral and political credit immediately, while the difficult, unglamorous, expensive work of actually *delivering* the benefit to the intended people (registering the undocumented, reaching the remote, building the payment infrastructure, enforcing the labour protections, actually running the returnee programme) goes unaddressed or under-resourced. The deception is in presenting the *announcement* of a benefit as though it were the *delivery* of that benefit to the people who need it — in letting the generous, humane, announceable promise stand in for the actual reach to the vulnerable, which is far harder and far less certain. "We have doubled the allowance for the marginalised" invites the public to believe the marginalised are being helped, when what has actually happened is that an amount has been increased — an amount that, as we have seen, may never reach the most marginalised it names, if the delivery mechanism to reach them is absent. The deception thrives because the announcement is vivid and the failure of reach is invisible — no one reports that an allowance announced for the poorest never reached the poorest in the remote villages, while the announcement itself was widely celebrated.

The defence is to *ask, of every social promise, about the delivery mechanism and the reach*. When a government announces support for a vulnerable group, ask: Is there a credible *mechanism* to actually deliver this to the intended people, including the hardest to reach? How will the undocumented be registered, the remote be reached, the payment actually be made? What is the evidence that benefits actually reach the most vulnerable, or do they reach only the accessible and documented? Is the labour protection actually enforced, or merely legislated? Is the returnee programme actually operating and helping, or merely announced? And, the sharpest question: of the people this benefit names, what fraction will actually receive it — all of them, or only the easy-to-reach? A citizen who asks about delivery and reach, who refuses to credit a generous announcement as though it were actual help to the vulnerable, and who insists on knowing whether the benefit will actually penetrate to the most marginalised, cannot be deceived by the announcement of programmes without delivery. And such a citizen applies the right test to this budget's genuinely humane social promises: not whether they were announced and the amounts set (they were), but whether, in the remote villages and marginalised communities where the need is greatest, the support actually arrives. That arrival — measured in benefits actually reaching the most vulnerable, not amounts announced in the budget — is the only true measure of whether the safety net works. The deception thrives on crediting generous announcements; the defence is to credit only benefits that actually reach the people they name, especially the hardest to reach.
