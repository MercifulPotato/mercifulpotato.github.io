### 1.1 What Is an Electric Vehicle?

Let us start at the absolute beginning.

A conventional gas car uses an internal combustion engine — a device that burns gasoline (or diesel) to push pistons, which spin a crankshaft, which (through a transmission and driveshaft) ultimately turns the wheels. The process is noisy, thermally inefficient (about 20–40% of the fuel's energy actually moves the car; the rest becomes heat), and requires a complex support system: an oil pump, a cooling system, an exhaust system, a catalytic converter, a starter motor, an alternator (which charges the 12V battery), a transmission with many gear ratios, and so on.

An electric vehicle replaces almost all of that with a fundamentally different system. A battery pack stores energy as electricity. An electric motor (or two) converts that electricity directly into rotation. The motor is connected to the wheels through a much simpler drivetrain — often just a single fixed-ratio reduction gear. There is no combustion, no exhaust, no oil to change, no coolant for an engine (though the battery and electronics have their own thermal management), and no transmission to service. The motor is silent and produces full torque — its maximum twisting force — from a complete standstill, which is why electric vehicles feel so effortlessly quick off the line.

The battery is the most important component and the most expensive. It is measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh), which you can think of as the "size of the fuel tank." A larger kWh number means more stored energy and, generally, more range. The Kia EV9 comes in two battery sizes: a 76.1 kWh standard-range pack and a 99.8 kWh long-range pack. For context, 99.8 kWh is roughly equivalent to the energy in about 3.3 gallons of gasoline — but because electric motors are so much more efficient at converting energy to motion, that 99.8 kWh will move a 5,500-pound SUV roughly 270–310 miles.

### 2.2 The Platform: What E-GMP Means and Why It Matters

The EV9 is built on Hyundai Motor Group's Electric Global Modular Platform, universally abbreviated E-GMP. This is the same platform underpinning the Hyundai Ioniq 5, Ioniq 6, and Kia EV6. It is important because E-GMP is not a gasoline platform with an electric motor bolted in as an afterthought — it is designed from the ground up for electric vehicles.

The key practical implications:

**800-volt architecture.** Most EVs operate at 400 volts. The EV9 operates at 800 volts. Think of voltage like water pressure in a pipe: higher pressure means you can move water faster through the same pipe. In electricity, higher voltage means you can transfer more power (measured in kilowatts) with less heat and less stress on the cables and connectors. The result: the EV9 can accept DC fast charging at up to 210–240 kilowatts, enabling a 10% to 80% charge in approximately 24–30 minutes at a capable charger. A 400V EV on the same charger would be limited to far lower speeds. We will explain charger types in detail in Part 4.

**Flat floor.** Because the battery pack is built into the floor (rather than being an add-on), the EV9 has a perfectly flat second-row floor. No center hump. Second-row passengers can sit straight with feet flat on a level surface, unlike in many gas-powered SUVs where the driveshaft creates a raised hump in the middle.

**Low center of gravity.** The battery is the heaviest single component in an EV. Because E-GMP places it low in the floor, the EV9's center of gravity is dramatically lower than a comparable gas SUV. Despite weighing approximately 5,500–5,700 pounds, the EV9 corners with more composure than its weight suggests. Body roll is noticeable but well-controlled.

**Vehicle-to-Load (V2L) and Vehicle-to-Home (V2H).** E-GMP supports bidirectional power flow. V2L means the car can power external devices and appliances — a 120V outlet built into the car (and an additional one available via an adapter at the charge port) can power a laptop, a camp stove, power tools, or a small air conditioner. V2H (available with a compatible external inverter and home wiring) allows the car to act as a home backup battery during a power outage. In areas prone to hurricane or severe weather power outages — like Virginia's Hampton Roads region — this is a meaningful capability that adds real practical value. A 99.8 kWh battery can power an average American home for roughly one to two days, depending on load.

### 2.5 Technology and Safety Features

**Driver-Assistance Systems**
The EV9 comes standard with Kia's Highway Driving Assist 2 (HDA 2), a system that combines adaptive cruise control (the car automatically maintains following distance from the vehicle ahead) with lane-centering assist (the car actively steers to keep itself centered in the lane). On highway driving, you can activate HDA 2 and the car will handle the throttle, braking, and steering within limits while you remain attentive and ready to intervene.

The system works well on interstates with clear lane markings. It struggles in construction zones, sharp curves, or scenarios where lane markings are absent or ambiguous. Think of it as a very capable co-pilot, not a self-driving system. You must always be ready to take over.

Additional standard safety features on all EV9 trims:
- Forward collision avoidance assist (auto-braking if a collision is imminent)

All EV9s have a large central touchscreen for climate, media, and navigation. Wind, Land, and GT-Line models get the full dual 12-inch panoramic display — a wide, uninterrupted screen surface housing both the instrument cluster and the center infotainment screen.

## Part 3: The True Cost Landscape — What You Are Actually Paying

### 3.1 The Depreciation Story

New cars lose value the moment they leave the dealer lot. This is universal and unavoidable. What varies is *how fast* they lose value. The EV market has seen unusually steep depreciation, particularly since 2023–2024, for several reasons:

1. **Rapid technology improvement.** New EV models arrive with meaningfully better range, faster charging, and lower prices than their predecessors. Buyers anticipate that waiting means getting a better car for less money, which suppresses demand for used models.

2. **Tax credit distortion.** When the federal $7,500 new EV tax credit was available (through September 30, 2025), it effectively reduced the cost of a new EV but did not reduce the cost of a used EV priced above $25,000. This widened the effective price gap between new and used, suppressing used-EV prices.

3. **Range anxiety perception.** Many buyers still worry about EV range and charging availability. This makes them reluctant to buy a used EV (where battery health is uncertain) even at dramatically lower prices, which keeps used-EV prices down.

4. **Market oversupply.** Automakers ramped EV production aggressively in 2023–2024 based on optimistic demand forecasts. Actual sales did not always keep pace, leading to inventory buildup and aggressive new-car incentives that further depressed used-car values.

The result for EV9 buyers in mid-2026: you can buy a car that sold for $73,000 two years ago for $36,000–$38,000. That is roughly 48–52% depreciation in two model years. For used-car buyers, this is an extraordinary opportunity.

The flip side: if you buy a used EV9 today and try to sell it in three years, you will face the same forces. The EV market's depreciation curve has been steep and shows little sign of reversing in the near term. Buy a used EV9 with the expectation that you will own it for five to ten years, not flip it in eighteen months.

## Part 4: Charging — Everything You Need to Know, Starting From Zero

If you have never owned an electric car, charging is the concept that requires the most explanation. People who have not lived with an EV often imagine charging as a complicated, anxiety-inducing process. In practice, it is simpler than going to a gas station — once you have the right setup at home.

### 4.1 The Three Levels of EV Charging

**Level 1 — Standard Household Outlet (120V / "Wall Outlet")**

This is the slowest form of charging and requires no special equipment. Every home in the United States has 120V outlets — the standard three-prong outlets that power lamps, phone chargers, kitchen appliances, and computers.

The EV9 comes with a portable Level 1/Level 2 charging cable (called an EVSE — Electric Vehicle Supply Equipment — but widely just called a "charging cable" or "charge cord"). One end plugs into a standard 120V wall outlet. The other end has a J1772 plug that connects to the EV9's charge port (located at the front of the vehicle, on the driver's side fender — an unusual position that takes some getting used to but means you approach the front of the car to plug in rather than the rear).

**The numbers:** A 120V outlet at 12 amps delivers approximately 1.4 kW of power. The EV9's long-range battery holds 99.8 kWh. Dividing: 99.8 ÷ 1.4 = approximately 71 hours to charge from completely empty to completely full. In practice, you never start from completely empty, but this illustrates the scale of the problem.

In range terms: Level 1 charging adds approximately 3–5 miles of range per hour of charging. In an 8-hour overnight session, you add about 24–40 miles. If you drive fewer than 30–40 miles on most days, Level 1 can technically keep up with your needs — you plug in at night and wake up with enough range to cover the next day.

**The honest assessment of Level 1 for an EV9:** It works, barely, for low-mileage drivers. If your daily commute is 20–25 miles round-trip and you have 10–12 hours each night to charge, Level 1 will probably cover your weekday needs. But you will find yourself constantly monitoring the charge level, unable to make unplanned longer trips without adequate planning, and stressed whenever you have a day with higher-than-normal mileage. The EV9's large battery means that a 50% depletion (say, after a 130-mile round trip) would require 25–30 hours to recover on Level 1. That is more than a full day.

For an occasional backup option — say, you are visiting family and they have only a standard outlet — Level 1 is perfectly usable. As a daily driver's primary charging method for a long-range SUV, it is a compromise that most people find frustrating within weeks.

**Safety on Level 1:** Before plugging in, verify that the outlet is:
- Properly grounded (three-prong, not two-prong)
- On a dedicated circuit or a lightly loaded circuit (not shared with a refrigerator, freezer, or other heavy appliance)
- In good physical condition (no discoloration, no heat when touched after charging)

Never use an extension cord for EV charging. The continuous current draw of EV charging will overheat most extension cords. If your outlet is not positioned correctly, have an electrician install a proper outlet in the right location.

**Level 2 — 240V Outlet (NEMA 14-50 or Hardwired)**

This is the standard home charging solution for EV owners, and it is what the vast majority of EV9 owners use day to day. It requires a 240V electrical circuit — the same voltage used by electric dryers, electric ranges, and central air conditioning units. If you have an electric dryer, you already have 240V service in your home; the question is whether it is located near your parking spot and whether your electrical panel has capacity for an additional circuit.

**What Level 2 provides:** Charging at 7–11.5 kW, adding approximately 25–40 miles of range per hour. In practical terms: an empty 99.8 kWh EV9 battery charges to 80% (the level recommended for everyday use, to preserve long-term battery health) in approximately 7–9 hours. A depleted-to-20% battery recovers to 80% in approximately 5–6 hours. In almost all real-world scenarios, you plug in when you get home in the evening, and the car is fully charged before you wake up. You never think about it. This is the magic of home charging — it is like having a gas station in your garage that fills your tank while you sleep, every single night.

The EV9's onboard charger accepts Level 2 at up to 11.5 kW (single-phase AC). Matching your installed charger's output to this is ideal; you do not benefit from a charger rated higher than what the car can accept.

**Hardware options for Level 2:**

*NEMA 14-50 outlet:* This is a 240V outlet with four prongs (two hot, one neutral, one ground), the same type used for RV hookups and some electric ranges. It is the easiest Level 2 option to install. Once installed, your EV9's portable EVSE (or a separately purchased Level 2 charger) plugs directly into it. NEMA 14-50 outlets are convenient because the charger is removable — you can unplug it and take it with you when traveling.

*Hardwired wall-mounted EVSE:* A dedicated Level 2 charging unit (brands include Grizzl-E, ChargePoint, JuiceBox, Emporia, and many others) permanently wired to its own circuit in your electrical panel. These typically offer smart features: scheduling charges to run during off-peak electricity hours, tracking energy usage, integrating with solar systems, and managing load if you have multiple EVs. A hardwired 48-amp EVSE delivers the maximum power your panel/wiring can provide to the EV9.

**The EV9's NACS port (2026 and later) vs. J1772 (2024–2025):** The 2024 and 2025 EV9 has a CCS1 (Combined Charging System) port, which accepts J1772 plugs for Level 2 AC charging and CCS DC fast charging. The 2026 model was updated to NACS (North American Charging Standard, formerly Tesla's connector). If you are buying a used 2024 or 2025 EV9, you will use J1772 Level 2 chargers at home — which is the majority of Level 2 infrastructure and presents no practical problem.

---

**Level 3 — DC Fast Charging**

This is the technology that makes long-distance travel in an EV feasible. Unlike Level 1 and Level 2, which deliver alternating current (AC) that the car's onboard charger converts to DC to charge the battery, DC fast charging bypasses the onboard charger entirely and sends high-voltage direct current directly into the battery at high power.

The EV9's 800V architecture enables DC fast charging at up to 210 kW (some sources list 240 kW on the very fastest chargers). At a 350 kW capable station like those found in the Electrify America or EVgo networks, the EV9 can charge from 10% to 80% in approximately 24–30 minutes. Adding 150–200 miles of range during a 20–30 minute coffee break makes road trips in an EV9 genuinely comparable to stopping for gas in a minivan.

Popular DC fast charging networks in the United States include:
- **Tesla Supercharger:** As of 2025, the EV9 can access Tesla Superchargers using its CCS port with a Tesla-provided adapter (for 2024–2025 models). The 2026 EV9's NACS port connects natively without an adapter. Tesla's Supercharger network is the largest and most reliable fast-charging network in the country.
- **Electrify America:** Widely distributed, with 350 kW capable chargers at many locations. The EV9 will charge at close to its maximum rate here.
- **EVgo:** Another major network, though with a mix of 50 kW and 350 kW chargers.
- **ChargePoint:** A large network with variable speeds.

DC fast charging is not recommended for daily use. The high power levels generate heat in the battery, and repeated high-speed charging accelerates battery degradation over time. The recommended practice: use DC fast charging for road trips and occasional top-ups, and rely on home Level 2 charging for daily needs.

### 4.2 Adding a 220V (240V) Outlet to Your Garage: Is It Feasible? What Does It Cost?

"220V" and "240V" refer to the same thing in American residential electrical. (The nominal line voltage in U.S. homes is 120V per leg; two legs combined give you 240V. The older "220V" label is technically outdated but still widely used.) Adding a 240V circuit to your garage is the single most impactful upgrade an EV buyer can make to their home.

**Is it feasible?**

For the overwhelming majority of American homeowners with a detached or attached garage, yes — it is feasible. The key questions are:

*Is there an electrical panel in or near the garage, or is the garage fed by a subpanel?* If yes, the electrician's work is shorter (less wiring run) and cheaper.

*Does your main electrical panel have available breaker slots?* You need a 50-amp double-pole breaker for a NEMA 14-50 outlet (or a 60-amp breaker for the largest EVSE installations). If your panel is full, the electrician may need to add a subpanel or consolidate existing circuits, adding cost.

*What is the amperage rating of your main panel?* Older homes (pre-1980s) sometimes have 100-amp service panels. Modern homes typically have 200-amp service. A 200-amp panel can comfortably support EV charging alongside a typical household's electrical load. A 100-amp panel may require an upgrade to 200-amp service (a major expense — see below) before adding an EV circuit.

*How far is the electrical panel from where you park?* The further the run, the more wire the electrician needs to install, and the higher the labor cost.

**What does it cost?**

Based on mid-2026 data, total installed cost for a Level 2 charging setup in a residential garage in the United States:

| Scenario | Approximate Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Simple run from existing 200A panel with available slots | $400 – $900 |
| Moderate run (25–50 feet), new breaker slot needed | $700 – $1,500 |
| Long run (50–100 feet) or finished walls/attic required | $1,200 – $2,500 |

## Part 5: The Battery — Health, Degradation, and What to Check When Buying Used

The battery is the heart of the EV9. It is also the component that most potential buyers worry about when considering a used electric vehicle. Let us address those concerns systematically.

### 5.1 Battery Basics

The EV9 uses lithium-ion battery cells (the same chemistry used in smartphones and laptops, but on a much larger scale). Lithium-ion batteries degrade gradually over time and with use. Degradation means the battery's usable capacity shrinks — a pack that once held 99.8 kWh effectively may only hold 93 kWh after several years of use. The car still works perfectly; it just has a little less range.

The rate of degradation depends on several factors:

- **Charging habits:** Regularly charging to 100% and depleting to 0% accelerates degradation. The sweet spot for daily charging is 20–80%. Kia's onboard software allows you to set a charge limit (most owners set 80% for daily use and 100% only before long trips).
- **Fast charging frequency:** Daily DC fast charging is harder on the battery than home Level 2 charging. Occasional fast charging on road trips is fine.
- **Temperature:** Extreme cold slows charging and temporarily reduces range. Extreme heat accelerates long-term degradation. The EV9 has an active thermal management system that heats or cools the battery as needed, which significantly mitigates temperature effects compared to EVs without thermal management.
- **Time:** Even sitting unused, lithium-ion batteries slowly self-discharge and degrade.

Real-world data from E-GMP vehicles (primarily the EV6 and Ioniq 5, which have been on the road longer than the EV9) shows that well-maintained batteries retain approximately 90–95% of their original capacity after 100,000 miles. The EV9 you are considering with 13,500 miles or 23,000 miles should have essentially all of its original capacity — degradation at those mileages is typically 2–5%, barely perceptible.

### 5.2 Kia's Battery Warranty

Kia provides a **10-year / 100,000-mile limited battery and EV system warranty** on the EV9 in the United States. This covers the high-voltage battery, electric motors, and associated power electronics against defects. If the battery's capacity falls below a manufacturer-specified threshold (typically 70% of original capacity) within the warranty period, Kia is obligated to repair or replace it.

This warranty is **transferable** to subsequent owners — meaning if you buy a used 2024 EV9 that was originally sold in late 2023, you still have nine-plus years and 100,000-minus-the-odometer-reading of battery warranty coverage. For a car with 13,500 or 23,000 miles, the majority of that 100,000-mile warranty is still in front of you.

Additionally, when you buy used from a Kia CPO program, you may receive additional powertrain warranty coverage on top of the original factory warranty.

### 5.3 How to Check Battery Health Before Buying

This is perhaps the most important pre-purchase step for any used EV.

**Ask for a battery health diagnostic report.** Any Kia dealer can run a diagnostic that reads the battery management system and reports state of health (SOH) — the battery's current capacity as a percentage of its original capacity. Ask the seller to obtain this report before you commit. If they are unwilling, treat it as a yellow flag. If a dealer runs the test and it shows SOH below 90% on a car with fewer than 30,000 miles, negotiate a significant discount or walk away.

**Check the range estimate on a full charge.** When a car is charged to 100% (or 80% with the charge limit set to 80%), the instrument cluster shows the estimated remaining range. For a 2024 EV9 Wind AWD with the long-range battery, the dash should show approximately 220–240 miles at 80% charge in moderate weather. If the dash range is noticeably lower, the battery may have degraded more than expected.

**Understand the limits of the range estimate.** The range estimate is computed by the car's computer based on recent driving history. If the previous owner drove aggressively with climate control blasting, the range estimate will be pessimistic. If they drove conservatively on flat roads, it will be optimistic. The diagnostic report (SOH percentage) is more reliable than the dash range estimate.

**Use a third-party EV diagnostic service.** Platforms like Recharged specifically pull battery state-of-health data and charge behavior history on used EVs before listing them. If you are buying from such a platform, the report is part of the transaction. If you are buying privately, consider hiring a mobile EV technician or taking the car to a Kia dealer for a pre-purchase inspection — typically $100–$200 well spent.

## Part 6: The ICCU — The Issue Everyone Is Talking About, Explained Clearly

If you have spent any time researching the EV9 on forums, you have encountered discussion of the "ICCU." This acronym generates more anxiety than almost any other topic in the Hyundai/Kia EV community. Let us explain exactly what it is, what the problem has been, and what it means for you as a potential used EV9 buyer.

### 6.1 What Is the ICCU?

ICCU stands for Integrated Charging Control Unit. It is a component roughly the size of a large hardback book, located in the engine compartment (technically, the front motor compartment, since there is no engine). Its functions:

1. **Onboard AC charging:** When you plug into a Level 1 or Level 2 charger, the ICCU converts the incoming AC power to DC power at the correct voltage and current to charge the main high-voltage battery. This is the "onboard charger" function. DC fast charging bypasses the ICCU entirely — it goes directly to the battery.

2. **12V battery maintenance:** The car needs to keep its 12V auxiliary battery charged for low-voltage accessories (lights, computers, locks, etc.). The ICCU performs this function, converting a small amount of energy from the high-voltage battery to the 12V system. This is the job that an alternator does in a gas car.

3. **Vehicle-to-Load (V2L) output:** The ICCU manages the power flow when you use the car's 120V outlet to power external devices.

### 6.2 What Is the ICCU Problem?

Starting with the Hyundai Ioniq 5 and Kia EV6 — the first E-GMP vehicles — owners began reporting ICCU failures that manifested in several ways:

- The 12V auxiliary battery depletes suddenly, making the car unable to start (it shows as fully charged but won't respond)
- AC charging (Level 1 or Level 2) fails — the car refuses to charge at home even though DC fast charging still works
- Error messages appear on the instrument cluster related to the charging system
- In some cases, the car loses power while driving

Hyundai and Kia issued recalls in 2024 covering approximately 146,000 vehicles (primarily Ioniq 5, Ioniq 6, and EV6) for ICCU-related issues. The recalls required software updates and, in some cases, ICCU hardware replacement.

The ICCU is Hyundai and Kia's integrated charge controller, which manages a lot of things, including onboard charging and keeping the 12-volt battery topped up. Several Hyundai and Kia EVs are part of ICCU-related recalls, and issues with the ICCU have been well documented, but data suggests they are rare and only affect approximately 1% of vehicles.

Hyundai redesigned the ICCU for vehicles built after certain production cutoff dates: for Hyundai, the extended warranty applies to Ioniq 5 models built up to April 2024 and Ioniq 6 units produced until September 2024, with vehicles manufactured after those dates equipped with an ICCU featuring an optimised design. At Kia, the measure covers eligible pre-facelift versions of the Kia EV6 and EV6 GT built before 15 June 2024.

Additionally, Hyundai has extended its warranty coverage on affected vehicles: Hyundai has already extended the warranty to a whopping 15 years and 180,000 miles for cars that are potentially affected by a faulty ICCU, and Kia is expected to do a similar thing for the EV6.

### 6.3 What About the EV9 Specifically?

Here is the genuinely reassuring news: the Kia EV9 has not been recalled for any ICCU-related issue, which is always a good sign.

Only the 2026 EV9 does not have the faulty ICCU design since it has a NACS port that required a different ICCU. However, EV9 owners have reported some ICCU-adjacent symptoms on the 2024 and 2025 models — primarily AC charging quirks rather than total failures. On earlier E-GMP vehicles like the EV6, ICCU faults could cause AC charging to fail entirely. A handful of EV9 owners have reported similar behavior — AC charging sessions that stop prematurely or won't start, while DC fast charging still works.

The good news is that once the right technical service bulletin is applied, and bad modules are replaced, most owners see their charging reliability improve dramatically. The bad news is that parts delays and misdiagnosis can leave a vehicle sidelined for weeks if your dealer isn't proactive.

**The practical implication for used EV9 buyers:**

Ask the seller for the vehicle's complete service history. Run the VIN through NHTSA's recall database (nhtsa.gov/recalls) and Kia's own recall lookup tool. Confirm that any open recalls or Technical Service Bulletins (TSBs) have been completed. If the car has a history of AC charging failures, repeated ICCU-related repairs, or the same repair done more than once without resolution, walk away or negotiate a substantial discount.

If the service history is clean and the car charges normally on both AC and DC, you are in good shape. The EV9 has a better ICCU track record than the EV6 and Ioniq 5, and the 10-year/100,000-mile battery and EV system warranty covers ICCU-related failures.

## Part 8: Maintenance — The Electric Advantage

This is where the EV9 story gets genuinely compelling for anyone who has owned a gas car and dreads its maintenance schedule.

### 8.1 No Oil Changes

Gasoline and diesel engines require engine oil to lubricate the hundreds of moving metal parts in the combustion process. Oil degrades with heat and use, picks up contaminants, and must be changed every 3,000–10,000 miles depending on the vehicle and oil type. At $50–$120 per oil change for a large SUV, this is a recurring cost that adds up over ownership.

The EV9 has no combustion engine. There is no engine oil. There are no oil changes. Ever. Not once. Not in 300,000 miles.

### 8.2 No Transmission Service

The EV9 uses a single-speed fixed-ratio reduction gear — not a traditional automatic or CVT transmission with clutch packs, solenoids, and transmission fluid that requires periodic service. The reduction gear uses a small amount of lubricant, but it does not require the regular service intervals of a traditional transmission.

### 8.3 No Spark Plugs, Timing Belt, or Exhaust System

The EV9 has no spark plugs (there is no spark combustion), no timing belt or timing chain (there is no valve train), no catalytic converter, no muffler, no resonator, no oxygen sensors for combustion management, and no exhaust system of any kind. The list of components you never need to replace is substantial.

### 8.4 Regenerative Braking Extends Brake Life Dramatically

In a gas car, slowing down is accomplished almost entirely by friction brakes — metal pads pressing against metal rotors, converting kinetic energy to heat. Brake pads wear out and must be replaced every 20,000–50,000 miles depending on driving style. Rotors eventually groove and must also be replaced.

The EV9 uses **regenerative braking** as its primary deceleration method. When you lift your foot from the accelerator (and particularly when you apply the brake pedal gently), the electric motors switch into generator mode and convert the car's kinetic energy back into electricity, which flows back into the battery. This slows the car while recovering energy (partially offsetting energy used for acceleration). The friction brakes are used only for hard stops.

The result: EV9 owners typically see their brake pads last 60,000–100,000 miles or more — two to three times the lifespan of brake pads in a comparable gas SUV. Some EV owners have driven 150,000+ miles with original brake pads. The tradeoff is that EV brake rotors can develop surface rust from infrequent use (moisture sits on the rotor surface and oxidizes) — harmless in most cases, but occasionally producing a scraping sound for the first stop of the day. This is normal and not a maintenance concern.

### 8.5 The Maintenance Schedule for a Used EV9

Here is what the EV9 actually needs on a regular basis:

**Tires (every 5,000–7,500 miles):** Rotate tires as per Kia's schedule. EVs are heavier than comparable gas vehicles (the battery adds roughly 1,000 pounds over a gas-powered SUV of similar size) and produce substantial torque from the motors, which accelerates front tire wear. Rotation is more important for EVs than for gas cars. Replacement intervals vary by driving style and tire type, but expect 40,000–60,000 miles per set.

**Cabin air filter (every 15,000–25,000 miles):** The EV9's HVAC system has a cabin air filter that should be replaced according to the maintenance schedule. This is a simple DIY job or a $30–$60 dealer service.

**Brake fluid (every 2 years):** Brake fluid is hygroscopic — it absorbs water from the atmosphere over time, lowering its boiling point and potentially causing brake fade under heavy use. Kia recommends replacing it every two years regardless of mileage. This is a $50–$100 service.

**Wiper blades (as needed, typically 12–24 months):** Yes, the EV9 needs wiper blades. This is one of the maintenance items that is exactly the same as a gas car. Wiper blades are simple to replace (a few dollars at an auto parts store) and take about five minutes. The EV9 also uses windshield washer fluid — yes, you fill the washer reservoir with the same fluid you use in any car. The EV9's front washer fluid reservoir is located under the frunk (front trunk) cover. It holds approximately 3.4 liters.

**Coolant for battery thermal management:** The EV9's battery pack and power electronics have their own liquid cooling system. Kia recommends inspecting the coolant level periodically and replacing the coolant per the maintenance schedule (typically at 120,000 miles or 6 years). This is not an annual item.

**12-volt auxiliary battery (as needed):** The 12V battery (the small traditional lead-acid battery that powers accessories when the main system is off) has a typical lifespan of 3–5 years. EV auxiliary batteries often last longer than in gas cars because the ICCU keeps them at optimal charge more consistently than an alternator in a gas car. But they do eventually wear out and need replacement. A 12V battery replacement is a $100–$200 service.

**High-voltage battery:** Under normal use, you should not need to replace the high-voltage traction battery within the 10-year/100,000-mile warranty period. If the car is well within that period when you buy it, battery replacement is Kia's responsibility if capacity falls below specification.

| Kia EV9 (annual average over 100K miles) | $500 – $900 |

The EV9 saves you real money on maintenance over a long ownership period. The bulk of the savings comes from eliminating oil changes, transmission service, and extending brake life.

## Part 9: Tax Implications — The Honest 2026 Picture

This section explains the federal tax landscape for EV purchases in mid-2026 in plain, accurate terms. Tax law changed significantly in 2025, and a great deal of outdated information is still circulating online.

### 9.1 What Happened: The One Big Beautiful Bill Act

For several years following the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, the federal government offered:
- A **new EV tax credit** of up to $7,500 for qualifying new electric vehicles (Section 30D)
- A **used EV tax credit** of up to $4,000 (30% of sale price, capped at $4,000) for qualifying used electric vehicles priced at $25,000 or less (Section 25E)

The Inflation Reduction Act's Clean Vehicle Credit (Section 30D) — which provided up to $7,500 for new EVs and $4,000 for used EVs (Section 25E) — was repealed by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025.

The credit remained available for vehicles purchased on or before September 30, 2025. After that date, the point-of-sale transfer, MSRP caps, battery sourcing rules, and income limits associated with the old credit are all gone — because the credit itself no longer exists.

The practical implication for you, buying a used EV9 in June 2026: **there is no federal used EV tax credit available for your purchase.** The $4,000 used EV credit is gone. The $7,500 new EV credit is also gone for new purchases. Any dealer or listing still advertising the $4,000 used EV credit for a 2026 purchase is working from outdated information.

### 9.2 What Replaced It: The OBBBA Auto Loan Interest Deduction

The old $7,500 IRA purchase credit expired September 30, 2025. It was replaced by a new $10,000 annual loan interest deduction under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) — above-the-line, repeating annually through 2028, and accessible to most buyers who do not itemize. New vehicles only, U.S. assembly required, no leases.

Key details of this new deduction:
- It applies only to **new vehicles**, not used vehicles
- The vehicle must be **assembled in the United States** (not just North America — specifically the U.S.)
- Maximum deduction: **$10,000 per year** in loan interest paid
- This is a **deduction**, not a credit — it reduces your taxable income, not your tax bill dollar for dollar. At a 22% marginal tax rate, a $10,000 deduction saves you $2,200 in taxes. At 12%, it saves $1,200.
- It runs through tax year 2028

**For used EV9 buyers in mid-2026:** This deduction does not apply. It is for new vehicles only.

### 9.3 The Home EV Charger Credit (Section 30C)

If you install qualified vehicle refueling and recharging property at your home, including electric vehicle charging equipment, and place it in service before July 1, 2026, you may be eligible for the Alternative Fuel Vehicle Refueling Property Tax Credit.

This credit provides 30% of the cost of your EV charger and its installation, up to **$1,000**. If you spend $1,500 on a Level 2 EVSE and its installation, you get a $450 federal tax credit. If you spend $3,000 or more, the credit maxes out at $1,000.

**This credit expires June 30, 2026.** If you are reading this in June 2026, you have a narrow window to install a charger and claim this credit. Move quickly.

### 9.4 State-Level Incentives

While federal incentives have contracted, many states maintain their own EV incentives. Virginia (where many of our readers are based) does not currently have a state EV purchase rebate, but the state does offer:
- Exemption from emissions testing requirements for EVs
- HOV lane access for EVs (depending on current state rules)

States with significant remaining used EV incentives in 2026 include Colorado ($6,000 VXC for used EVs) and Illinois ($4,000 for used EVs). If you live in one of these states, research current eligibility carefully — these programs have income limits and vehicle price caps.

### 9.5 Sales Tax, Registration, and Annual Fees

Depending on your state:
- **Sales tax** applies to vehicle purchases in most states. On a $35,000 used EV9 purchase, sales tax of 5–8% means $1,750–$2,800 in additional cost. Factor this into your total budget.
- **Registration fees** for EVs often include an annual "EV surcharge" or "road use fee" in lieu of the gas taxes EVs do not pay at the pump. In Virginia, this fee is $109.70 per year for EVs. It is modest compared to the fuel savings but worth knowing about.
- **Property tax** on vehicles applies in some states (Virginia again — you pay annual personal property tax on the assessed value of your vehicle). An EV9 assessed at $30,000 with a 4% personal property tax rate means $1,200 per year in property tax. This is not EV-specific, but it is a meaningful annual cost.

He has used the V2L feature twice: once to power a portable speaker and small cooler at a tailgate, and once during a brief power outage to run a box fan and phone chargers for about 4 hours. "The car being a home battery backup was not something I expected to care about," he notes. "But when the power went out last October, I was really glad I had it."

His one complaint: the Kia Connect app has had two outages where remote climate control and charging monitoring stopped working for 24–48 hours. Both resolved on their own without dealer involvement. He considers this a minor annoyance, not a meaningful problem.

### Case Study 3 — The Chen Family, Dallas, Texas

The Chen family was initially attracted to the EV9's towing capacity. They own a 19-foot camping trailer (dry weight: 3,800 pounds, GVWR: 5,200 pounds). They were concerned that the EV9's EPA range of 270 miles would be severely reduced while towing.

Their concern was correct. Towing with an EV reduces range significantly — typically 30–50% depending on speed, terrain, and trailer aerodynamics. At highway towing speeds (65–70 mph), the EV9 realistically delivers 130–170 miles of range with a trailer. For their camping trips to lakes and state parks within 80 miles of home, this is completely workable — they arrive, plug into the campsite's 30-amp service (a NEMA TT-30 RV outlet, which the EV9's portable cable can adapt to), and charge overnight for the return trip.

For longer trips (200+ miles with the trailer), they plan around Electrify America stations, adding a 30-minute charging stop every 100–120 miles. It requires more planning than filling up with diesel, but they find the fuel savings — camping weekends that previously cost $120–$140 in fuel now cost $20–$30 in electricity — make the planning worthwhile.

Their advice: "Don't buy the EV9 expecting gas-car towing range. It is not that. But if your towing use case is realistic distances with overnight recovery time, it works."

## Part 12: Frequently Asked Questions

**Does it need oil changes?**
No. The EV9 has no internal combustion engine and uses no engine oil. There are no oil changes, ever. Ever.

**It needs wipers and wiper fluid, right?**
Yes. Windshield wiper blades are standard maintenance, identical to any car. Replace them when they streak, typically every 12–24 months, for $15–$35 at any auto parts store. The windshield washer fluid reservoir is located under the frunk cover at the front of the car. Fill it with standard washer fluid. The capacity is approximately 3.4 liters (3.6 quarts).

**Can I charge it in the rain?**
Yes. EV charging ports and connectors are weatherproofed. You can plug in during light rain without concern. The car will not shock you. EV charging connectors have elaborate safety interlocks — power does not flow through the connector until it is fully seated in the port.

**Can I drive it through a car wash?**
Yes, including touchless automated car washes and hand washing. The battery, motors, and charging ports are sealed against water ingress. You should keep the charge port door closed when going through a car wash (it closes automatically when you unplug).

**What happens if the battery dies completely?**
You would need to be towed. The EV9 will give you extensive low-range warnings well before you reach 0% — typically starting at 20%, with increasingly urgent warnings below 10%. The recommended practice: do not let the battery go below 10% in daily driving. Below 10%, performance and regenerative braking are reduced, and charging behavior changes. Plan routes to ensure you have adequate charge.

**Can I keep it plugged in all the time?**
Yes. The EV9 (like virtually all modern EVs) is designed to remain plugged in indefinitely. Once the battery reaches your set charge limit (typically 80%), it stops drawing power. It maintains that charge level passively. You will not "overcharge" it by leaving it plugged in.

**What about cold weather?**
Range decreases in cold weather, typically 10–30% depending on temperature and whether you use cabin heating. The EV9 has a heat pump (on AWD models) that improves heating efficiency compared to simple electric resistance heating. Pre-conditioning — warming the cabin while the car is still plugged in — is the key habit: you warm the car on plug-in power rather than on battery, so you leave home with a warm cabin and full range. The Kia Connect app enables pre-conditioning scheduling.

**What about very hot weather?**
Heat increases air conditioning load, reducing range by 10–20% in very hot conditions. The battery thermal management system actively cools the battery to protect it. Parking in shade or a garage reduces the cooling load.

**Do I need a special insurance policy?**
No. Standard auto insurance covers EVs. However, EV9 repairs can be expensive due to the complexity of the high-voltage system — not all body shops have EV-trained technicians. When shopping for insurance, ask if your insurer has preferred repair shops with EV certification. Some insurers offer slightly higher rates for EVs to account for higher repair costs; others offer discounts for EVs as a recognition of lower fire risk and lower mechanical complexity.

**What is V2H and do I need special equipment?**
Vehicle-to-Home (V2H) allows the EV9 to power your home during an outage. It requires a bidirectional home charger (separate from a standard Level 2 EVSE, and considerably more expensive — typically $3,000–$7,000 installed) and appropriate electrical panel modifications. Standard Level 2 charging installations do not enable V2H. V2L (powering a 120V device directly from the car) requires no special home equipment — just plug in through the car's built-in outlet.

The average American family buys a large SUV for space, safety, and capability. The EV9 delivers all three. It adds the practical advantages of home charging convenience, dramatically lower fuel costs, substantially reduced maintenance, and technology that continues to improve through software updates. It asks in return that you install a Level 2 charger, plan road trips slightly more carefully than in a gas car, and stay attentive to the warranty and recall history of the specific VIN you buy.

If you have read this article in full, you now know more about the EV9, EV charging, battery technology, the ICCU, maintenance, and the 2026 tax landscape than most buyers who walk into a dealership. It is the part that makes EV owners overwhelmingly likely to say, a year into ownership, that they will never go back to a gas car.

The used Kia EV9 is a very good deal. More importantly, for the right buyer, it is a very good car.
