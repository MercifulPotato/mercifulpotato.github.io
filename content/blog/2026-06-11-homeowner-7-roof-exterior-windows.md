## Part 1: The Envelope Is Everything

The building envelope is the boundary between the conditioned interior of your home and the exterior environment. It includes the roof, the exterior walls and their cladding, the windows, the doors, and the foundation — everything that separates inside from outside.

The envelope has three primary functions:
1. **Keep water out** — rain, snow, groundwater, condensation
2. **Control heat transfer** — resist the movement of heat between inside and outside
3. **Control air movement** — prevent uncontrolled infiltration and exfiltration of air

All three functions interact. A roof leak lets water in, damages insulation (which reduces heat transfer resistance), and can create air pathways where none existed. An air leak creates a condensation site where water vapor can condense and cause mold. A failed window seal reduces thermal performance and admits moisture.

But of the three, water is the dominant enemy. Almost every serious failure mode in the building envelope involves water — its infiltration, its movement through assemblies, its condensation on cold surfaces, its freezing and expansion. Understanding how water behaves and how building systems are designed to manage it is the master key to exterior maintenance.

## Part 2: The Roof — Your Home's First Defense

The roof is the element most homeowners think of first when they think about exterior maintenance, and for good reason: it is the largest, most exposed, and most consequential part of the building envelope. A failed roof allows water into the structure with alarming speed. A properly maintained roof protects everything below it for 20 to 50 years depending on the material.

### Roof Systems — Understanding the Layers

A roof is not a single material — it is a system of layers, each of which contributes to weather resistance:

**The structural deck:** Usually plywood or oriented strand board (OSB) sheathing, nailed to the rafters or trusses. This is the structural foundation of the roof assembly. Water that reaches the deck from either above (through failed roofing material) or below (condensation, ice dam backup) damages the deck rapidly — plywood delaminates and OSB swells, loses structural integrity, and must be replaced before re-roofing.

**The underlayment:** A layer of water-resistant material (traditionally 30-lb. roofing felt; now often synthetic underlayment or self-adhering membrane) installed over the deck beneath the finish roofing. The underlayment is the secondary line of defense — if water penetrates the finish material, the underlayment buys time and directs water off the roof rather than into the deck and structure.

**Ice and water shield:** A self-adhering, self-sealing rubberized asphalt membrane installed in vulnerable areas: the eaves (to protect against ice dam backup), the valleys (where two roof planes meet), and around penetrations. Required by code at the eaves in cold climates and at valleys in most jurisdictions.

**The finish roofing material:** The visible roof surface. This is what most people mean when they say "the roof."

**Flashing:** Thin strips of metal (typically aluminum or galvanized steel, occasionally copper or lead) installed at all transitions and penetrations: where the roof meets walls (step flashing), where two roof planes meet at a valley (valley flashing), around chimneys (step and counter flashing), around skylights, and around any pipe or equipment that penetrates the roof. Flashing is the most failure-prone element in any roof system — not because it fails first, but because it is the element where roofing and other building components interface, and those interfaces require the most detail work.

### Roofing Materials — What You Have and What It Means

**Asphalt shingles (most common):** The dominant residential roofing material in North America. Available in three-tab (flat) and architectural/dimensional (textured, layered look) styles. Architectural shingles are thicker, heavier, and longer-lasting than three-tab.

Lifespan: 20 to 30 years for architectural shingles; 15 to 25 years for three-tab. Both degrade faster in hot, sunny climates (UV radiation and heat are the primary degradation mechanisms) and in climates with extreme temperature cycling.

Signs of aging asphalt shingles: granule loss (the granules that give shingles their texture erode over time; a shingle approaching the end of its life looks smooth and bare in patches — check the gutters for accumulated granules); curling (the edges of shingles curl up or the centers cup down); cracking; and shingles that slide out of position.

Cost to replace: $10,000 to $25,000 for a typical residential home, depending on size, slope, complexity, and region.

**Metal roofing:** Standing seam steel or aluminum panels, or metal shingles. Much longer lifespan than asphalt (40 to 70 years for steel; longer for copper). Fire-resistant, low maintenance, energy-efficient (reflects solar radiation). Higher first cost ($15,000 to $35,000+). Excellent choice for a roof you want to be the last one you ever install.

**Architectural concrete or clay tile:** Common in the Southwest, Florida, and the Mediterranean-influenced architecture of coastal California. Very long lifespan (50+ years for clay, 40+ years for concrete). Heavy — the structure must be designed to support the weight. Individual tiles can crack and must be replaced; the underlayment beneath can fail before the tiles do.

**Slate:** The gold standard of roofing materials. Genuine slate roofs last 75 to 150 years. Very expensive ($30,000 to $80,000+ for a typical home), very heavy (requires reinforced structure), and requires specialized installation and repair by slate-experienced roofers. If your home has a slate roof, treat it as the asset it is.

**Wood shakes and shingles:** Beautiful, historical, but high maintenance. Require treatment every few years to prevent rot, moss, and fire risk. Many insurers charge higher premiums or decline to insure wood shake roofs. Not appropriate in fire-risk zones.

**Flat roofs (low slope):** Common on commercial buildings and on some residential styles (mid-century modern, additions, bump-outs). Flat roofs use different materials than pitched roofs: modified bitumen (torch-down or self-adhering), EPDM (synthetic rubber), TPO (thermoplastic polyolefin), or built-up roofing (tar and gravel). These are not truly flat — they must slope at minimum 1/4 inch per foot toward drains. Flat roofs require different maintenance and inspection protocols than pitched roofs and are most vulnerable to ponding water and to failure at seams and penetrations.

### Roof Inspection — What to Look For

**From the ground, every year:**
Use binoculars for a clear view. Look for:
- Missing or obviously damaged shingles
- Visible granule loss (smooth, bare-looking areas)
- Sagging at the ridge or between rafters (structural concern)
- Lifted, bubbled, or curled shingles
- Flashing that appears lifted or separated from adjacent surfaces
- Visible moss or algae (dark streaks — usually algae — or green patches — usually moss)

**Professional inspection every 2 to 3 years:**
A licensed roofing contractor should walk the roof (safely) and check:
- Shingle condition in detail
- All flashing: chimney, walls, valleys, pipe penetrations
- Ridge cap condition
- Gutter attachment and fascia condition
- Underlayment integrity at exposed edges
- Decking condition (any soft spots indicating rot)

Many reputable roofing contractors will do a roof inspection for free, especially if you are approaching replacement age. Get multiple inspections if you receive dramatically different assessments.

### Chimney Flashing — The Most Failure-Prone Roofing Detail

Where a chimney passes through the roof, water management is complex. Properly detailed chimney flashing consists of:
- **Step flashing:** L-shaped metal pieces that alternate with shingles up the sides of the chimney
- **Counter flashing:** Metal embedded in the mortar joints of the chimney masonry, lapping over the step flashing to prevent water running down the chimney face from getting behind the step flashing
- **Saddle or cricket:** A small triangular peaked structure behind the chimney that diverts water around it rather than allowing water to pond against the back of the chimney

Failed chimney flashing is one of the most common causes of roof leaks. The mortar holding counter flashing can crack and allow the flashing to lift. Step flashing can corrode or be improperly installed. The cricket may be absent on older roofs.

If you have a chimney, include it in every roof inspection.

### When a Roof Leak Occurs

When you discover a roof leak — water stain on the ceiling, wet insulation in the attic, water dripping — take these steps:

1. **Locate the water entry.** Water does not always enter directly above the stain. It can travel along rafters, decking, or insulation for several feet before dripping down. Get into the attic with a flashlight and look for wet insulation, water stains on rafters or decking, or daylight visible through the roof.
2. **Temporarily stop the damage.** If you can safely access the roof area, a tarp secured over the leak source is a reasonable temporary measure while waiting for a roofer.
3. **Photograph everything** for insurance purposes.
4. **Call a roofing contractor** for evaluation and repair.

### Moss and Algae

Dark streaks on asphalt shingles are algae — specifically Gloeocapsa magma, which thrives in warm, humid climates. Algae does not immediately damage shingles but is unsightly.

Green moss actually harms shingles by holding moisture against the surface and physically lifting shingle edges. It must be treated.

Treatment options:
- Zinc or copper strips installed at the ridge (rain washes zinc or copper ions down the roof, which inhibit algae and moss growth)
- Spray-on zinc sulfate, potassium salts of fatty acids, or commercial moss killers (do not use a pressure washer — it strips granules)
- Algae-resistant shingles (contain copper granules) when re-roofing

## Part 3: Gutters — The Water Management System Nobody Respects

Gutters are among the least glamorous features of a house and among the most important. Their job is simple: collect rainwater from the roof and direct it away from the foundation. When they fail to do this — through clogging, damage, improper slope, or inadequate discharge — the water they fail to direct goes to the foundation.

### The Consequences of Clogged or Failing Gutters

- **Foundation damage.** Water pooling at the foundation can saturate the soil, increase hydrostatic pressure against the foundation wall, and cause water infiltration into the basement or crawl space. Over time, saturation and freeze-thaw cycling can damage the foundation itself.
- **Fascia and soffit rot.** Water that overflows a clogged gutter runs down the fascia board (the trim board the gutter attaches to) and the soffit (the underside of the eave overhang). These wood members rot when repeatedly wet and can require expensive repair or replacement.
- **Ice dams.** Clogged gutters hold ice in winter, contributing to ice dam formation at the eaves.
- **Siding damage.** Overflow from gutters can cause staining, paint failure, and water intrusion at the wall below the eaves.
- **Landscape erosion.** Concentrated water discharge at downspout discharge points erodes soil and damages plantings.

### Gutter Cleaning — Frequency and Method

**Frequency:** Twice a year minimum — once in spring after pollen and early tree debris has fallen, once in late fall after most of the leaves have fallen. Homes under or near heavy-leafing trees (maple, oak, sweet gum) may need 3 or 4 cleanings per year.

**Method:**
1. Work safely — use a properly secured ladder. Never lean the top of the ladder against the gutter (gutters are not structural and will bend). Use ladder stabilizers (ladder stand-offs) that contact the exterior wall above the gutter.
2. Scoop debris by hand (gloved) or with a plastic gutter scoop into a bucket
3. Flush gutters with a garden hose, working toward the downspout
4. Check that water flows freely and quickly to the downspout — standing water after flushing indicates the gutter is not sloped correctly (gutters should slope 1/4 inch per 10 feet toward the downspout)
5. Flush downspouts — they should run freely. A clogged downspout can be cleared from the top with a garden hose at full pressure, or from the bottom with a plumber's snake, or by disconnecting the downspout sections and clearing each section.

**Gutter guards:** Various products (screens, micro-mesh, foam inserts, reverse curve) are marketed to reduce or eliminate gutter cleaning. The results are mixed. Micro-mesh guards from quality manufacturers (Gutter Helmet, LeafGuard, Mastershield, and others) work reasonably well and can significantly reduce cleaning frequency. No guard eliminates cleaning entirely. Less expensive screen and foam products tend to accumulate debris in the product itself. Evaluate gutter guards based on your specific situation — heavy deciduous tree coverage versus pine needles versus minimal tree coverage will have different best solutions.

### Downspout Discharge

Downspouts must discharge well away from the foundation. The minimum is 4 to 6 feet from the house, but 8 to 10 feet is better. Accomplish this with:
- **Downspout extensions:** Rigid or flexible plastic extensions that attach to the downspout elbow and redirect water further from the house. Not decorative, but extremely functional.
- **Splash blocks:** Concrete or plastic blocks placed at the downspout discharge point to distribute water and prevent erosion at that point.
- **Underground discharge:** In some situations, the downspout connects to underground drainage pipe that carries water to a drywell, a rain garden, or daylight at a low point of the property. This is the most elegant solution and the most maintenance-intensive (underground pipes can clog or crush over time).

## Part 4: Siding and Exterior Walls

The exterior wall cladding — siding — is the visible skin of your home. Beyond aesthetics, its function is to shed water (direct rain off the wall surface and away from the structure) and provide a first layer of protection against wind and weather. Below the siding is a water-resistant barrier (building wrap, felt paper, or housewrap) that provides a second line of defense.

### Common Siding Types

**Vinyl siding:** The most common siding material in new American construction. Durable, low-maintenance, never needs painting. Typical lifespan: 30 to 40 years. Vulnerabilities: can crack from impact (especially in cold weather, when it becomes brittle), fades over time (color is typically not uniform through the thickness, so scratches reveal different-colored material underneath), and can warp if installed too tightly or exposed to high heat. Individual panels can usually be replaced without full replacement.

**Fiber cement siding (James Hardie is the dominant brand):** A composite of Portland cement, sand, and cellulose fiber. Durable, fire-resistant, insect-resistant, paintable. Requires painting maintenance every 10 to 15 years. Does not rot, warp, or crack in cold. More expensive than vinyl at first cost but often preferred for its appearance and longevity. Typical lifespan: 40 to 50+ years.

**Wood siding (clapboard, shiplap, board and batten, shingles):** Traditional and attractive. Requires the most maintenance — painting or staining every 5 to 10 years, prompt repair of any damaged or rotted boards, attention to caulking at all joints and penetrations. High-quality wood siding (cedar, redwood, Douglas fir) properly maintained can last 50+ years. Poorly maintained wood siding can fail rapidly.

**Brick and stone:** Low-maintenance cladding with very long lifespans. The maintenance focus is the mortar joints — mortar is softer than brick and erodes over decades. Tuck-pointing (removing deteriorated mortar and replacing with fresh mortar) is the primary brick maintenance task. Deteriorated mortar allows water infiltration that can damage the structure behind the brick and cause brick spalling (freeze-thaw damage when water infiltrates the brick and freezes). Also check the flashing at window and door lintels (the horizontal structural element above each opening) and at the foundation transition.

**Stucco:** A cementitious coating applied in layers over metal lath attached to the wall sheathing. Traditional three-coat stucco (over a moisture-resistant barrier) is extremely durable. EIFS (Exterior Insulation Finish Systems, synthetic stucco) was widely used in the 1980s and 1990s and has a documented history of water intrusion problems when not properly detailed at windows, doors, and other penetrations. If your home has EIFS, have it inspected by a stucco specialist — any cracks at penetrations are potential water entry points.

**Engineered wood siding (LP SmartSide, Advantech Siding):** Treated wood composite products that offer wood appearance with improved water resistance. Requires painting. Generally well-regarded.

### Siding Maintenance

**Painting wooden siding:** Paint protects wood siding from moisture. When paint fails — cracks, peels, or chips — moisture can reach the wood directly and cause rot. Repainting is needed every 5 to 10 years. Signs that repainting is urgent: chalking, peeling, bare wood visible, cracking at joints.

**Caulking:** All joints in siding, around windows, doors, penetrations, and at corners should be caulked with appropriate exterior paintable caulk. Inspect and re-caulk as needed — caulk is the detail that fails most frequently. See Post 1 for caulk type guidance.

**Rot repair:** Wood siding rot is a localized problem when caught early. Rotted sections of individual boards can be cut out and replaced. Epoxy wood consolidant and filler products (LiquidWood, WoodEpox) can reinforce and fill small areas of soft rot in trim and framing. But these are temporary solutions — the source of moisture that caused the rot must be identified and eliminated, or the rot will return.

**Vinyl siding:** Cleaning with a garden hose and mild detergent annually maintains its appearance. Pressure washing is effective but use a low-pressure setting and work downward — water forced upward behind panels can cause moisture problems. Replace cracked or broken panels before water enters behind them.

## Part 5: Windows — Efficiency, Failure Modes, and Maintenance

Modern double-pane windows are a dramatic improvement over single-pane glass in thermal performance. A double-pane window has two glass layers separated by a sealed air or gas space (argon or krypton is used to improve thermal performance further). Low-E coatings on the glass selectively reflect radiant heat, keeping solar heat out in summer and interior heat in during winter.

### Failed Seals — The Fogged Window Problem

Every homeowner with older double-pane windows knows the fogged window: a window that is perpetually hazy or has visible condensation between the panes. This happens when the hermetic seal at the edge of the insulated glass unit (IGU) fails, allowing humid air to enter the space between the panes and condense there.

A failed seal reduces the window's thermal performance because the gas fill escapes and the desiccant that was absorbing moisture becomes saturated. The window is still functional as a weather barrier, but it is less energy-efficient than a functioning unit.

Options when a seal fails:
1. **Defogging service:** A technician drills small holes in the glass, injects a cleaning solution, and installs small vents. This removes the visible fogging but does not restore the gas fill or full thermal performance. An imperfect fix, but less expensive than replacement.
2. **Replacement of the IGU:** The glass unit itself can be replaced in many windows while keeping the existing frame. This is the better solution if the frame is in good condition.
3. **Full window replacement:** Necessary if the frame is also deteriorated or if the window is single-pane.

### Window and Door Weatherstripping

Every operable window and every exterior door relies on weatherstripping to create an airtight seal when closed. Weatherstripping degrades through UV exposure, temperature cycling, and compression over time.

Test weatherstripping: close the window or door on a piece of paper. If you can pull the paper out without resistance, the seal at that point is inadequate.

Replace worn weatherstripping before the heating season. Weatherstripping materials include foam tape (least durable), V-strip (spring metal or vinyl, very durable), felt (least durable), door sweeps (rubber or brush seals at the bottom of doors), and compression strips (foam with a rigid backing). Match the replacement to the existing type.

### Window Frame Maintenance

**Wood frames:** Paint and/or stain the exterior surfaces every 5 to 10 years. Caulk the joint between the frame and the surrounding siding annually. Check the sill (horizontal bottom piece) for rot — this is where water accumulates.

**Vinyl frames:** Clean with mild soap and water. No painting needed. Check and re-caulk the joint between frame and siding annually.

**Aluminum frames:** Check for oxidation (white powdery buildup) and clean. Lubricate window hardware annually.

### Condensation on the Interior Side of Windows

Condensation on the interior surface of windows (not between the panes) is a normal winter phenomenon when indoor humidity is high and outdoor temperatures are very cold. If it is excessive or persistent:
- Reduce indoor humidity (run exhaust fans in bathrooms and the kitchen, ensure the dryer vents to the exterior, avoid large numbers of indoor plants in winter)
- Consider a dehumidifier
- Improve air circulation at the windows (open blinds and curtains partially to allow heated room air to reach the glass)

This is not a window problem — it is a humidity management problem.

## Part 6: Exterior Doors

Exterior doors are part of the building envelope. They must seal against air and water infiltration, provide security, and resist weathering.

**Door weatherstripping:** Inspect and replace annually as described above.

**Door sweeps and thresholds:** The bottom of every exterior door should have a functioning door sweep (a rubber or brush seal that presses against the threshold) and a threshold with an adjustable seal that creates a watertight joint when the door is closed. Cold air infiltration at the bottom of doors is very common and very easy to fix with a replacement sweep or threshold adjustment.

**Door hardware:** Lubricate hinges, deadbolts, and locksets annually with a dry lubricant (graphite powder or a silicone-based spray — avoid oil-based lubricants which attract dirt). A door that is difficult to close or lock is often caused by a sagging hinge (tighten the hinge screws; if the holes are stripped, use longer screws or wood filler before reinstalling the hinge) or a door that has swollen seasonally (this is normal for wood doors in high-humidity climates).

**Door frame and threshold decay:** Wood door frames and thresholds are the most failure-prone exterior wood elements because they are in contact with or very near grade and receive water impact from rain. Inspect for rot annually. The bottom corners of door frames and the threshold are the highest-risk areas.

**Garage doors:** The garage door bottom seal should be replaced when it shows cracking, gaps, or poor contact with the floor. Lubricate the tracks, rollers, and hinges with a garage door lubricant (not WD-40 for ongoing lubrication — it attracts dirt; use a silicone spray or lithium grease). Test the auto-reverse function monthly as described in Post 2. The springs above the garage door are under extreme tension and are dangerous — spring replacement and adjustment should be done by a garage door professional, not by a homeowner.

## Part 7: Grading and Drainage — The Foundation of the Envelope

All of the roof, gutter, and siding work above is about managing water after it has landed on or near the house. Grading and drainage manages water before it reaches the house — in the soil and on the surface.

**Grade:** The slope of the soil around the house. Code and best practice require the finish grade to slope away from the foundation at a minimum of 1 inch per foot for the first 6 feet from the house. Over time, soil settles, organic mulch decomposes and lowers the grade, and grading that was once adequate can reverse.

Walk around your house after a heavy rain. If water is flowing toward the house, pooling against the foundation, or staying on the surface for an extended period near the house, you have a grading problem. Correcting it by adding soil and re-grading toward the minimum slope is a significant but manageable improvement — for most homes it requires a few cubic yards of fill soil and several hours of work.

**Window wells:** Basement windows below grade are set in window wells — curved metal or plastic retaining walls that hold back the surrounding soil and allow light and air to reach the window. Window well drains should connect to the foundation drain system or at minimum be filled with gravel to allow water to percolate away. Clean window wells of debris seasonally. Consider covers for window wells that are prone to filling with leaves or debris.

**Foundation drains:** Below the footing of most basements is a perforated drainage pipe (French drain) that collects groundwater and directs it to a sump pit (from which the sump pump discharges it away from the house) or to daylight at a low point on the property. This system can fail if the drain pipe becomes crushed, clogged with silt, or root-infiltrated. Symptoms of a failing foundation drain include water seeping through the floor-wall joint in the basement, hydrostatic pressure on the foundation wall, and a sump pump that runs continuously during dry periods.
