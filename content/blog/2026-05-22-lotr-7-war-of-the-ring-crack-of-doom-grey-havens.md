# The War of the Ring: From the Breaking of the Fellowship to the Grey Havens

*This is the final article in the comprehensive in-universe chronological history of Middle-earth. The previous six articles covered everything from the creation of Arda through the breaking of the Fellowship at Amon Hen. We complete now the story of the War of the Ring — the events of The Two Towers and The Return of the King — and carry the account forward to the true end of the Third Age: the last ship departing the Grey Havens, and the world that was left behind.*

*A note on structure: the events of The Two Towers and The Return of the King unfold in multiple simultaneous threads. Tolkien told them sequentially — first the Rohan/Ent thread through to Helm's Deep and Saruman's fall, then the Frodo/Sam/Gollum thread through to Shelob's lair, then the Return of the King's Gondor/Rohan thread to the Battle of the Pelennor Fields, then the siege and the final push, then the last thread at the Crack of Doom. We will follow this structure, noting where events are simultaneous.*

---

## Part 1: Gandalf's Fall and Return — What Happened in the Abyss

The Fellowship left Moria believing Gandalf dead. They were wrong, but the truth of what happened is more extraordinary than simple survival.

When Gandalf fell with the Balrog into the pit below the Bridge of Khazad-dûm, they fell for a very long time. The abyss beneath Moria was not merely deep in the geological sense; it was one of the oldest and deepest spaces under the Misty Mountains, carved by the Dwarves over millennia. They fell into water — an underground lake — and from there the Balrog dragged Gandalf through tunnels and passages in the dark, fighting as they went. Water, fire, narrow stone — they fought across all of it.

Then they climbed. Tolkien's account of this, in the chapter "The White Rider" of The Two Towers, is told retrospectively in Gandalf's own words to Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli in Fangorn. He describes the ascent as something beyond ordinary exertion — the Balrog became a creature of fire again in the open air of the upper passages, and they fought on the peak of Zirakzigil, above the clouds, in the high cold, for two days and two nights. This is Olórin against a Balrog of Morgoth, two Maiar in their full terrible nature fighting on a mountain summit.

At the dawn of the third day, Gandalf threw down the Balrog. It broke the mountainside as it fell. Then Gandalf himself died — of exhaustion, of the wounds of the long battle, of the sheer expenditure of power involved. His body lay on the peak of Zirakzigil while his spirit departed.

But the story was not done. Manwë, or Ilúvatar directly — the narrative does not specify — sent him back. He returned to his body, renewed. He was no longer Gandalf the Grey; he was Gandalf the White. Saruman had been the White and had failed his office; the office was now given to Gandalf, and with it came an increase of power, a lifting of the restrictions that had previously bound him. He was not the full power of a Maia unleashed — the Istari's physical embodiment still constrained him — but he was more than he had been.

He was found by Gwaihir the Windlord and carried to Lothlórien, where Galadriel healed him and clothed him in white. Then he went to Fangorn, arriving before Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli found him there.

This return — death and renewal, the transformation from Grey to White — is one of the most clearly Tolkien-coded moments in the entire mythology. The parallels to resurrection are deliberate. Tolkien did not intend allegory — he was always firm about that — but he was a devout Catholic who understood that certain narrative structures carry certain meanings, and the pattern of the chosen servant who dies in the course of his mission and is sent back renewed, with greater authority, is a pattern he chose deliberately. Gandalf's return is not merely a plot convenience. It is a statement about what the story is doing.

## Part 2: Merry and Pippin in Fangorn — The Ents Go to War

The Uruk-hai who had taken Merry and Pippin were running north, heading for Isengard on Saruman's orders. Saruman wanted the Hobbits — he knew something of the Ring, had been watching the Shire (Gríma Wormtongue, his agent in Rohan, had provided him with information about the Shire and about Hobbits, and Saruman himself had been secretly investigating the matter of the Ring), and believed one of the Hobbits might have it.

The Riders of Rohan — the Rohirrim, the horse-lords, whose kingdom lay between the Misty Mountains and Gondor — caught the Uruk-hai at the edge of Fangorn Forest. Their commander was Éomer, Third Marshal of the Mark, nephew of King Théoden. The Rohirrim surrounded the Uruk-hai camp through the night and attacked at dawn, killing every Orc. They did not know about the Hobbits; in the chaos, Merry and Pippin escaped into Fangorn.

Fangorn Forest — called Entwood by the Rohirrim, who stayed away from it on principle — was the last great forest of the Elder Days, the remnant of the forests that had once covered much of Middle-earth. It was tended by the Ents — the Tree-herders, the Shepherds of the Trees, made by Yavanna in the First Age in response to her grief at seeing her growing things unprotected. The Ents were ancient beyond reckoning, and they were slow. Deliberately slow: an Ent-moot, a gathering to make a decision, took days, because Ents were very careful not to say anything unless they meant it.

Treebeard — Fangorn himself, the oldest living thing in Middle-earth who was not a Maia or a Vala — found Merry and Pippin. He was fourteen feet tall, tree-like in his appearance, with bark-like skin and deep eyes that had seen the First Age. His Elvish name was Fangorn, meaning Treebeard in Quenya; his name in his own tongue no one mortal could say. He called the Hobbits orcs at first, then revised his thinking, and eventually called them Hobbits and accepted them as guests.

Merry and Pippin spent their time in Fangorn eating and drinking Ent-draughts — the drink of the Ents, which may have permanently added an inch or two to both Hobbits, explaining why they were slightly taller than ordinary Hobbits when they returned to the Shire — and telling Treebeard what was happening in the world. The news that Saruman had been cutting down trees along the borders of Fangorn for his furnaces was the decisive thing. The Ents were not quick to anger; but once decided, they were unstoppable.

The Ent-moot assembled. For three days, the Ents deliberated in their own slow language — Tolkien's Entish was a language in which nothing could be said quickly, where names were complete histories of their subjects. At the end of three days, they decided: they would go to war.

The march of the Ents on Isengard is one of the great images of The Two Towers. The forests of Fangorn moving — trees that moved slowly but moved with the weight and power of very large things that have been patient for a very long time and are now done with patience. Huorns preceded them — tree-like creatures of uncertain nature, not quite Ents, which moved quickly under cover of darkness and smelled of trees and darkness. The Huorns reached the outer walls of Isengard and surrounded them, and when the Ents broke the walls — tearing up the gates, breaking the dams of Saruman's river-diversions, flooding the pits and machinery of his industrial apparatus — the Orcs inside ran into the Huorns and were never seen again. Not one escaped.

Saruman himself was not destroyed. He locked himself in Orthanc, the black pillar-tower of indestructible stone, and the Ents could not break the tower. He was trapped there. When Gandalf, Aragorn, and the riders of Théoden arrived, Saruman appeared on Orthanc's balcony and attempted his most characteristic weapon: his voice. Saruman's voice was his greatest natural gift as a wizard — it was persuasive beyond ordinary rhetoric, capable of making any suggestion seem reasonable, of finding the specific angle that would move each individual listener. Even knowing what he was doing, the sound of it was almost irresistible.

Gandalf broke the spell. He raised his staff and there was a crack, and Saruman's voice fell silent. Then Gandalf formally expelled Saruman from the order of Istari and broke his staff. Saruman's power as a Wizard was ended. He retreated into Orthanc, and Treebeard was left to guard him.

From a window of Orthanc, Gríma Wormtongue — Saruman's spy and agent who had been with him in the tower — threw down a heavy object in frustration. It was a palantír, a Seeing Stone, that Saruman had possessed and had been using to watch the world and to communicate with Sauron. Pippin picked it up before Gandalf saw him; later, in camp that night, Pippin could not resist looking into it. He looked into the palantír and the Eye of Sauron found him and questioned him. Gandalf recovered the stone before the damage was complete, but Sauron had seen a Hobbit with the Stone — and assumed, characteristically, that Pippin was the Ring-bearer, which was wrong. This misdirection was actually useful: it confirmed for Sauron that the Ring was somewhere in the vicinity of Isengard or Rohan, not in Mordor, and led him to deploy his forces accordingly — which bought time.

Gandalf rode hard with Pippin to Minas Tirith, fearing that Sauron would move against Gondor immediately.

## Part 3: Théoden and Helm's Deep — The Rohirrim Ride

King Théoden of Rohan had been under the influence of Gríma Wormtongue for years — the exact mechanism is described as a kind of slow poisoning of thought and spirit, whether through actual corrupting spells from Saruman through Gríma or simply through constant pessimistic counsel that sapped the king's will. When Gandalf arrived at Edoras, Théoden's great hall, with Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli, Théoden was an old man slumped in his chair, already seeming ancient though he was not yet seventy.

Gandalf's intervention was not magic in the simple sense — it was closer to a breaking of the spell, a restoration of Théoden's own strength. He spoke to Théoden directly, forcefully, and the king stood and walked to the door of Meduseld and breathed the air of Rohan, and found himself again. He was old, but he was himself. He picked up his sword. He banished Gríma. He called his people to war.

The Rohirrim rode to Helm's Deep — the great fortress in a valley of the White Mountains, the stronghold of the Hornburg. Saruman had sent a massive army of Uruk-hai, supplemented by wild men of Dunland (Men who held ancient grievances against Rohan), to destroy the Rohirrim. Estimates in the text suggest ten thousand Uruk-hai against approximately a thousand defenders — Rohirrim warriors who had gotten to the Hornburg, peasant refugees sheltering in the cave-system called the Glittering Caves behind the fortress, and Gimli.

The Battle of Helm's Deep is one of Tolkien's finest battle sequences — several hours of fighting, beginning at nightfall and running through the night, the walls being held by increasingly desperate defenders, the Deeping Wall blown open by Saruman's explosive device (which anticipates gunpowder but is specified as a fire-based substance, the same kind used by the Orcs at the siege of Gondor). Aragorn and Gimli fought on the causeway; Legolas counted kills with his usual competitive precision (it is always Legolas counting, never Aragorn or Gimli proposing the game, and this small character note is consistent throughout the three books). The gate was nearly broken.

Then dawn came. Gandalf arrived with Erkenbrand and a force of Rohirrim who had been gathering in the west of the Mark. And from the east, from the forest that had not been there the night before — Huorns, the tree-creatures of Fangorn — a forest had walked through the night and now stood at the foot of the valley. The Uruk-hai fleeing the battle ran into the Huorns and vanished. The battle was won.

Tolkien does not show us what the Huorns did to the Uruk-hai in the forest. He was deliberate about this. The reader's imagination is sufficient, and the image of ten thousand soldiers disappearing into a living forest that then stands quietly is more powerful than any explicit violence would be.

## Part 4: Frodo, Sam, and Gollum — The Long Road to Mordor

We must return to the other thread, running simultaneously with all of the above: Frodo and Sam on the eastern bank of the Anduin, heading toward Mordor.

Frodo had the Ring. He had Sam. He had no map, no guide, no plan beyond "go east, find the Black Gate, get into Mordor somehow." He had the Phial of Galadriel. He had Sting and the mithril coat. He was a small Hobbit from a comfortable valley in the northwest corner of Middle-earth, and he was walking toward the worst place in the world.

They became lost in the Emyn Muil — the rocky hills above the east bank of the Anduin, a maze of broken stone and sliding shale that offered no obvious path forward. And then they found Gollum.

Gollum had been following them since they left Lothlórien — perhaps since they left the Fellowship's campsite. He had been drawn by the Ring, as the Ring drew him, as it always drew those who had borne it. Frodo caught him by surprise and Sting's blue glow told him what he held. He would not kill Gollum, though Sam urged it — Gandalf's words came back to him, words Gandalf had said in the Shire about pity: that Bilbo had shown pity to Gollum when he could have killed him, and that pity would determine many fates. Frodo made Gollum swear an oath to the Precious — to the Ring itself — to serve and guide them.

This was a deeply ambiguous decision. Sam was right that Gollum was dangerous and untrustworthy. Frodo was right that killing him would have been wrong, and that they needed a guide. Gollum knew the way into Mordor because he had been in and out of Mordor for decades, slipping through the Dead Marshes and the passes of the Ephel Dúath. No other guide existed. The choice to use Gollum was necessary and costly and inevitable.

The character of Gollum as rendered in The Two Towers is one of Tolkien's supreme achievements. He is genuinely two personalities — Sméagol, the remnant of the Hobbit he had been, who responded to Frodo's mercy with genuine gratitude and something like devotion, who wanted to be good and could not sustain it; and Gollum, the Ring-corrupted creature of endless self-pity and malice, who hated the Ring and loved it, who hated Frodo and Sam and wanted to serve them, who plotted against them while genuinely trying to guide them. Neither half was fake. Both were real. This is what the Ring did to its long-term bearers: it split them, set their original nature against the corruption, and wore both down in the wearing.

Sam never trusted Gollum. His instincts were correct. But Frodo saw in Gollum what Gandalf had suggested he might see: himself, in a long-enough run. What Frodo might become if the Ring had him long enough. The compassion he showed Gollum was not merely moral reflex; it was a recognition of shared fate.

The three went south and east, through the Dead Marshes — the ancient battlefield of Dagorlad, where the armies of the Last Alliance had fought Sauron's forces, and where the dead had been absorbed into the marsh. The faces of the dead could be seen under the water, preserved in the bog: Elves and Men of the Last Alliance, lying in their ancient armour, their faces lit by ghost-lights that sometimes seemed to move. The temptation to follow the lights was real — the lights led into the water. Frodo, looking at the faces of the dead, almost fell in. Gollum dragged him back.

They came to the Black Gate — the Morannon, the main entrance to Mordor. It was watched and warded, vast iron gates set in a wall across the pass between the Ash Mountains and the Ephel Dúath. An army of Men was marching through it as they watched, headed into Mordor from the south. There was no way through the Black Gate.

Gollum told them of another way. A secret path, the path he had used himself to enter and leave Mordor: through the mountains to the south, through a pass called Cirith Ungol — the Pass of the Spider — and down into Mordor from the west. The name should have warned them. The Elves of Lothlórien had warned them about Gollum and specifically about Cirith Ungol. But there was no other way, and they went south.

## Part 5: Faramir and the Men of Gondor

In Ithilien — the region of Gondor east of the Anduin, by this time occupied by Sauron's forces but patrolled by Gondorian rangers who emerged from hidden bases to ambush supply lines — Frodo and Sam and Gollum were captured by Faramir, Captain of Gondor and brother of Boromir.

Faramir is the character most carefully constructed by Tolkien to show what Men at their best can be. He was the younger son of Denethor, Steward of Gondor, and he stood in the shadow of Boromir — who was the stronger, the more apparently heroic, the more obviously admirable in his father's eyes. Boromir had gone north to the Council of Elrond because Denethor had wanted him to, and Denethor had wanted the eldest and most capable son to secure the Ring for Gondor. Faramir had wanted to go. He had had the dream that sent them to Rivendell. But he was not sent.

He captured Frodo and Sam (Gollum he missed; Gollum was never truly capturable unless one had the Ring), brought them to the cave-refuge of Henneth Annûn — the Window of the Sunset, a beautiful hidden waterfall-cave — and questioned them. He was intelligent and perceptive and he pieced together quickly that they were connected to his brother's mission. And then Frodo made an admission: that there had been a Ring of Power at the Council of Elrond, and that it was present in Ithilien now.

Faramir held the Ring's bearer in his power. He knew the Ring was there. He was under enormous pressure from his father's ambitions and his city's need. The Ring would have been — in any reasonable military calculus — a weapon worth seizing.

He let them go.

He did not touch the Ring. He saw what it would cost. He told Frodo, when Frodo expressed surprise at this: "I would not take this thing, if it lay by the highway. Not were Minas Tirith falling in ruin and I alone could save her."

This is the statement that distinguishes Faramir from his brother. Boromir loved Gondor too much to resist the Ring's promise of power to save it. Faramir loved Gondor enough to refuse the Ring because he understood that Gondor saved by the Ring would not be Gondor saved. The contrast is deliberate, and Tolkien knew exactly what he was doing. He also knew that Faramir would face his father's contempt for this decision — Denethor regarded it as weakness. It was the opposite.

Faramir warned them about Cirith Ungol — warned them directly, with specific knowledge. He knew of Shelob. He knew of what waited in that pass. But there was no other way, and he let them go.

## Part 6: Shelob — The Last Child of Ungoliant

The climb to Cirith Ungol took them through Minas Morgul first — the Tower of the Moon, now the Tower of Sorcery, the lair of the Witch-king. They watched from a ridge as a vast army poured out of its gates: the beginning of the great assault on Gondor, led by the Witch-king himself. The Nazgûl's black mount flew overhead and Frodo felt the Ring pulling toward it with agonising force. He put his hand on the Phial of Galadriel and breathed.

They climbed. Gollum led them up stairs carved in the mountain — the Stairs of Cirith Ungol — and into the tunnels of Shelob. Shelob was ancient beyond reckoning: Tolkien describes her as the last child of Ungoliant, the great spider-spirit of the First Age. Her connection to Ungoliant is not genetic in the simple sense — Ungoliant was not a material creature that could breed in the ordinary way. Shelob was a being of immense age and appetite, dwelling in the high passes of the Ephel Dúath in a complex of tunnels and webs, fed by whatever Gollum brought her: Orcs, lost travellers, animals.

Gollum had betrayed them. This was the plan all along — this was Gollum's design from the moment he agreed to lead them to Cirith Ungol. He would lead them to Shelob. Shelob would kill them. Then Gollum would get the Ring from whatever remained. He had convinced himself this was acceptable — that Shelob killing them was not killing them himself, that the oath to the Precious was technically maintained if he did not do the actual killing, that the whole scheme was perfectly rational.

In Shelob's tunnels, Gollum disappeared. Frodo and Sam were alone in absolute darkness, in a passage blocked ahead by a web of threads and smelling of decay and venom. Frodo took out the Phial of Galadriel and the light blazed up — the light of Eärendil's star, carried through ages of history from the Silmaril of Beren and Lúthien to this moment, in a spider's tunnel in the mountains of Mordor. The webs shrank from it. They passed.

But Shelob came.

She came from behind, where she had been moving silently in the darkness behind the light. She stung Frodo. The sting of Shelob was an anaesthetic venom — it did not kill immediately but rendered the victim insensible, ready to be wrapped and stored and eaten at leisure. Frodo collapsed.

Sam turned back. He had been in front, had been driven ahead by something that he later realised was Gollum's manipulation — Gollum had somehow directed him forward while leaving Frodo behind. He found Frodo lying on the ground and Shelob advancing to wrap him.

Sam did something extraordinary. He attacked Shelob. Sam Gamgee — gardener, loyal servant, entirely untrained in the manner of heroes — attacked a creature that had been old when the First Age began, that no single being had ever defeated, that had fed on things far larger and more dangerous than one small Hobbit with a sword. He attacked her with Sting and drove her back. She came for him. He threw himself under her as she lunged and drove Sting upward into her belly, and when she crushed down on him in her pain, she drove the blade deeper into herself. She screamed and retreated. She was not killed — she was too old and too vast for a single sword-wound to kill — but she was driven back, and Sam stood in the tunnel entrance facing her, holding the Phial of Galadriel above his head, and she retreated into her dark.

Then Sam turned back to Frodo. He checked him. He could not find a heartbeat. He could not feel breath. He concluded, because he did not know about Shelob's anaesthetic venom, that Frodo was dead.

Sam's grief in this moment — sitting in the tunnel entrance with Frodo's body, talking to him, unable to do anything useful — is one of the most genuinely heartbreaking passages in the whole of The Lord of the Rings. He was alone. He thought his master was dead. He held the Ring, which Frodo had pressed into his hands in his last moment of consciousness. He had to decide what to do.

He decided to go on. He would take the Ring. He would finish the quest, because the quest had to be finished. He put the Ring on and became invisible and went up toward Cirith Ungol.

He almost went too far. The Ring on his finger was already magnifying him, showing him a vision of what he could do — Sam as a great warrior, Sam as a great gardener-king, Sam remaking the whole of Mordor into a garden of extraordinary beauty. He caught himself. The vision was beautiful and wrong. He was Sam Gamgee, not a great warrior. He was going to find his master.

He heard Orc voices and took the Ring off. The Orcs were discussing Frodo — they had found him, recognised the venom, and identified him as a prisoner, not a corpse. Shelob had done this before. She liked her food fresh. The Orcs were taking him to Cirith Ungol to hold him for questioning. Frodo was alive.

Sam ran after the Orcs and was separated from them at the gate of the tower of Cirith Ungol, which closed between him and them. He was on the wrong side of the gate. Frodo was inside, imprisoned.

## Part 7: Gondor Besieged — Pippin and Faramir in Minas Tirith

While all of this was happening east of the Anduin, the western war was accelerating. Gandalf had arrived in Minas Tirith with Pippin. Pippin entered the service of the Steward Denethor — son of his folly in looking into the palantír, which had announced the presence of a Hobbit to Sauron and which Pippin offered as a kind of symbolic compensation — and became a Guard of the Citadel, which was at once absurd (a small Hobbit in Gondorian armour several sizes too large) and genuinely moving (the oath of service, taken seriously, binding).

Denethor was one of the most psychologically complex characters in The Lord of the Rings. He was brilliant, proud, politically capable, genuinely devoted to Gondor, and catastrophically brittle in the face of despair. He had been using the palantír of Minas Tirith — one of the Seeing Stones — to watch the movements of the enemy, and what he had seen had convinced him that Gondor was doomed. He had seen the fleets of the enemy, the strength of Mordor, the vastness of the forces arrayed. He had not seen — because Sauron controlled what could be seen in the Stone — any hope.

He also knew that Faramir had allowed the Ring-bearer to go on to Mordor rather than seizing the Ring for Gondor. He regarded this as an unforgivable dereliction. His younger son had had the weapon that could save Gondor and had let it walk away. The fact that Faramir was correct, that using the Ring would have been catastrophic, that Denethor's own vision of how to use it was probably impossible — none of this mattered to Denethor's pride and grief.

The forces of Mordor and the Witch-king arrived before the walls of Minas Tirith. Faramir, who had led the defence of the Pelennor Field in front of the city — the great open plain between the city and the river Anduin — was struck by a Nazgûl's dart and brought back to the city wounded and feverish. Denethor, believing Faramir dying, collapsed entirely into despair. He ordered Faramir's body carried to the House of the Stewards on Rath Dínen — the Silent Street — and a pyre built for them both. He intended to burn himself and Faramir alive.

This is Tolkien's darkest portrait of pride destroying wisdom: Denethor had every practical skill a leader needed, and it was his pride — the belief that defeat was impossible to bear, that he would rather die than accept help from a wandering Ranger who claimed to be Isildur's heir, that the Stewards of Gondor were the final arbiters of Gondor's fate — that killed him. He went mad in the specific way that the very proud go mad when the world does not cooperate with their vision of it.

Pippin fetched Gandalf. Gandalf interrupted the pyre, confronted Denethor, and saved Faramir. Denethor, robbed of Faramir, leaped onto his own pyre with the palantír clutched to his chest and burned. He died seeing in the Stone what Sauron had always shown him: the image of a great black fleet coming up the Anduin, which Denethor interpreted as the fleet of the enemy.

He was wrong. The ships were Aragorn's.

## Part 8: The Paths of the Dead — Aragorn's Dark Road

After the fall of Isengard and Théoden's return to Edoras, Aragorn was brought a message by a company of Dúnedain — the Rangers of the North, his own people — and by the sons of Elrond: Elladan and Elrohir, the twin sons of Elrond who periodically rode with the Rangers in the wars against the servants of Sauron. The message was from Elrond and from Galadriel, carried by a palantír communication that Aragorn had claimed the right to use: the time had come for Isildur's heir to use the Paths of the Dead, to summon the Dead Men of Dunharrow.

The Dead Men of Dunharrow were the remnant — the oath-bound remnant — of the Men of the Mountains, a people who had lived in the White Mountains since before Gondor's founding. In the Second Age, they had sworn allegiance to Isildur against Sauron. When the time came to fulfil the oath, they had broken it — they had submitted to Sauron instead, either from fear or from corrupted allegiance. Isildur cursed them: they would not rest until they had fulfilled their oath.

They had haunted the Paths of the Dead — the tunnel through the White Mountains, the closed door below the Dwimorberg, the Haunted Mountain — for three thousand years. No living man could go in. Any who tried came out mad or did not come out at all.

Aragorn went in. He went with Gimli and Legolas and the Dúnedain of his company, and he called the dead to him at the Stone of Erech — a great black sphere set on a hill, brought from Númenor, the stone on which the oath had been sworn. The Dead Men came. They followed him south in silence — a silence that those who saw the army described as absolute, as the army that was no army, the grey shadows of men with grey weapons, moving without sound.

Aragorn brought them to the port of Pelargir on the Anduin, where the fleet of the Corsairs of Umbar — sworn enemies of Gondor, the descendants of Castamir's faction from the Kin-strife nearly fifteen hundred years ago — was assembled and waiting to sail north to join the attack on Minas Tirith. The Dead Men destroyed or drove off the Corsairs, took their ships, and Aragorn declared the oath fulfilled. The Dead Men were released. They passed from the world.

Aragorn sailed north up the Anduin with the captured fleet, crewed by the men of Lebennin and Lamedon who had been freed from the Corsairs. He arrived at the Pelennor Fields during the Battle.

## Part 9: The Ride of the Rohirrim — Eowyn and the Witch-king

Théoden had ridden to Minas Tirith's aid with the full muster of Rohan — six thousand riders, the largest army the Rohirrim had fielded in living memory. His niece Éowyn had come with them, disguised as a Rider named Dernhelm, carrying Merry hidden in her gear. Merry had asked to come and been refused — it was no place for Hobbits, he had been told, which was arguably correct and also wrong in a way that the outcome would demonstrate.

Éowyn's story is one of the most important in The Return of the King and also one of the most often misread. She is not simply a woman who wants to fight — she is a woman who has watched men ride to glorious deaths while she was required to remain home and manage the household, a woman who has felt the specific shame of being told that the thing you do well and love is not appropriate for you, a woman who has come to regard death in battle as preferable to the diminishment of being left behind again. When she tells Aragorn that she fears only a cage, she means it with the full weight of someone for whom the cage is not metaphorical.

Her love for Aragorn — which Tolkien includes as a real thing, genuine feeling rather than mere plot device — is also not simply a romantic subplot. It is a desire for a life that includes greatness, for a connection to something worth fighting for. Aragorn gently refuses her because he has Arwen. Faramir, who eventually becomes her husband, sees in her something that Aragorn could not give: genuine peer recognition, the acknowledgement that she is great in herself, not as someone who reflects another's greatness.

The Rohirrim arrived on the Pelennor Fields at dawn. The great horns of Rohan — the Horns of the Mark — sounded. Théoden led the charge, and the charge broke into the Orc-host besieging the city and the great armies that Mordor had sent. For a time, the battle was winning.

Then the Witch-king came. He flew on his great fell beast — a winged creature of ancient darkness — and Théoden's horse Snowmane reared in terror and fell on the king, crushing him. Théoden lay dying. And the Witch-king descended.

The Witch-king was the chief of the Nazgûl, the most powerful Ringwraith, and over him lay the prophecy that Glorfindel had made centuries before, and that the Mouth of Sauron had mocked, and that the Witch-king himself had laughed at: no living man would slay him. He believed himself invincible. He had reason to.

Éowyn stepped forward. She had revealed herself when Théoden fell — she could not hide any longer, not with the king dying. "I am no man," she said, and took off her helm. It was not a clever ruse; it was the literal truth. The prophecy said no man. Éowyn was a woman.

But the prophecy was also fulfilled in a less obvious way. Merry, from behind, drove his Westernesse blade — one of the ancient swords from the Barrow of the North, enchanted against the servants of the Witch-king when it was made in the long wars of Angmar — into the back of the Witch-king's leg at the knee. The blade dissolved as it struck, fulfilling its purpose, but the purpose was accomplished: the black magic sustaining the Witch-king's physical form was disrupted. And into the disruption, Éowyn drove her own sword through the crown between the mantle and the helm.

The Witch-king was destroyed. The Lord of the Nazgûl, who had brought down the kingdom of Arnor, who had broken Gondor's line of kings, who had endured for three thousand years — was undone by a Hobbit's enchanted dagger and a woman's refusal to step aside. Both conditions of the prophecy were required. Neither alone would have been enough.

Éowyn and Merry were both struck down by the Black Breath — the emanation of the Witch-king's presence, a kind of despair made physical, a weapon of the soul more than the body. Aragorn healed them both with athelas — kingsfoil, a weed in the fields of Gondor that had healing properties activated by a king's touch, properties that the ordinary healers of the Houses of Healing in Minas Tirith had long since forgotten. The athelas dissolved the Black Breath. Faramir was also healed. And in the healing, Faramir and Éowyn met, and their love — quiet and lasting, very different from the great romantic loves of the Elder Days but not lesser for that — began.

---

## Part 10: Sam Rescues Frodo — The Tower of Cirith Ungol

While the Battle of the Pelennor Fields was being fought, Sam was outside the gate of Cirith Ungol with the Ring in his pocket and Frodo inside somewhere. He needed to get in.

He put on the Ring and became invisible and walked through the outer gate — which opened because the Ring's power suppressed the gate's wards. Inside the tower, he found Orcs fighting each other. The garrison of Cirith Ungol had been divided between two Orc clans — Shagrat's large Orcs from Mordor proper, and Gorbag's smaller Orcs from Minas Morgul — and the presence of Frodo's mithril coat (which the Orcs had stripped from him and which each clan wanted for themselves) had set off a killing dispute. They had largely destroyed each other.

Sam fought his way up the tower — using Sting, which he had taken when he thought Frodo was dead, and which glowed blue with Orc-nearness like a beacon. He found Frodo in a top chamber, naked and stripped and bound with cords, but alive. He cut him free. He gave Frodo back the Ring. He gave Frodo back his clothing. He found cloaks.

One detail of this reunion: Sam had kept the Ring on his person for a relatively short time — perhaps a day — but he had felt its weight. The visions it had shown him were real, and the feeling of it had been different from what he expected. He had not felt grandiose; he had felt small, as though the Ring was looking through him, as though he was being assessed and found insufficient for its purposes. He gave it back to Frodo with genuine relief.

They escaped from Cirith Ungol. They were now in Mordor.

The land of Mordor — the actual interior of Mordor, not the passes — was a place of ash and slag and darkness, lit by the fires of Orodruin in the distance and by the general pall of smoke from Sauron's furnaces. The earth was poisoned: nothing grew except thorns. The air smelled of sulphur. The roads were built for the feet of Sauron's armies, not for Hobbits. It was not a landscape that was supposed to support living beings crossing it; it was designed to make any passage an ordeal.

Frodo and Sam crossed it anyway. The journey from Cirith Ungol to Orodruin — fifteen or twenty miles as the crow flies, but longer on foot through the broken terrain — is the most sustained test of endurance in the entire trilogy. Frodo was carrying the Ring. The Ring, which grew heavier as it drew closer to where it had been made, which pressed down on its bearer with a weight that was not physical but was felt more severely than any physical weight, which whispered constantly. Frodo could not eat. He could barely drink. He moved by will alone. Sam carried him when he could not walk. Sam shared his water when Frodo's water ran out. Sam never stopped.

The great shadow that Sauron's attention cast across the land — the Eye, sweeping back and forth, searching, hunting the Ring — was terrifyingly present. But Sauron was not looking inward. He was looking outward, at the armies assaulting his territory, at the western front where Gandalf and Aragorn were fighting. The Ring was supposed to be in the west somewhere — Sauron had seen it in Pippin's eyes through the palantír, had deduced it was near Isengard or Rohan, had oriented his forces accordingly.

The Ring was not in the west. The Ring was walking toward Mount Doom through the ashy plains of Gorgoroth.

---

## Part 11: The Black Gate Opens — The Last Debate

After the Battle of the Pelennor Fields, there was a period of days during which the armies regrouped, the wounded were tended, and the commanders debated what to do next. The debate — called in the text "The Last Debate" — was between Gandalf's strategy and the immediate temptation to simply consolidate their victory.

Gandalf's argument was this: Sauron had been struck a heavy blow at the Pelennor Fields — the Witch-king was destroyed, his fleet was taken, the field was his — but Sauron still had Barad-dûr, still had the greater part of his armies, still had the nine Nazgûl (minus the Witch-king, temporarily), and still had a reserve of forces that could overwhelm any army the West could put in the field. The only reason Sauron had not yet committed those forces was that he did not know about the Ring-bearer in Mordor; he was watching the western front. Every day that Sauron was watching the western front was another day that Frodo and Sam had to cross Gorgoroth and reach Orodruin.

The strategy was the most tactically unsound in military history and the most strategically essential: march the combined armies of Rohan and Gondor to the Black Gate of Mordor and challenge Sauron to battle there — not to win (they could not win; they were outnumbered beyond any hope of military victory) but to draw Sauron's full attention to the western front and away from the interior of Mordor, buying time.

They were going to march to the Black Gate knowing they could not prevail. They were going to do it anyway.

Aragorn led. Gandalf was with them. Merry and Pippin and the surviving members of the Fellowship who were present. Legolas and Gimli. Éomer. The combined armies: perhaps seven thousand men and Elves, against the armies of Mordor.

They marched.

At the Black Gate, the battle was joined. The armies of Mordor poured out — Eagles came, Gwaihir and his people, fighting the Nazgûl who flew overhead — and the armies of the West were surrounded, outnumbered, not winning.

And then the Ring was destroyed.

---

## Part 12: The Crack of Doom — Gollum's Last Act

On the slopes of Orodruin, Frodo had climbed. He had climbed alone — Sam could not carry him anymore, could not move him by force, could only follow. The Ring was crushing him; the weight of it and the proximity of its making-place were compounding each other. He moved by something beyond ordinary will, something the texts leave unnamed but that Tolkien's theology would call grace: a sustaining from outside his own resources.

At the Crack of Doom — the fissure in the cone of Orodruin that led directly to the mountain's fires — Frodo stood.

He did not cast the Ring in.

He put it on his finger. He claimed it for himself. "The Ring is mine," he said, and vanished.

This is one of the most important moments in all of Tolkien. Frodo — the most suitable Ring-bearer who had ever lived, Gandalf's choice, the one whose mercy and gentleness and love had sustained him across all of Mordor — could not do it at the last moment. The Ring was too strong. He had carried it for months, and the corruption had accumulated, and at the moment when everything depended on one action, he failed.

And the failure was saved by a failure. Gollum, who had been following them through Mordor — Gollum, who had attacked Frodo on the slopes of Orodruin and been driven back by Sam — Gollum attacked again. He bit off Frodo's finger — the ring-finger — and recovered the Ring. He had the Precious. He had what he had wanted for over five hundred years. He held it up, dancing with joy on the edge of the Crack of Doom.

He fell. Whether he lost his footing or leaped or the Ring itself guided him over the edge — Tolkien's text is ambiguous, and the ambiguity is deliberate. Gollum and the Ring fell together into the fire of Orodruin, and the Ring was destroyed.

The destruction of the Ring was the end of Sauron. His power, his capacity to exist in any coherent form, was bound up in the Ring — which is why he had made it, which is also why it was the one thing that could not be used against him except by destroying it. When the Ring went into the fire, Sauron's physical form was destroyed again, far more completely than the Last Alliance had managed. His spirit was torn from his body. Without the Ring, he could not reconstitute. He dispersed, thinned, became a shadow of a shadow, diminished to nothing — or nothing that could affect the world again. The Nazgûl, whose existence depended on the Ring, were destroyed. The armies of Mordor at the Black Gate fell into disorder and broke.

The War of the Ring was over.

The eucatastrophe — Tolkien's concept of the sudden joyous turn in the darkest moment, which he considered the hallmark of the true fairy-story — came from the most unlikely source imaginable. Not a great warrior. Not a wizard. Not a king. It came from a miserable, wretched creature who had been destroyed by the Ring over five hundred years, who bit off a Hobbit's finger to get it back, who fell into a volcano in his joy. The saviour of Middle-earth was Gollum, who did not mean to save anything.

And it came from Frodo's mercy, decades before — Bilbo's mercy first, then Frodo's choice not to kill Gollum in the Emyn Muil. Without Gollum alive and present and desperately wanting the Ring, the quest would have ended at the Crack of Doom with Frodo's claiming. The mercy that had seemed like weakness, or at best uncertain wisdom, turned out to be the pivot of the entire history.

Gandalf had said in the Shire, years before: "My heart tells me that Gollum has some part to play yet, for good or ill, before the end; and when that comes, the pity of Bilbo may rule the fate of many — yours not least." He was understating the case considerably.

---

## Part 13: The Eagles and the Rescue

Sam and Frodo were on the slopes of Orodruin, with lava flows advancing and the mountain in eruption — the destruction of the Ring had somehow translated directly into Orodruin's activity, as if the mountain was the extension of Sauron's will and was responding to his destruction. They could not get down. The fires were closing around them.

They sat on a small rock above the lava. Sam held Frodo's hand. They watched the mountain burn. Frodo, for the first time in many months, was without the Ring — the Ring was gone, and the relief of it was something he could not describe, something that went all the way through him. His hand ached where the finger was gone. He did not care.

They talked about the Shire. They would not go home, Sam thought. And Frodo told him they would — not really believing it, but saying it because Sam needed to hear it. It was the kind of small courage that runs through Sam's character from beginning to end: the courage of maintaining hope as a performance when you don't feel it, because the person beside you needs hope.

Then Gwaihir came. The Lord of Eagles came with two companions, sent by Gandalf — who had known that Eagles could not carry the Ring-bearer into Mordor (that would have been obvious, a great Eagle swooping toward Mount Doom, the Eye of Sauron would have seen it immediately and responded with the full force of Mordor's aerial forces) but who could hope that Eagles might come after, when the task was done. They came. They lifted Frodo and Sam from the rock above the lava and carried them west.

---

## Part 14: The Return and the Coronation — Aragorn King

There was a time of healing and reunion at the Field of Cormallen in Ithilien, where the armies had made camp after the victory. Frodo and Sam were brought there, and there was a great celebration — a celebration that Tolkien describes with characteristic gentleness, noting that Frodo and Sam did not quite understand what was being celebrated or why everyone was weeping and laughing simultaneously. They had been too deep inside the experience to see it from outside. It required a minstrel to sing about it before they could hear it as a story rather than simply as what had happened.

The coronation of Aragorn took place in Minas Tirith, in the ruins and the beauty that was already being repaired. He was crowned with the Crown of Gondor — retrieved from the tombs of the kings — and he took his throne name: Elessar, the Elfstone, as had been foreseen. His full title was Aragorn II Elessar Telcontar, King of the Reunited Kingdom of Arnor and Gondor, the Elessar of the Elf-stone, Lord of the Dúnedain.

Then Arwen came. She came over the mountains from Rivendell with Elrond and Galadriel and Celeborn and with the Lords of Lothlórien and Rivendell — with a great train of Elves making what many of them understood was one of their last formal entries into the world of Men. She came over the pass and down into Minas Tirith, and Aragorn went to meet her at the gate, and she gave up the Evenstar — the jewel of the Dúnedain, the heirloom of Elrond's line, made of the same elf-light as the star Eärendil had worn — and Aragorn placed it on her brow, and they were married.

Arwen's choice was Lúthien's choice made again — mortality for love, giving up the immortal life for the finite one. She could have sailed west with her father and lived in Valinor. She chose Aragorn and Middle-earth and the mortal life. Elrond's parting from his daughter — the father who had lived thirty centuries finally losing the daughter he had raised through all of them to a choice he could not overturn — is told very briefly in the text, which is exactly right. Some things are too large for extensive description.

The White Tree of Gondor bloomed again. A seedling was found on the slopes of Mindolluin, above the city — a seedling of the ancient line, descended from the trees of Valinor, growing wild on the mountainside as if waiting for the king's return. Aragorn planted it in the court before the Citadel. It flowered.

---

## Part 15: The Long Farewell — Lothlórien Diminished

As the great figures departed from Minas Tirith, the long process of farewell that characterises the end of The Return of the King began. These farewells are not triumphant — they carry the weight of everything that was ending alongside the victory.

Galadriel and Celeborn rode north with their people, and as they rode they passed through the now-empty paths of Lórien — empty because the Elves of Lórien had mostly departed or were departing, sailing west. Nenya had failed when the Ring was destroyed: the Three Rings of the Elves had derived their power from the One Ring's existence — not from its control over them, but from the principle of ring-craft that the One Ring had established. When the One Ring was destroyed, the Three lost their power. Lothlórien began to fade the moment the Ring went into the fire.

The mallorn-trees would eventually lose their golden leaves permanently. The timeless quality of the Wood would drain away. What was preserved by the Ring's power could not be preserved without it. This is the cost of victory: the Three Rings bought Middle-earth three thousand years of fading beauty, and now that time was over. Galadriel went to the Grey Havens and sailed. Lothlórien was left to the trees and the birds and the slow return of the world's ordinaries.

Celeborn remained for a time in Rivendell — Elrond was also leaving. Rivendell, like Lothlórien, lost the power that Vilya had sustained. The timelessness of the Last Homely House would end. Elrond sailed in the same year as Galadriel, in the company of Gandalf and Bilbo and Frodo, on the last great ship.

---

## Part 16: The Scouring of the Shire — Evil Closer to Home

But before all of that, there was one more thing: the Scouring of the Shire.

The four Hobbits returned home after the coronation and the long journey back, and they found the Shire changed. Sharkey had taken it — Saruman, who had slipped away from Isengard in Treebeard's custody and gone north (Treebeard had let him go, because Ents did not keep prisoners forever, and because he underestimated what Saruman would do with his freedom). Saruman had taken the Shire as a last act of spite: he could not hurt Gandalf or Aragorn directly anymore, but he could ruin the thing that the Hobbits had fought for, the home they had wanted to come back to.

Under the name Sharkey — a name his Orc-servants had given him, meaning "old man" in their tongue — he had installed himself in Bag End (he had wanted Bag End for a long time, and had been trying to acquire it through Lotho Sackville-Baggins, a corrupt Hobbit whose family had been Bilbo's bane for decades). He had turned the Shire into a small-scale tyranny: Ruffians from the south, Men who did Saruman's bidding, collecting "gatherings" (extorted goods) and enforcing petty rules and burning what they liked and generally making the Shire miserable.

Tolkien was quite deliberate about including this episode, though many readers and all adaptors have omitted it as anticlimactic. It is not anticlimactic. It is the point. The four Hobbits who came home were not the same Hobbits who had left — they had faced the Nazgûl and the Balrog's aftermath and Shelob and the plains of Mordor and the breaking of the Fellowship, and they were not going to be pushed around by a few Ruffians and a diminished ex-Wizard. They organized the resistance of the Shire, called the Hobbits to arms, won the Battle of Bywater (twenty-odd Ruffians versus a few hundred Hobbit militia — no contest), and confronted Saruman.

Saruman's end was pathetic and fitting. He had been reduced to this: running a petty tyranny in a small country, acting out his spite in the pettiest possible way. When Frodo gave him the chance to leave, he attacked Frodo with a knife. The mithril coat stopped it. Gríma Wormtongue, who had been with Saruman all this time and who had endured decades of Saruman's contempt and cruelty, cut Saruman's throat — cut it from behind, in a final act of revenge that was also itself a sign of how corrupted Gríma had become, that his only remaining recourse was this. Saruman's spirit rose like smoke and was blown away by a wind from the West. He was unmade — not destroyed the way Sauron was destroyed, but dispersed, reduced to nothing that could matter to the world. Whatever had been Curunír the Maia was gone.

Gríma was shot by three Hobbit arrows before he had gone ten steps. He lay down by the gate and did not rise.

Bag End was cleaned. The Shire was cleaned. Saruman had deliberately imported a large number of industrializing machines — his fascination with industry extended to small-scale despoliation of the Shire's countryside — and trees had been cut, mill-wheels replaced with larger, uglier ones, ugly buildings erected. Sam planted Lórien-seeds that Galadriel had given him, and within a year the Shire was more beautiful than it had been before, with trees growing in a single season that should have taken decades.

But there was one thing that could not be cleaned or replanted: Frodo's wound. His ring-finger where Gollum had bitten it off was not all that hurt him. The stab from the Barrow-wight's knife and the Morgul-blade wound and the long carrying of the Ring had left marks that Sam's gardening could not fix and Aragorn's athelas could not entirely heal. He lived in the Shire for about two years after the return — writing, completing Bilbo's book which he called The Red Book of Westmarch, living quietly, but not well.

## Part 17: The Last Ship — The Grey Havens

On September 22, 3021 of the Third Age — Frodo's fiftieth birthday and Bilbo's hundred-and-thirty-first, the same date as Bilbo's great party twenty years before — a party of travellers left the Shire going west. Frodo was with them. Sam rode with him. Merry and Pippin came to see him off, and as they rode they met another party coming from the east: Gandalf, and Galadriel, and Elrond, and Bilbo, who was very old now and very small and very tired.

At the Grey Havens, Círdan was waiting with a white ship. The ship was ready. The tide was right. The wind was in the west.

Frodo had been given the gift that Arwen gave up her place for — the gift that was unique to him, a Ring-bearer who had been marked by what he had carried. He was permitted to take the straight road to Valinor, permitted to heal there, permitted to go to the Undying Lands that no mortal was ordinarily allowed to enter. Bilbo was also going, at a hundred and thirty-one, for his own long carrying of the Ring.

They walked to the ship. Frodo embraced Merry and Pippin. He spoke to Sam last. He told Sam that what he was doing was not giving up, not abandoning — he was going somewhere to heal where the healing was possible. He told Sam that Sam had so much to live for: Rosie, and their children (Sam had many children), and the Shire, and the beginning of the Fourth Age. He told Sam that not every wound can be healed in Middle-earth, and this was not Sam's failing or anyone's failing.

He went aboard. The ship moved. The wind rose. The ship went out into the evening, and Frodo smelled the air and found it was good — sweeter than anything he had smelled since before the Ring came to him, the smell of the sea and something beyond the sea, something that had no name but that he was moving toward.

Sam stood on the shore and watched the ship until the white light was gone over the horizon, and then he turned his horse and rode home to the Shire and to Rosie and to his children and to everything that was left.

And the Third Age ended.

## Part 18: What Came After — The Fourth Age

The Fourth Age began with Aragorn's coronation and the re-establishment of the Reunited Kingdom. A brief account of what is known:

Aragorn and Arwen ruled for over a hundred years. Aragorn died in the year 120 of the Fourth Age, having lived two hundred and ten years — the longest-lived of the Kings of Gondor, a sign of the purity of his Númenórean blood and perhaps of the blessing his extraordinary life had given him. Arwen went to Lórien after his death — to the empty, fading Lórien — and lay down on Cerin Amroth, where she and Aragorn had pledged themselves in the Third Age, and died. She had chosen mortality and she kept that choice to the end.

Sam returned to the Shire and to Rosie Cotton, who became Rosie Gamgee. They had thirteen children, the most of any Hobbit family in the records. Sam was Mayor of the Shire for seven terms. He was held in enormous esteem by the Shire and by the wider world, though the Shire did not fully understand why.

Merry and Pippin eventually left the Shire in old age to go to Rohan — to visit Éomer, who had become the great King of Rohan after Théoden's death, and who was himself now old. They did not return. They are buried in Minas Tirith in the House of the Kings, beside Aragorn. This was what they wanted.

Legolas built a ship in Ithilien — he had lived there after the War of the Ring, planting trees, returning the green to the region — and eventually sailed west. He took Gimli with him. This is remarkable. Gimli was a Dwarf. Dwarves did not sail to Valinor. But Galadriel had given Gimli the gift of seeing her clearly in his heart, and the love he bore Lothlórien and what it represented seems to have qualified him for the journey — or Legolas's friendship pulled him into a grace extended beyond what was normally possible. Christopher Tolkien notes that Tolkien was uncertain whether this was permitted, but it is what the texts say.

Sam, the last of the Fellowship in Middle-earth, eventually gave the Red Book to his daughter Elanor and then — in the last entry recorded, when he was very old — went to the Grey Havens and sailed. He had been a Ring-bearer, briefly. He had been permitted, at Frodo's departure, a place among those who had the right to take the straight road. He waited until his time was fully come and then went.

And that is where the record ends.

## Part 19: What the Third Age Meant — A Reckoning

The Third Age lasted three thousand and twenty-one years. What it meant, in the context of the whole mythology:

It was the age of diminishment. The great things of the Elder Days were present but fading: the last Elvish kingdoms held by the power of the Rings; the last Númenórean bloodline hidden in the wilderness; the last great forests; the last Ent-meetings; the last time Balrogs walked Middle-earth. The Third Age existed in the long shadow of the First and Second Ages, drawing on their legacy and slowly spending it.

It was also the age in which the ordinary became decisive. Not the great Elvish lords with their ancient powers, not the Númenórean kings with their heritage of Valinorean craft, not the Istari with their Maia-nature — but a Hobbit from the Shire, a gardener, two young Hobbits who went to war without any clear idea what they were doing and found themselves in the middle of everything. Tolkien's argument, running through the whole of The Lord of the Rings, is that the world is not saved by the great in their greatness but by the ordinary in their love and their stubbornness and their refusal to give up even when giving up would be entirely rational.

This is also a statement about heroism that differs from the heroism of the First Age. Tolkien admired the heroism of Fingolfin and Húrin — the terrible courage that faces destruction without flinching. But he found in the Third Age a different kind of heroism more congenial to his own experience and temperament: the heroism of continuing. Of putting one foot in front of the other. Of getting up again. Of carrying the Ring across Mordor not because you are extraordinary but because it needs carrying and there is no one else.

Sam carries Frodo. This is the image Tolkien returned to most often when pressed about what The Lord of the Rings was about. Not the Ring, not Sauron, not the battle — Sam carrying Frodo up the slope of Mount Doom.

## Part 20: The Complete Mythology — What It All Adds Up To

We have now told, across these seven articles, the complete chronological story of Middle-earth from before the world was made to the departure of the last ship from the Grey Havens. Let us stand back and consider what shape that story has.

It begins with music and ends with a gardener going home. Between those two points, the story moves through creation and catastrophe, through ages of beauty and ages of destruction, through the heroism of Elves who knew they were doomed and the heroism of Men who didn't know anything except that something needed doing. It moves through the theft of a Silmaril and the singing of a Maia's daughter and the fall of Gondolin and the voyage of Eärendil and the drowning of Númenor and the forging of the Ring and a Hobbit's birthday party and an unexpected journey and a very small person on a very large slope asking their sword to kill them quickly.

The recurring argument of the mythology is this: creation is good. The world was made in joy and the making was beautiful and the beauty is permanent — it cannot be destroyed, only deformed. Every act of Morgoth's corrupted what was beautiful but could not uncreate it. The Silmarils existed and were holy and contained the original light of the Trees and that light is still in the sky, in the earth, in the sea. The music was sung and is still being sung, because music is not an event that ends but a pattern that continues. Lúthien chose mortality and that choice echoes through three ages until Arwen stands on Cerin Amroth and makes the same choice, and the echo is itself an argument that love is permanent even when the lovers are not.

Evil diminishes. Morgoth went from the mightiest of the Ainur to a captive expelled through the Door of Night. Sauron went from the greatest of the Maiar to a formless shadow unable to trouble the world. The servants of evil — the Nazgûl, the Balrogs, the dragons, the corrupted Maiar — are reduced across the ages, destroyed or dispersed or rendered powerless. Darkness is not sustainable. It can win battles; it cannot win the war.

Good persists. Sometimes it persists in very small things — in a fruit saved from a dying tree, in a sword kept clean and ready, in a lineage maintained through wilderness and poverty, in a friendship between a Dwarf and an Elf. Sometimes it persists in the memory of the Elves, who remember everything that happened and who carry the history of the world in their songs. Sometimes it persists in an ordinary Hobbit's stubborn love for his master, which turns out to be what the whole history was building toward.

Tolkien wrote The Lord of the Rings during the Second World War. He was not writing about the war — he rejected that reading explicitly. But he was writing from within the experience of a world that had recently demonstrated how far evil could go and how great the cost of opposing it was. The mythology is not consolatory in the sense of pretending the cost is small. The cost is enormous. Beleriand sinks beneath the sea. Frodo goes to Valinor with a missing finger and a wound that the mortal world cannot heal. Gollum falls into the fire. Théoden dies on the Pelennor. Boromir dies on the hill above the Anduin.

But there is also Tom Cotton's daughter Rosie, and Sam's thirteen children, and the Shire returned to beauty with Lórien-trees in the hedgerows. There is Faramir and Éowyn finding each other in the Houses of Healing while the war continues outside. There is the White Tree blooming in the court of the Citadel. There are the Eagles.

The mythology says: this is what it costs. And this is what it was for. Both are true. The cost is real and the purpose is real and they do not cancel each other out.

## Epilogue: A Note on Tolkien Himself

J.R.R. Tolkien worked on the mythology of Middle-earth from 1914 until his death in 1973. That is nearly sixty years of continuous elaboration, revision, reconception, and deepening of a single imaginary world. He published The Hobbit in 1937, The Fellowship of the Ring and The Two Towers in 1954, and The Return of the King in 1955. The Silmarillion was published posthumously in 1977, edited by his son Christopher. The twelve-volume History of Middle-earth, published between 1983 and 1996, contains the drafts and revisions and essays and letters that show the work's extraordinary development over those sixty years.

He wrote the names Beren and Lúthien on his wife Edith's gravestone, and on his own.

He described the mythology in letters as his attempt to give England a mythology — to do for the British Isles what the Norse mythology did for Scandinavia and the Finnish Kalevala did for Finland. He succeeded at this, and at much more than this. He created a world that has been inhabited, in imagination, by hundreds of millions of readers, that has influenced virtually every work of fantasy published in the seventy years since The Lord of the Rings appeared, that has generated a scholarly tradition of its own.

He was also, by his own account, not simply constructing a mythology but discovering it. He described the writing as a process of finding out what was there, of the stories revealing themselves rather than being imposed. This is not an unusual description by creative artists of their process, but it is worth noting in Tolkien's case because the mythology has the feel of something discovered — its internal consistency, its sense of deep roots, the way in which small details in The Lord of the Rings turn out to connect to events in the First Age described in the Silmarillion, the sense of a world that exists beyond the edges of any text. That sense is not created. It accumulates.

The mythology is complete in its incompleteness. The questions it does not answer — the fate of Men after death, the final form of the Dagor Dagorath, what Aulë truly is, whether Ungoliant was a spirit or something older — are not failures of imagination but deliberate mysteries. Tolkien believed that a secondary world, a sub-creation, should participate in the texture of the primary world: mysterious in the right places, knowable in the right places, always suggesting more beyond the horizon.

There is always more beyond the horizon.
