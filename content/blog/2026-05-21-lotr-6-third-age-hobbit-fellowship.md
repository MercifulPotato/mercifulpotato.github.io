# The Long Watch and the Unexpected Journey: The Third Age from Isildur's Fall to the Breaking of the Fellowship

*This is Part Six of the comprehensive in-universe chronological history of Middle-earth. Part Five covered the entire Second Age — Númenor's gift and fall, the forging of the Rings of Power, the Last Alliance, and Isildur's fatal choice not to destroy the One Ring. The Third Age begins two years after Sauron's defeat, when Isildur is killed in the Gladden Fields and the Ring is lost. It ends three thousand and twenty-one years later when the Ring is destroyed in the fires of Orodruin. This article covers the full span of the Third Age through the end of the Fellowship's journey together — from Isildur's fall through Amon Hen.*

---

## Part 1: The Third Age Begins — A World Without a High King

The Third Age began without ceremony and without clarity. Sauron had been defeated — his physical form destroyed, his Ring lost, his Nazgûl temporarily leaderless and diminished. The Last Alliance had won. But the price of winning was enormous, and the victory was incomplete in ways that would not be obvious for centuries.

Gil-galad, the last High King of the Noldor in Middle-earth, was dead. There would never be another High King of the Noldor. Elrond assumed the lord-ship of the Noldorin remnant from his seat in Rivendell, but he did not take the kingship — he was counsellor and keeper, not king. The great Elvish kingdoms that had shaped the history of the Second Age were gone or diminished: Eregion destroyed, Lindon reduced to Círdan's harbour-community, Lothlórien existing in the southeast of Eriador under Galadriel and Celeborn's care, Rivendell a hidden valley in the Misty Mountains.

Elendil was dead. Anárion was dead. Isildur was dead — killed in the Gladden Fields two years into the new age. The kingdom of Arnor in the north was without its founder and king, its capital Annúminas on the shores of Lake Evendim already beginning the long process of diminishment. The kingdom of Gondor in the south was in the hands of Anárion's son Meneldil, who was competent but who presided over a kingdom already depleted by the seven-year siege.

The Third Age opened, then, as a world in which the great powers of the Elder Days had been removed or weakened, in which the enemy was seemingly gone, and in which the peoples of Middle-earth — Elves, Men, and others — faced the long task of rebuilding and enduring without the structures that had previously sustained them. It was, in many ways, the loneliest age.

The Dúnedain — the Númenórean-descended Men of Arnor and Gondor — were the dominant human culture of the northwestern world. They were still extraordinary by ordinary human standards: tall, long-lived (though not as long-lived as the pure Númenóreans had been), possessed of the arts and knowledge of Númenor. But the pure blood was already diluting. Each generation was slightly less than the last. The Kings of Gondor in the first centuries of the Third Age still lived several hundred years; by the middle of the Age, the line of kings would be broken entirely, and the people of Gondor would live and die as ordinary Men.

This diminishment — slow, quiet, inexorable — is one of the defining themes of the Third Age. The world was running down. The great lights of the Elder Days were fading. The Three Rings of the Elves held beauty and wisdom against the tide, but they could not reverse it, only slow it. The world was becoming less, generation by generation, and only something decisive could change that course.

## Part 2: The Division of Arnor and the Coming of the Witch-king

The northern kingdom of Arnor lasted intact for only about three centuries after its founding. In the year 861 of the Third Age, King Eärendur died and left three sons, and the kingdom was divided among them into three smaller realms: Arthedain in the northwest, Rhudaur in the northeast, and Cardolan in the south. This was a catastrophic decision — not because division was inherently wrong, but because Arnor's strength depended on the unified authority of the Dúnedain, and divided, the three kingdoms were too small and too suspicious of each other to coordinate effectively.

Arthedain maintained the line of Isildur — the rightful line, descended from Arnor's founding — and maintained the Elvish friendship and the traditions of the Faithful. Rhudaur was quickly infiltrated by the Hillmen of the north, who had little loyalty to the Dúnedain tradition and who were susceptible to darker influences. Cardolan attempted to maintain independence but was caught between its two neighbours.

Into this situation, in the year 1300, something new appeared in the north: a realm called Angmar, founded in the cold lands east of the Misty Mountains beyond the northern reaches of Eriador. Its lord was never openly named — but he was the Witch-king, the chief of the Nazgûl, who had apparently been active and growing in power since the early Third Age.

The wars between Angmar and the northern Dúnedain constitute one of the longest and most grinding conflicts in Middle-earth's history. They lasted nearly eight hundred years, from approximately 1300 to 1975. Over that span, Rhudaur was conquered and turned into an Angmarian puppet. Cardolan was depopulated by plague — Angmar sent the Great Plague from the east in 1636, which killed enormous numbers across Eriador and as far south as Gondor. The Barrow-downs, south of Bree, became haunted by wights — spirits of darkness sent by the Witch-king to inhabit the burial mounds of the Cardolanian dead.

These Barrow-wights are the same creatures that nearly kill Frodo and his companions in The Fellowship of the Ring. Tom Bombadil rescues them; Tom Bombadil can do this because he is genuinely ancient and genuinely beyond the Ring's influence — he is outside the story in a metaphysical sense that Tolkien was always somewhat mysterious about. He was perhaps a Maia who had chosen a different path from all others, or something older than Maia. He had been in Middle-earth since before the Elves came, and the darkness of the Barrow-wights is no threat to him the way it would be to any other being in Middle-earth. The wights represent a direct First Age–through–Third Age continuity of evil: the darkness Morgoth seeded in the earliest ages is still there, in the burial mounds of a people who have been dead for a thousand years, waiting to harm whoever comes too close.

Arthedain survived longest, maintained by the line of Isildur and by the friendship with Rivendell. But in 1974, Angmar overwhelmed Arthedain completely. The last king of Arthedain, Arvedui — whose name means "Last King" — fled to the north, to the Icebay of Forochel, where a remnant of an ancient people called the Lossoth (snow-people, ancestors of the Lappish-type cultures Tolkien drew on) took him in and sustained him through the winter. He died when a Lossoth ship that was attempting to carry him south was caught in a storm and sank. With him died the kingship of the northern Dúnedain.

The palantír of the north — the Seeing Stone that Arvedui had with him — was lost in the same shipwreck and lies at the bottom of the Icebay of Forochel, where it remains at the time of The Lord of the Rings. It is one of Tolkien's many details of things lost and unrecoverable.

But the line of Isildur did not end. Arvedui's son Aranarth survived, and his descendants survived, living in Rivendell under Elrond's care and guidance, serving as Rangers in the wilderness of Eriador — the guardians of a territory that had no king, protecting the scattered inhabitants from the dangers that the dwindled kingdoms could no longer resist. These are the Rangers of the North, the Dúnedain of the North, who appear in The Lord of the Rings as the mysterious wanderers of whom the people of Bree and the Shire know little and fear somewhat. Aragorn is the last and greatest of this line.

The Witch-king's kingdom of Angmar did not long outlast its victory over Arthedain. In 1975, Eärnur of Gondor led a fleet north — the southern kingdom still had the strength to respond, though it was itself diminished — and combined with the remainder of the northern Dúnedain and the Elves of Rivendell and Círdan's forces, he defeated the Witch-king at the Battle of Fornost. The Witch-king fled before the battle was fully decided — Glorfindel, who was present (the same Glorfindel who had died at Gondolin and been reembodied, and who in the Third Age was the most powerful Elvish warrior remaining in Middle-earth), prophesied that no living man would slay him. This prophecy was correct.

The Witch-king went south, eventually to Mordor, where he gathered the Nazgûl and took command of Minas Morgul. And Eärnur, the Gondorian prince, developed a burning personal hatred for the Witch-king that would eventually cost him his life.

## Part 3: The Troubles of Gondor — Division and Dragon-fire

While the north was being ground down by Angmar, Gondor in the south faced its own crises, though of different kinds.

The most significant structural problem in Gondor's Third Age history was the Kin-strife — a civil war that lasted from 1432 to 1447 and was caused by a succession dispute with racial underpinnings. King Valacar of Gondor had married a woman of Rhovanion, a people descended from the Northmen of the East who were not of pure Númenórean descent. Their son Eldacar, though personally excellent, was regarded by the purist faction in Gondor as insufficiently pure-blooded. A noble named Castamir seized the throne, drove Eldacar into exile, and inaugurated a reign that was brief and particularly cruel — Castamir executed Eldacar's son Ornendil, which made reconciliation impossible, and his naval policies depopulated Osgiliath.

Eldacar returned with an army of Northmen supporters and defeated Castamir at the Battle of the Crossings of Erui, killing him in single combat. But Castamir's sons fled south to the haven of Umbar, which became a permanent centre of Gondorian opposition — the Corsairs of Umbar, who trouble Gondor through the entire Third Age, are the descendants of Castamir's faction, nursing their grievance across generations. The Battle of the Pelennor Fields in The Return of the King is partly decided by Aragorn's arrival with the Dead Men of Dunharrow, who he then releases from their oath; but also by the fleet of captured Corsair ships that Aragorn sails up the Anduin, whose appearance on the river releases the army of Lebennin and Lamedon from their defensive positions and allows them to reinforce the Pelennor. The Corsairs of Umbar, born from the Kin-strife nearly fifteen hundred years earlier, are still shaping the battle.

The Great Plague of 1636 struck Gondor as well as the north. The White Tree died during the plague — it had a kind of spiritual connection to the line of the kings, and when the people were dying, the tree died with them. A seedling was preserved and planted, but the great tree's death was an omen.

The Wainriders — great confederations of Easterling peoples, pushed or driven by Sauron's influence from the far east — made war on Gondor repeatedly through the middle centuries of the Third Age, straining Gondor's resources and contributing to its slow decline. Gondor lost Rhûn. It lost Khand. The great eastern and southern territories that the Númenóreans had claimed in the early Second Age were gone by the middle of the Third.

The last King of Gondor was Eärnur, who had defeated the Witch-king at Fornost. In 2050, the Witch-king, now established in Minas Morgul, sent a second challenge to Eärnur — they had met once before without decisive result, and the Witch-king taunted Eärnur with this. The king's advisors urged him not to go. He went. He rode to Minas Morgul alone, and was never seen again. He had no heirs. The line of kings of Gondor ended.

From that point forward, Gondor was ruled by the Stewards — officers of the royal household who maintained the governance of the kingdom in the king's absence, always formally as regents waiting for the king's return, but in practice as rulers. The Steward's chair was placed before the throne, not on it. The throne was never sat in after Eärnur. This tradition — the empty throne, the steward's chair before it — lasts until Aragorn's coronation at the end of The Return of the King, when three thousand years of waiting are ended.

## Part 4: The Istari Arrive — The Wizards Come to Middle-earth

Around the year 1000 of the Third Age, when Sauron's shadow was beginning to stir again in the east (he had been gathering strength since approximately the year 1000, moving secretly), the Valar took council and decided to send help to the peoples of Middle-earth. The help they sent was the Istari — the Wizards.

They came as old men — deliberately limited in power, deliberately embodied in forms that were mortal in appearance. This was the condition of their mission: they were not permitted to match Sauron's power directly, not permitted to dominate the peoples of Middle-earth for their own good, not permitted to use the full power of their Maiar natures. They were counsellors and encouragers, able to act but constrained in how they acted.

Five came: Curunír (Saruman, later the White), Aiwendil (Radagast the Brown), Olórin (Gandalf the Grey), and two others whose names in Valinor were Alatar and Pallando (in Tolkien's later writing, Morinehtar and Rómestámo — Dark-slayer and East-helper). The two who went east are barely present in the main narrative — Tolkien mentions them as having gone into the east and perhaps having done some good there by converting peoples away from Sauron's influence. They never returned to the north. Their ultimate fate is unknown.

Gandalf arrived last among the five — or perhaps came separately. He was the youngest and seemingly the least among the Istari in terms of power and apparent authority. But he was the student of Nienna, the Vala of mercy and grief, and he was the most patient and the most genuinely loving of the five toward the small and the weak and the ordinary. Círdan perceived his true nature immediately — which is why Círdan gave him Narya, the ring of fire, upon his arrival at the Grey Havens. Narya was the ring of inspiration, of kindling hearts against weariness. It was exactly what Gandalf needed for his work.

Saruman was given the leadership of the Istari. He was the most obviously powerful, the most clearly authoritative, the most impressive in his learning and his rhetoric. He established himself in the tower of Orthanc in Isengard and studied the arts of the enemy — ring-lore, Mordor's dark craft — with academic thoroughness. Tolkien's point about Saruman is that the study of evil for the purpose of understanding it is not itself safe: spending centuries examining the enemy's methods, thinking constantly about the Ring and how to use it, eventually produced a desire for the Ring that went beyond the scholarly. By the time of The Lord of the Rings, Saruman wanted the Ring for himself, and his wanting of it had corrupted his mission entirely.

Radagast went to the forests and the birds and the beasts and paid less attention to the affairs of Elves and Men than his mission required. He was not corrupt — he was simply distracted by what he loved. His love of animals was genuine and good; his abandonment of the wider struggle was a failure of duty. He becomes, somewhat ironically, an important figure in Gandalf's movements simply because Saruman uses Radagast's genuine reputation for trustworthiness to lure Gandalf into the trap at Orthanc.

## Part 5: The White Council and Dol Guldur — The Shadow Identified

From approximately the year 1100 of the Third Age, a power began to grow in the south of the forest called Greenwood the Great — later called Mirkwood. The forest was already darkening: great spiders had come into it, the trees were becoming close and unhealthy, the shadow under the boughs was thickening. The power called itself the Necromancer and dwelt in a fortress called Dol Guldur — the Hill of Sorcery — in the southern part of the forest.

The Wise — the Istari and the great Elvish lords — suspected from early on that the Necromancer was Sauron. Gandalf went to Dol Guldur to investigate, perhaps around the year 2063, and Sauron fled before his coming — or more precisely, Sauron feigned departure, withdrawing to the east before Gandalf could confirm his identity. This is called the Watchful Peace, a period of approximately four hundred years during which Sauron appeared to be gone.

He was not gone. He returned to Dol Guldur in 2460, stronger than before.

The White Council was formed to coordinate the response of the Wise to the growing shadow. It included Saruman as its head, Gandalf, Radagast, Galadriel, Elrond, and Círdan. Galadriel had wanted Gandalf to be the head — she perceived his true quality better than the others — but Gandalf refused, and Saruman was elected. Saruman's leadership of the White Council was not neutral: he used it to suppress discussion of the Ring, to misdirect the Council's attention, to prevent any coordinated search for the Ring that might lead someone other than himself to it.

In the year 2850, Gandalf went again to Dol Guldur and this time found confirmation: it was Sauron. He also found Thráin, a Dwarf of Erebor's line — the father of Thorin Oakenshield — who had been captured and imprisoned there. Thráin gave Gandalf a key and a map before he died: the key and map of Erebor, the Lonely Mountain, which Thráin had carried since the Dwarves were driven out of Erebor by the dragon Smaug in 2770. These items Gandalf would eventually pass to Thorin Oakenshield, and they become the mechanism of the Quest of Erebor — which, as we shall see, was not simply a Dwarven business venture but a piece of strategic planning by Gandalf to deny Sauron a potential ally and weapon.

The White Council met in 2851 after Gandalf's return. He urged an immediate assault on Dol Guldur to drive Sauron out. Saruman argued against it. Saruman won the argument. His reason was ostensibly that the time was not right; his actual reason was that he hoped the One Ring would surface near Dol Guldur — the Gladden Fields where it had been lost were downriver from Dol Guldur's general area — and he wanted Sauron to remain in Dol Guldur so that the Ring, drawn to Sauron, might reveal itself to Saruman rather than to Sauron. He was already playing his own game.

The assault on Dol Guldur finally happened in 2941 — the same year as the Quest of Erebor, the same year as Bilbo's finding of the Ring. The Council moved, and Sauron fled — he had been preparing to move to Mordor in any case, his preparations there being sufficiently advanced. He abandoned Dol Guldur deliberately, choosing his moment. The Council's assault was a success in that Dol Guldur was cleared; it was not a success in that Sauron had already achieved what he needed to achieve and simply relocated.

## Part 6: The Dwarves and the Dragon — The Quest of Erebor

To understand The Hobbit fully, we need to understand the backstory that Tolkien laid out in considerable detail in Unfinished Tales, in his essay "The Quest of Erebor."

Erebor — the Lonely Mountain — was one of the great Dwarven kingdoms of the Third Age. It had been founded by Thráin I in approximately 1999 of the Third Age, after the Dwarves of Khazad-dûm had been driven out of Moria by the Balrog that awoke there in 1980 (of which more shortly). The Dwarves of Erebor prospered greatly, partly because Erebor sat above an enormous vein of gold and partly because the Dwarves who settled there were of the line of Durin — the longest-lived and most gifted of the Dwarvish peoples. The greatest treasure of Erebor was the Arkenstone — the Heart of the Mountain — a gem of such beauty that it became the symbol of the kingship, sought by whoever claimed the throne.

Smaug was a fire-drake of the north, one of the last and greatest of the dragons that had survived since the First Age or been bred in its aftermath. He heard of Erebor's wealth and in the year 2770 he descended on it — a single dragon, attacking without warning, sweeping the southern slopes of the mountain with his fire and driving the Dwarves out. He settled in the mountain's halls, lying on the accumulated treasure of the Dwarves, warming himself on their gold. Dragons are not purely acquisitive in the simple sense — they love gold, but they also love power and dominance and the knowledge that they hold what others want. Smaug on his hoard is a portrait of pride and greed and the pointless guarding of things that no longer serve any purpose.

The Dwarves driven from Erebor scattered. Thrór, the King Under the Mountain, eventually went with a few companions to try to reclaim Khazad-dûm, which the Dwarves called Moria. He was captured and killed by the Orcs who occupied the mountain — specifically killed by the Orc-chieftain Azog, who wrote Thrór's name on his forehead as an insult. This single act of cruelty started the War of the Dwarves and Orcs, the most complete conflict between those two peoples in the Third Age's history, lasting from 2793 to 2799. The Dwarves pursued the Orcs systematically through every mountain-hold from the Misty Mountains to the Iron Hills, killing them wherever they found them. The last great battle was at the gates of Moria — the Battle of Azanulbizar — where the Dwarves won decisively but at enormous cost, and Azog was killed by Dáin Ironfoot.

Thorin Oakenshield — grandson of Thrór, son of Thráin — received his nickname at Azanulbizar: his shield was cut from his arm and he fought on using an oak branch as both weapon and shield. This detail tells you everything you need to know about Thorin. He was not a comfortable king. He was a warrior and an exile, driven by the memory of what had been taken from his people and by the specific pride that characterised the line of Durin.

Gandalf saw in the Erebor situation a strategic problem and a strategic opportunity. A dragon in Erebor was dangerous not merely because Smaug was destructive in himself (though he was) but because Sauron might attempt to use Smaug as a weapon — to send the dragon against the peoples of the north, against Lake-town and Dale and the men of Rhovanion, as a preparation for war. Gandalf wanted Smaug removed, but he also wanted the Dwarves of Erebor restored as a power in the north, because a strong Dwarven kingdom in Erebor could serve as an anchor for the northern defences against Sauron that the north would desperately need.

He engineered the meeting between Thorin and Bilbo Baggins.

## Part 7: The Shire and Bilbo Baggins — An Unexpected Hobbit

The Hobbits require introduction, because they are perhaps the most original contribution of Tolkien's mythology — beings that exist nowhere in the earlier tradition, invented entirely by him, occupying a place in the world that is simultaneously central to its final crisis and entirely marginal to its history up to that point.

Hobbits appear in Middle-earth in the records of the Third Age only in approximately the year 1050, when they first appear in the chronicles of Eriador. Their origin is murky — they came from the east, from the region of the Anduin, moving west in a migration that eventually brought them over the Misty Mountains and into Eriador. In 1601, a party of Hobbits led by the brothers Marcho and Blanco crossed the Brandywine River and obtained leave from the King of Arnor (the nominal overlord of Eriador — by this time a Chieftain of the northern Dúnedain rather than a true king) to settle the land they called the Shire. This is the founding of the Shire, still within the reckoning of the Third Age but nearly thirteen hundred years before the events of The Lord of the Rings.

Hobbits are small — between two and four feet tall — and extraordinarily conservative in temperament. They love comfort: food (six meals a day), pipe-weed (an addiction to which they are culturally devoted), their hobbit-holes, their gardens, their families, their routines. They distrust adventures, dislike the extraordinary, prefer the familiar to the new. They are not cowardly, exactly — they have a surprising toughness when pressed — but they are not the kind of people who seek danger. In a world of heroes and wizards and kings, Hobbits are the ordinary people.

This ordinariness is the whole point. Tolkien's deepest argument in The Lord of the Rings — an argument that The Hobbit sets up and The Lord of the Rings executes — is that the ordinary person matters. Not because they are secretly extraordinary (though Frodo and Bilbo and Sam each have extraordinary qualities), but because ordinary people can do things that the great cannot. The great are too known, too significant, too watched. The great are exactly what Sauron is watching for. What he is not watching for, what he cannot imagine, is the small and the modest and the unassuming.

Bilbo Baggins was fifty years old when Gandalf came to his door in the Shire with thirteen Dwarves and an adventure. He was a Baggins — respectable, unadventurous, entirely comfortable — and also a Took on his mother's side, the Tooks being a family with a reputation for occasional eccentric behaviour. The Took strain in him, suppressed by decades of respectable Baggins living, was the crack through which Gandalf inserted his lever.

Bilbo is one of Tolkien's most fully realised characters precisely because he is so ordinary. He is funny and slightly pompous and capable of surprising courage and occasionally more interested in his creature-comforts than is strictly heroic. He is not brave in the way that Túrin was brave or Fingolfin was brave — the terrible courage of people who have decided that death is preferable to submission. He is brave in the more everyday sense: he does the frightening thing because it needs doing, even though he would genuinely prefer not to, and he manages to be rather cheerful about it.

The Dwarves — Thorin, Fíli, Kíli, Balin, Dwalin, Óin, Glóin, Dori, Nori, Ori, Bifur, Bofur, Bombur — are a company of thirteen, which Tolkien chose deliberately as an unlucky number. They are not uniformly characterized in The Hobbit with the individuality they receive in the film adaptations, but they are a community, and their community dynamics are important: Thorin is the leader and the one who carries the weight of the quest's meaning most fully; Balin is the eldest and wisest; Fíli and Kíli are Thorin's young nephews; Bombur is cheerfully corpulent and usually in difficulties of some kind.

They met at Bag End, ate Bilbo's food, and set out the next morning — or rather, the Dwarves left and Gandalf had to send Bilbo running after them without a handkerchief, because respectable Hobbits don't leave without their handkerchiefs.

## Part 8: The Quest of Erebor — The Journey East

The journey from the Shire to Erebor is the substance of The Hobbit, and it covers a physical distance of over a thousand miles and an interior distance — Bilbo's transformation from comfortable homebody to seasoned adventurer — that is considerably harder to measure.

The first adventure was with the Trolls — William, Bert, and Tom — three great cave-trolls who captured the company and were arguing about cooking methods when Gandalf's voice imitated their arguing long enough to keep them occupied until dawn petrified them all. Tolkien's trolls are descended from the trolls of the First Age — beings made in mockery of Ents, just as Orcs were made in mockery of Elves, incapable of surviving sunlight. Their names are deliberately prosaic and their argument about cooking methods is deliberately comic — Tolkien was writing The Hobbit initially as a story for his children, and the lighter register of its early sections reflects that. The troll-cave contained swords from the ancient wars: Gandalf took Glamdring, Thorin took Orcrist, and Bilbo found a short Elvish blade that glowed blue near Orcs, which he would name Sting.

Rivendell was the next stop — the Last Homely House, as Tolkien called it. The Company rested there, Elrond read the moon-runes on Thrór's map (runes that could only be read by the light of the same kind of moon under which they were written), and they set out for the Misty Mountains.

The Goblin-tunnels under the Misty Mountains were the site of the most important event of the entire Third Age, though nobody — including Bilbo — understood it at the time. The company was captured by the Goblins of the mountains; Gandalf killed the Great Goblin; they escaped; and in the chaos of the escape, Bilbo was separated from the others and fell deep into the mountain, landing in a subterranean lake.

There he found the Ring.

## Part 9: The Riddle-game — Gollum and the Ring Found

The Ring had been lying at the bottom of the Anduin for nearly two and a half millennia since it slipped from Isildur's finger. Exactly how it came to be under the Misty Mountains requires a brief account of Gollum's history.

Sméagol was a Stoor Hobbit — one of the three breeds of Hobbits, the Stoors being the most river-loving — who lived with his family near the Gladden Fields in approximately the year 2463 of the Third Age. He was a natural fisherman, a curious creature, somewhat solitary even before the Ring came to him. His cousin Déagol found the Ring while fishing in the Anduin — the Ring, drawn by Sauron's renewed influence (Sauron had just returned to Dol Guldur in 2460, and the Ring was responding), surfaced into the shallows where Déagol was fishing.

Sméagol saw the Ring and wanted it immediately — with an immediacy and totality that went beyond ordinary desire into the Ring's specific corruption. He killed Déagol on Déagol's birthday and took the Ring, claiming it as his "birthday present."

Over the following years, the Ring extended Sméagol's life far beyond the Hobbit norm (Hobbits ordinarily lived to about a hundred, though the oldest had been a hundred and thirty) while destroying everything else about him. His family called him Gollum — after the unpleasant swallowing sound he made in his throat — and eventually drove him away. He went under the mountains, deeper and deeper, into the cold tunnels under the Misty Mountains, and there he lived alone for nearly five hundred years, catching fish in the underground lake, occasionally emerging at night to catch Goblins for food, talking to himself in a split dialogue that expressed the Ring's corruption perfectly: "Gollum" the corrupted voice, and what remained of "Sméagol" — the being he had been.

Bilbo found the Ring on the cave floor, felt it in his pocket before he knew what it was, and put it in his pocket. He found Gollum. They played the Riddle-game — an old and formally sacred tradition among Hobbits, and by implication among older peoples, with genuine rules and consequences. If Bilbo won, Gollum would show him the way out; if Gollum won, he would eat Bilbo. Bilbo won with his last riddle: "What have I got in my pocket?" — which was technically not a proper riddle at all but which Gollum accepted under duress.

Gollum went to get his "birthday present" to kill Bilbo with — and found it gone. The Ring was in Bilbo's pocket. In his rage and despair, Gollum ran for the exit — which showed Bilbo where the exit was. Bilbo, wearing the Ring (which made him invisible), escaped through the Gate and rejoined the company outside.

The Ring was already working on Bilbo. His account of how he found it — which he gave to Gandalf, and which differed from what actually happened, substituting a gift from Gollum for the finding and the Riddle-game — was the Ring's first successful manipulation of its new bearer. The Ring encouraged the lie. The lie would matter: Gandalf noticed the discrepancy years later, and the discrepancy was one of the pieces of evidence that led him to investigate the Ring's nature.

## Part 10: Eagles and Bears — Over the Misty Mountains

The company, reunited outside the Goblin-gate, was immediately pursued by the Wargs — wild wolves allied with the Goblins — and took refuge in pine trees. The Goblins and Wargs set fire to the trees below them. And then Gandalf summoned the Eagles.

The Great Eagles of the Misty Mountains were descendants of Manwë's Eagles — the same lineage as Thorondor who had fought in the First Age. Their king, Gwaihir the Windlord, was not Thorondor but was of his kin. They rescued the company and carried them east of the mountains.

At the eastern edge of the Misty Mountains lived Beorn — a skin-changer, a man who could take the form of a great bear. He was large in human form, enormous in bear form, hospitable after his fashion, and deeply suspicious of strangers. His origin is never explained in Tolkien's texts — he was descended from ancient men (or bears) who had lived in the mountain valleys before the Orcs came, and his shape-changing was simply part of what he was. He provided the company with food and ponies and guidance for the crossing of Mirkwood, and his bear-form fought at the Battle of the Five Armies.

Mirkwood — Taur-nu-Fuin in the old tongue, the same name as the darkened forest of Dorthonion in the First Age — was by this time fully under the shadow of Dol Guldur. It was a place of great spiders (descendants of Shelob, who was herself a descendant of Ungoliant), of disorienting darkness, of paths that disappeared and streams whose waters caused forgetfulness. The company became lost; they were captured by the spiders; Bilbo used Sting and the Ring's invisibility to rescue them; and then they were captured by the Wood-elves.

The Wood-elves of Mirkwood were the subjects of Thranduil — called by Tolkien the Elvenking — a Sindar Elf who had come to Mirkwood in the Second Age and whose people were Silvan Elves, the eastern Wood-elves descended from those who had never gone to Valinor. Thranduil was not corrupt, but he was suspicious, proud, and extremely possessive about treasure — characteristics that would create the conflict with the Dwarves at Erebor later. He imprisoned the Dwarves but was more hospitable to Bilbo, who being small and quick and invisible was harder to catch.

Bilbo got the Dwarves out in wine-barrels floated down the Forest River to the Long Lake and the town of Esgaroth — Lake-town — whose Master was a politician of the ordinary variety, calculating advantage and popularity while Thorin spoke of the restoration of the Dwarven kingdom and the people of Lake-town cheered for the promised share of Erebor's wealth.

---

## Part 11: Erebor and the Dragon — Bilbo Beneath the Mountain

The Lonely Mountain. Bilbo went into the mountain through the hidden door that Thrór's map had described — a small, almost invisible door in the west face of the mountain, opened by the last light of Durin's Day (when the setting sun and the young moon hung together) with Thrór's key. He went alone, because that was the arrangement: Bilbo was the Burglar, the fourteenth member of the company hired to do the work that thirteen Dwarves could not do without revealing themselves.

The tunnels inside the mountain led down into Smaug's hall. Tolkien's description of that hall — the enormous vaulted space filled with the accumulated treasure of an ancient kingdom, and at its center the vast sleeping form of the dragon, lying on a bed of gold and jewels, one eye half-open — is one of his finest pieces of descriptive writing. There is a terrible grandeur to Smaug, even asleep: the scale of the hoarding, the scale of the creature, the warmth of the dragon-heat on the stone, the gleam of reflected torchlight on ten thousand gold coins.

Bilbo stole a cup. He came back a second time and had a conversation with Smaug — Smaug woke but could not see the invisible Bilbo, and they talked, and Bilbo was flattering and clever and Smaug was vain enough to be pleased by flattery even from an unseen intruder. But in the conversation, Bilbo noticed something: a patch on Smaug's underbelly — his left breast — where the encrusting of gems and gold had not covered the skin, left a bare patch. A weak point. He did not tell Smaug this.

He told the Thrush. There is a tradition in the old northern lands around Erebor that the Thrushes could understand the speech of Men and Dwarves, and one of those Thrushes heard Bilbo speak of the weak point. That Thrush flew to the ruin of Dale and found an old man named Bard — a descendant of Girion, the last lord of Dale — who could understand the bird's speech. Bard was standing on the ramparts of Lake-town when Smaug came to burn it.

Smaug left the mountain in rage — someone had stolen from him, had mocked him, had intruded — and went to Lake-town. He breathed fire on the town. He had burned Dale before, a century and a half ago, and he remembered how to do it. But Bard, armed with his last black arrow (a special arrow, an heirloom of his house), shot Smaug in the bare patch on his left breast as the dragon swept overhead. Smaug fell into the Long Lake and died, and his fall crushed the town's great market, and the waves from his fall swamped the remaining buildings, and Lake-town was destroyed.

The death of Smaug is both triumphant and understated — Tolkien kills his dragon at a distance, through a bird's intelligence and a Man's marksmanship, with Bilbo not even present. The hero of the story is elsewhere when the dragon dies. This is deliberate. The Hobbit consistently subverts the conventions of dragon-slaying tales while delivering the emotional satisfactions of them.

---

## Part 12: The Battle of Five Armies — An Old World Conflict

With Smaug dead, every power in the north had reason to converge on Erebor. Bard led the surviving people of Lake-town and the Elvenking Thranduil came with his army — both had legitimate claims to a share of the dragon's treasure, both needed it for their peoples. Thorin, already showing the first signs of dragon-sickness (the gold-madness that Smaug's treasure induced in those who guarded it), refused all claims and barricaded himself in the mountain.

The standoff between Thorin in the mountain and Bard and Thranduil outside it was resolved not by diplomacy but by the arrival of an Orc army from the Misty Mountains — Bolg, son of Azog (the same Azog who had killed Thrór), leading the collected remnant of the northern Orcs who had survived the War of the Dwarves and Orcs, seeking revenge.

The Battle of Five Armies — Men, Elves, and Dwarves (reinforced by Dáin Ironfoot's Iron Hills Dwarves who arrived in the nick of time) against Orcs and Wargs — is the climactic battle of The Hobbit. Thorin came out of the mountain and fought with his old courage; he killed Bolg, but was mortally wounded in the doing. The Eagles arrived — Gwaihir and his people — and Beorn arrived in bear-form and turned the battle decisively. The Orcs were routed.

Thorin's death, and his reconciliation with Bilbo before it, is the emotional climax of the book. He had driven Bilbo away earlier in his gold-madness — had been on the point of having him killed after Bilbo tried to give the Arkenstone (which he had secreted from the hoard) to Bard as a negotiating tool to prevent the battle. But dying, he found his way back to himself. His last words to Bilbo — that if more of us valued food and cheer and song above hoarded gold, it would be a merrier world — are among the most important sentences in The Hobbit, the statement of its moral in direct speech.

Thorin was buried under the mountain with the Arkenstone on his breast, and Orcrist was returned to him by the Elves. Balin and Dwalin and the surviving Dwarves went on to restore Erebor under Dáin Ironfoot's kingship. Bard rebuilt Dale. Thranduil returned to Mirkwood. And Bilbo went home.

He went home with the Ring in his pocket. And with Sting, and his mithril coat — a gift from Thorin, a shirt of rings made of true silver that a dragon's claw could not pierce. Both the mithril coat and Sting would be given to Frodo sixty years later.

---

## Part 13: Bilbo's Long Autumn — Sixty Years with the Ring

Bilbo came home to find that he had been declared dead and his belongings were being auctioned. He recovered most of them. He was regarded thereafter by his neighbours as "queer" — a Baggins who had gone on an adventure and come back slightly changed. He wrote about it, eventually, in the book he called There and Back Again.

He kept the Ring. He called it "my precious" on occasion — a slip of the tongue that he would have denied — and he used it occasionally: to disappear from his relatives, to avoid the Sackville-Bagginses, to slip away when he found social obligations tedious. The Ring extended his life. At one hundred and eleven years old — at his "eleventy-first birthday," as The Lord of the Rings begins — he did not look old. He felt stretched thin, as he told Gandalf. The Ring was wearing on him, and he could feel it.

Gandalf had been watching. He had noticed the discrepancy in Bilbo's account of how he found the Ring — the gift story versus the finding story. He had pressed Bilbo on it, gently, and Bilbo had become uncharacteristically sharp and possessive. Gandalf had begun to suspect something more than an ordinary magic ring. He had begun, in the years between The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings, to research the history of the Ring.

The research took decades. Tolkien gives this careful attention in the Lord of the Rings itself — Gandalf tells Frodo that he spent long years in study before he was certain, including time in the archives of Minas Tirith examining the records of the Second Age. What he was looking for — and eventually found — was confirmation that the Ring matched the description of the One Ring made by Sauron in the Second Age. The fire-writing test was the final confirmation: the One Ring, when heated, revealed the inscription in the Black Speech that Sauron had put on it.

He also discovered something about Gollum. Aragorn was enlisted to track Gollum — the Rangers of the North had been keeping an eye on many things in the wild, and Aragorn, as their Chieftain, was one of Gandalf's most trusted confederates. Gollum was found in the Emyn Muil, captured by Aragorn, and brought to Mirkwood, where Thranduil held him. Gandalf interrogated him. From Gollum's fragmented and untrustworthy account, he pieced together the history: Sméagol, the Gladden Fields, five hundred years under the Misty Mountains, the loss of the Ring to a "Baggins." This confirmed the chain of possession. The Ring was Sauron's Ring.

Meanwhile, Gollum was held in Mirkwood — and then escaped, or was released. Tolkien's account of how Gollum got free is that the Mirkwood Elves, keeping him in a treetop pen with some freedom to move around, were attacked by Orcs one night, and in the confusion Gollum vanished. This escape was not innocent: Sauron's servants had found Gollum, had tortured from him the name "Baggins" and the word "Shire," and had released him hoping he would lead them to the Ring.

---

## Part 14: Bilbo's Party and the Passing of the Ring

On the evening of September 22, in the year 3001 of the Third Age — the year of Bilbo's hundred-and-eleventh birthday — the great party happened. It was one of the most famous events in Hobbit history: fireworks by Gandalf, a feast of legendary proportions, the presence of all the Shire's worthies, and Bilbo's speech. The speech concluded with a conjuring trick: he put on the Ring and vanished, walked out of the party, and went home to Bag End.

Gandalf had made Bilbo promise to leave the Ring behind for Frodo. The exchange between Bilbo and Gandalf in the study of Bag End is one of the most revealing passages in The Lord of the Rings. Bilbo's resistance — his anger at the suggestion, his calling the Ring his "precious" (and then being appalled at himself for saying it), his accusing Gandalf of wanting it for himself — shows exactly what sixty years with the Ring had done. He was not evil. He was one of the most decent beings who had ever carried the Ring. And still it had done this to him. When Gandalf's full presence pressed against his resistance, Bilbo let go — and immediately felt lighter, freer. He had not known he was burdened.

He left the Ring on the mantelpiece and walked out of Bag End, and went to Rivendell, and lived there happily for the remaining years of his life, writing and translating and being rather beloved by the Elves. He gave Frodo everything: Bag End, his property, his book, his mithril coat, Sting.

Frodo received the Ring.

---

## Part 15: The Shadow Closes — Seventeen Years and the Hunt Begins

Here is a detail that readers sometimes miss: between Bilbo's leaving and Frodo's leaving, seventeen years pass. Gandalf disappeared for most of that time, doing his research. Frodo lived quietly at Bag End with the Ring in his possession, not knowing what it was, not using it, carrying it as a keepsake. The Ring was influencing him — he had noticed he felt oddly well, oddly young for his age — but not yet urgently.

When Gandalf returned in the spring of year 3018 of the Third Age, he knew the full truth. He told Frodo what the Ring was. He told him that Sauron was looking for it, that the Nazgûl were already abroad, that they knew the name "Baggins" and the word "Shire," that Frodo had to leave — had to leave now, that same night if possible, or very soon. He told Frodo to go to Rivendell, to go by way of Bree, to go as soon as could be managed and not along the main road.

The leaving of Bag End — Frodo, Sam Gamgee (who had overheard Gandalf's conversation through the kitchen window and was compelled to accompany his master as consequence and as love), and eventually Merry Brandybuck and Pippin Took — is the beginning of the actual narrative of The Lord of the Rings. Everything before it is prologue.

Sam Gamgee deserves a pause here. He is, on one level, the archetype of the loyal servant — the devoted helper, the working-class companion to the gentleman hero. On another level, he is the most fully developed character in the trilogy and the most genuinely heroic. His love for Frodo is not subservient; it is chosen, over and over, under conditions that no one could have predicted or required him to endure. He carries Frodo up Mount Doom. He faces Shelob alone. He keeps going when everything rational would say to stop. Sam is what the ordinary person looks like when they choose, repeatedly and with full understanding of what they're choosing, to be extraordinary.

## Part 16: The Road Goes Ever On — From the Shire to Bree

The four Hobbits went south through the Shire, then east by the back-roads, avoiding the main road (which Gandalf had warned was watched). Their first encounter with the Black Riders — the Nazgûl, now visible and active in Middle-earth for the first time since the Third Age began — happened on the road near Woodhall, where Frodo smelled something dark and felt the Ring pulling at his finger and himself pulling at it. The Nazgûl could not see the Hobbits — they perceived the world mostly through the Ring's influence and through smell and hearing — but they could sense the Ring.

The Hobbits were saved repeatedly in this early section by Tom Bombadil — who found them lost in the Old Forest, rescued them from the Barrow-wights, and gave them the ancient blades from the barrow, blades of Westernesse (Númenórean make) that had been enchanted specifically against the servants of the Witch-king. These blades matter: the sword that Merry uses to stab the Witch-king at the Battle of the Pelennor Fields is one of them, and it is that stab — into the back of the Witch-king's knee — that destroys the black magic holding the Witch-king's form together, making it possible for Éowyn to finish him.

At Bree they met Strider — Aragorn son of Arathorn, Chieftain of the Dúnedain of the North, heir of Isildur, the future King of the Reunited Kingdom. He was sitting in the corner of the Prancing Pony looking like exactly what he appeared to be: a rough Ranger, travel-stained, smoking a pipe, watching. He gave them a letter from Gandalf — who had been delayed, captured by Saruman at Orthanc, and had only recently escaped — explaining that Strider was to be trusted.

This is one of Tolkien's finest character introductions. Aragorn is everything that the heroic archetype promises — great lineage, great skill, great destiny — and he is also genuinely humble and patient about it, having spent eighty-seven years as a Ranger serving the north, doing the unglamorous work of keeping the Shire safe from things the Hobbits never knew were dangerous. He knows who he is and what he is, but he has not romanticised it. He knows the cost of it.

The Nazgûl attacked the inn at Bree that night. They found the Hobbits' rooms empty — Aragorn had suspected the attack and moved them.

---

## Part 17: Weathertop and the Wound That Would Not Heal

The road from Bree to Rivendell crossed the great empty lands of Eriador — the ruins of Arnor, former kingdom of the North, which had been inhabited and cultivated for centuries and was now empty save for scattered villages, the Rangers who patrolled it, and the various dangers of the wild. The Midgewater Marshes annoyed them. The Weather Hills, the range of hills running north to south that had been watchtowers in the days of Arnor, were the next significant landmark.

On Weathertop — the ruined watch-tower called Amon Sûl in Sindarin, where the greatest palantír of the north had been kept before Arvedui lost it — the five Nazgûl came. Aragorn drove them off with fire, but not before their leader — the Witch-king — stabbed Frodo in the left shoulder with a Morgul-blade. Not the full blade: the hilt, but with the tip broken off. The broken tip was in the wound, moving toward his heart, and if it reached his heart he would become a wraith, subject to the will of the Nazgûl.

The race to Rivendell that follows — with Frodo increasingly sick, the cold of the Morgul-wound spreading through him, the Nazgûl pursuing across the last days of road — is one of the finest sustained sequences in the book. Frodo's bearing of the wound and his riding to Rivendell alone when the other horses could not be taken across the Ford of Bruinen (because the Nazgûl controlled horses from the air through terror) shows exactly what the Hobbits are capable of: not the ability to fight, but the sheer endurance of doing what must be done even when everything in your body says to stop.

Arwen — Elrond's daughter, the most beautiful being in the Third Age's world, the descendant of Lúthien — met Frodo at the Ford. She had ridden from Rivendell at Glorfindel's urging (Glorfindel had found the company first, given Frodo his horse Asfaloth). She commanded the Ford, and the Nazgûl pursued Frodo into the river, and Elrond released the flood — the waters of the Bruinen rose in a great wave shaped like white horses, and nine dark riders were swept away, their horses destroyed, the wraiths themselves temporarily scattered and robbed of physical form.

Frodo was brought to Rivendell and healed by Elrond.

---

## Part 18: Rivendell — The Council of Elrond

At Rivendell, the great threads that had been developing separately were drawn together for the first time into a single conversation. The Council of Elrond was attended by representatives of every free people of Middle-earth who had a stake in the Ring's fate: Elves (Glorfindel, Galdor of the Grey Havens, Legolas of Mirkwood — the son of Thranduil), Dwarves (Glóin, who had been a member of Thorin's company sixty years before, and his son Gimli), Men (Boromir of Gondor, the son of the Steward Denethor), and Hobbits (Frodo, Bilbo, and eventually Sam, who was hiding behind a bush).

Gandalf told the full story of the Ring — its making, its history, Bilbo's finding of it, the fire-writing test. Boromir raised the question that was on every mind: why not use the Ring? If it was a weapon of power, why not use it against Sauron? The answer — Elrond's, Gandalf's, eventually Galadriel's when it was put to her — was that the Ring could not be used by anyone for good, because it was made to dominate, and domination was what it produced in its user regardless of their intent. The good person would wield it and become merely Sauron with a different face. The Ring had to be destroyed.

It could only be destroyed in the fires of Orodruin, where it was made.

Frodo volunteered to take it. This is the moment that the Council arrives at — the quest is not assigned, it is volunteered. And Frodo volunteers not because he is brave in any ordinary sense, not because he is powerful, but because he recognises that if someone has to do it, he might as well be the one. He is a small Hobbit from the Shire with a Ring in his pocket and he offers to carry it to the worst place in the world. Tolkien stages the moment with characteristic understatement: "I will take the Ring, though I do not know the way."

The Fellowship was assembled: Frodo, Sam, Merry, Pippin; Gandalf; Aragorn; Legolas; Gimli; Boromir. Nine walkers, against the nine Nazgûl.

---

## Part 19: The Fellowship's Road — From Rivendell to Moria

The Fellowship went south from Rivendell. Their first attempt at a route was over the pass of Caradhras — the Redhorn Gate — through the Misty Mountains. The attempt was defeated by winter storms that Tolkien implies were partly natural and partly willed by some power (possibly Sauron, possibly a spirit of the mountain itself, Caradhras the Cruel as the Dwarves called it). They were driven back.

The alternative was Moria. The Mines of Khazad-dûm, the ancient Dwarven realm under the Misty Mountains, abandoned since the awakening of the Balrog in the year 1980 of the Third Age. The awakening had been caused by the Dwarves mining too deep under Balin's leadership — they had reached the deepest level of the mine, what they called the Kheled-zâram, and had freed something that had been sleeping since the First Age or possibly since the Second, one of the Balrogs that had survived the War of Wrath by hiding in the deep places of the earth.

Balin had attempted to recolonize Moria — this was in the year 2989, about thirty years before the Fellowship. He had led a company of Dwarves from Erebor and for a few years they had managed to establish themselves. Then the Orcs had come in great numbers, and the colony had failed. Gandalf knew this was likely. He also knew there was no alternative.

They entered through the Doors of Durin — made by Celebrimbor, with the Elvish inscription that yields "Mellon" as its answer — after Gandalf solved the riddle and they dealt with a Watcher in the Water that guarded the gate.

Inside Moria, they found Balin's tomb — found the record of the colony's end in a few desperate journal entries: "We cannot get out. The end comes." They found the Orcs still there, and fought them. And then the Balrog came.

---

## Part 20: Gandalf and the Balrog — The Bridge of Khazad-dûm

The creature that came out of the deep places of Moria was a Balrog — a Maia of the First Age who had chosen Morgoth's service and been wreathed in fire and shadow. It was vast: a being of flame and darkness that filled the bridge before the Fellowship and behind it massed Orcs and Wargs beyond number. The Fellowship ran for the Bridge of Khazad-dûm — the narrow span over the bottomless pit that was the only crossing between the eastern and western parts of Moria.

Gandalf stood on the bridge. He faced the Balrog and spoke words that have become among the most famous in Tolkien's work: "You cannot pass. I am a servant of the Secret Fire, wielder of the flame of Anor. You cannot pass. The dark fire will not avail you, flame of Udûn. Go back to the Shadow! You cannot pass."

The Balrog stepped onto the bridge. Gandalf struck the bridge with his staff and the span broke. The Balrog fell. And as it fell, it wrapped its whip around Gandalf's ankle and pulled him with it.

Gandalf fell into the abyss. His last words to the Fellowship, shouted as he fell, were: "Fly, you fools."

The Fellowship escaped from Moria into the light of the East Gate, the Dimrill Dale, weeping. They had lost their guide, the one who had understood most clearly what needed to be done and how. They were leaderless in the wilderness of Rhovanion, with Sauron's forces alert and the Road to Mordor still before them.

The fall of Gandalf in Moria — which will be discussed in more detail in the next article, when his return is also addressed — is the structural centre of The Lord of the Rings. Before it, the Fellowship is one unit moving with a plan. After it, the Fellowship is broken, moving in fragments, each making choices that the absence of Gandalf makes harder. Everything in the second half of the story flows from that fall.

Gandalf did not die. But no one in the Fellowship knew that.

---

## Part 21: Lothlórien and the Breaking of the Fellowship

The Fellowship came to Lothlórien — the Golden Wood, the realm of Galadriel and Celeborn, maintained by the power of Nenya, the Ring of Water. Time moved differently there, as several of the Fellowship noticed. The trees were extraordinary, the light was extraordinary, and the silence had quality. Galadriel and Celeborn received them.

Galadriel tested them, one by one, with visions and with offers — she showed them their heart's temptations and observed how they responded. Each of the Fellowship was offered something: Boromir was not offered a specific vision but his desire for the Ring was already acute, visible to her. Frodo, at the Mirror of Galadriel, saw things past and present and possible — he saw the Eye of Sauron, and the Eye saw him. He offered Galadriel the Ring. She refused it — one of the great refusal scenes in Tolkien, in which she shows what she would become if she accepted it and chooses to remain what she is.

She gave the Fellowship gifts before they left: the Phial of Galadriel for Frodo (the light of Eärendil's star, caught in water), the elven-cloaks for all, lembas bread, boats for the journey. Gimli's gift was the most personal: three hairs from Galadriel's head, which he asked for and she gave, the only personal treasure he sought. This exchange between the Dwarf and the Elf is one of the lovelier moments in the books, a gesture of genuine connection across what had been centuries of hostility between the two peoples.

The Fellowship left Lothlórien by boat down the Anduin, and the river carried them south toward the choice point: east to Mordor directly, or west with Boromir toward Gondor.

The breaking of the Fellowship at Amon Hen — the Hill of Sight, an ancient Númenórean watchpoint — was triggered by Boromir's attempt to take the Ring from Frodo by force. Boromir was a great man and a genuinely good man corrupted by the Ring's influence and by the specific pressure of his situation: he loved Gondor, he knew Gondor was failing, he knew the Ring was power, and the logic of using it seemed irresistible to him no matter what Gandalf and Elrond and everyone else had said. He seized Frodo, demanded the Ring. Frodo put on the Ring — became invisible — and ran.

Boromir recovered himself almost immediately. He was horrified by what he had done, and ran back to the others. But the damage was done.

Frodo made his decision: he would go to Mordor alone, or as nearly alone as he could manage. He would not endanger the others. He would not let the Ring work on the Fellowship the way it had worked on Boromir. He tried to take one of the boats and cross the Anduin alone.

Sam went after him. Sam, who could not swim, waded into the river rather than let Frodo go. Frodo, already in the boat and rowing, saw this and turned back and pulled Sam into the boat. They crossed together.

"Of course you are," said Frodo. "And I'm coming with you, even if I don't know the way."

Meanwhile, the Uruk-hai sent by Saruman — large, fast, bred specifically to travel in daylight and to fight better than ordinary Orcs — attacked the remaining Fellowship members. Boromir died defending Merry and Pippin. He died fighting Orcs, alone on a hilltop, defending the Hobbits even as they were taken. He died redeemed — the last thing he told Aragorn, who found him with three Orc-arrows in him and still standing, was that he had tried to take the Ring. "I am going to Gondor," he said, and died.

Aragorn released the boats with Boromir's body in the largest, with his sword and shield and the Orc-weapons he had killed. The river carried him south over Rauros Falls and to Gondor ahead of any news. The Fellowship was broken.

Frodo and Sam were in the east, heading toward Mordor alone. Merry and Pippin were captive of the Uruk-hai, being carried north. Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli were on the western bank, making their choice: pursue Merry and Pippin, or follow Frodo. They chose Merry and Pippin — Frodo at least had Sam, and Sam was worth more than most armies, though they did not yet fully know this.

The war of the Ring had begun in earnest. In three directions, the Fellowship moved through a world that was closing in around them.

---

## Conclusion: The Third Age at Its Crisis

The Third Age had lasted three thousand years. In those years, the kingdoms of the Dúnedain had diminished from the glory of Elendil's founding to the reduced but still-present states of the late Third Age — Gondor still magnificent but the line of kings broken, Arnor entirely gone with only the Rangers remaining, the line of Isildur reduced to wanderers in the wild. The Elves had faded from the height of the First Age — Lothlórien was beautiful but it was maintained by a Ring of Power, not by Elvish vitality unaided. The Dwarves of Erebor had been restored but remained one kingdom among the few that had survived. The Hobbits had gone undisturbed in their Shire.

And the Ring had passed from Gollum to Bilbo to Frodo, and was now on Frodo's finger in the wilderness east of the Anduin, and Sauron was looking for it with everything he had, and the Nine Nazgûl were abroad, and Saruman had joined the enemy, and Merry and Pippin were prisoner of the Uruk-hai.

Everything hung on Frodo and Sam, alone in the dark.
