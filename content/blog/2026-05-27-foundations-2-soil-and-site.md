# Part 2 of 10 — What Lies Beneath: Soil Science, Site Evaluation, and Geotechnical Investigation

Picture this: two houses on the same street, built the same year, by the same contractor, with the same floor plan. Thirty years later, one of them is perfectly plumb, all its doors and windows open and close smoothly, and the basement is dry. The other has diagonal cracks running from every window corner, a visible hump in the living room floor, and a basement that weeps water every spring. The neighbors think it is just bad luck. It is not bad luck. It is soil.

The ground beneath those two houses is different in some critical way — different moisture content, different clay mineralogy, different depth to a water table, different history of fill or disturbance — and that difference determined the fate of the foundation. The tragedy is that this difference was knowable before construction began, if anyone had looked.

This article is about looking. It is about the science that explains why soils behave so differently from each other, the field and laboratory tests that reveal what a specific site's soil will do under load, and the process of translating those test results into actionable foundation recommendations. By the end, you will be able to read a basic geotechnical report, understand what the engineer is telling you, and ask the right follow-up questions.

## Part 1: Soil Composition — What Soil Actually Is

Soil is not a material in the way that steel or concrete is a material. It is a three-phase system: solid particles, water occupying the voids between particles, and air in whatever void space the water does not occupy. The behavior of soil depends on the proportions and properties of all three phases simultaneously.

### The Solid Phase: Particle Size Distribution

The most fundamental property of soil is the size of its solid particles. Geotechnical engineers use a specific classification system, standardized through ASTM (American Society for Testing and Materials) and AASHTO (American Association of State Highway and Transportation Officials):

**Gravel**: Particles between 4.75 mm and 75 mm in diameter. Gravel has very high shear strength, drains freely, and is almost never problematic for foundations unless it is so loose that it has negligible bearing capacity (unusual in natural deposits but common in improperly placed fill).

**Sand**: Particles between 0.075 mm and 4.75 mm. The boundary between fine sand and silt is critically important — sand drains freely, silt does not. Coarse sand (0.5–4.75 mm) is excellent for foundations. Fine sand (0.075–0.25 mm) can be problematic in saturated, seismically active areas due to liquefaction potential.

**Silt**: Particles between 0.002 mm and 0.075 mm. Too fine to drain freely, but not fine enough to have significant plasticity. Silt is a frustrating material: it has low bearing capacity when wet, is frost-susceptible (the worst material for frost heave problems), and tends to settle under load. The silts of the Mississippi Valley and the Midwestern river floodplains have challenged builders for generations.

**Clay**: Particles smaller than 0.002 mm (2 micrometers). At this scale, particle shape and mineralogy matter enormously. Clay particles are typically plate-shaped (flaky), giving them enormous surface area relative to their mass. This surface area is where water adsorption, ion exchange, and plastic behavior originate.

Real soils are almost never a single particle size. They are mixtures, and the mixture matters. The Unified Soil Classification System (USCS), developed by Arthur Casagrande and now standardized as ASTM D2487, classifies soils based on their particle size distribution and plasticity into groups that have predictable engineering behavior:

- **GW, GP**: Well-graded and poorly-graded gravels — excellent foundation material
- **GM, GC**: Silty and clayey gravels — generally good
- **SW, SP**: Well-graded and poorly-graded sands — good in most conditions
- **SM, SC**: Silty and clayey sands — moderate; SM is frost-susceptible
- **ML**: Low-plasticity silt — problematic; worst for frost heave
- **MH**: High-plasticity silt — highly problematic
- **CL**: Low-plasticity clay — moderate; manageable with proper design
- **CH**: High-plasticity clay — the expansive clay problem; highly problematic
- **OL, OH**: Organic silts and clays — essentially unbuildable without deep foundations
- **PT**: Peat — completely unbuildable on directly

When a geotechnical report says the site has "CH soils in the upper 8 feet," it is telling you that the top 8 feet of soil is high-plasticity clay — the most demanding type for foundation design. You now know what that means.

### Clay Mineralogy: The Crucial Detail Within Clay

Not all clays are created equal. The specific clay mineral present determines how expansive a clay is.

The three most common clay mineral groups in the context of foundation engineering are:

**Kaolinite**: Relatively stable. Kaolinite clays have modest plasticity and low swell potential. Common in the southeastern United States (Georgia, South Carolina, Virginia), where deeply weathered granitic and metamorphic rocks have decomposed in place over millions of years. Kaolinite clay is not ideal foundation material, but it is manageable.

**Illite**: Intermediate plasticity and swell. Common in the Midwest and Great Plains. More problematic than kaolinite but far less than smectite.

**Smectite (Montmorillonite)**: Extremely high plasticity and swell potential. Smectite is the primary constituent of the infamous "black cotton soil" of Texas, Oklahoma, and Colorado, and of similar deposits worldwide. Smectite clay crystals can absorb enormous quantities of water between their layers, expanding like a sponge. A pure smectite clay sample can swell by 1,500% when exposed to water. In field conditions with mixed soils, 5% to 25% volume change is common. The Dallas-Fort Worth area sits on marine shale deposits rich in smectite that have been producing foundation nightmares since European settlement.

Identifying clay mineralogy requires X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis in a laboratory, or can be inferred from the Atterberg limits (Liquid Limit and Plastic Limit) — high values strongly suggest smectite.

### The Liquid Phase: Groundwater and Soil Moisture

Water in soil exists in two distinct situations:

**Groundwater (phreatic water)**: Free water in the pore spaces of saturated soil, in hydraulic connection with a water table. The water table is the surface below which all pore spaces are saturated. Water table depth varies with season (higher after rainy seasons, lower in drought), with proximity to rivers and the ocean, and with site topography.

**Capillary water**: Water drawn upward above the water table by capillary suction — the same force that draws water up through a paper towel. Fine-grained soils have smaller pores and higher capillary suction. Clay can maintain capillary moisture well above the water table — in some cases, 30 feet or more above it. This capillary zone is not fully saturated, but it has significant moisture that affects both strength and shrink-swell behavior.

**Vadose zone**: The unsaturated zone above the water table, including the capillary fringe. Moisture in the vadose zone fluctuates with rainfall, evaporation, plant uptake, and seasonal changes. This fluctuation drives the expansion and contraction of clay soils in an active zone near the surface.

The **active zone** (also called the zone of seasonal moisture change) is the depth within which soil moisture fluctuates significantly with seasons. Below the active zone, soil moisture is relatively stable year-round. Above it, soils wet and dry cyclically, driving shrink-swell movement.

The depth of the active zone varies dramatically by climate and soil type: 2 to 4 feet in humid coastal areas, 5 to 10 feet in the interior South, 10 to 15 feet in the arid Southwest, and over 20 feet in some Australian and Middle Eastern climates. In Dallas-Fort Worth, the active zone is typically 7 to 12 feet deep. Foundation design must account for the active zone depth.

### The Air Phase: Void Ratio and Porosity

Void ratio (e) is the ratio of void volume to solid volume. Porosity (n) is void volume divided by total volume. These are related mathematically (e = n/(1-n)) and they characterize how loosely or tightly the soil particles are packed.

High void ratio = loose packing = high compressibility = more settlement potential.

Dense, well-graded soils (GW, SW) have low void ratios and settle minimally under load. Soft, normally consolidated clays and loose sands have high void ratios and can settle substantially.

The degree of compaction of placed fill is measured by comparing its dry density (mass of solids per unit volume) to the maximum dry density achievable with a standard compaction effort in the laboratory (the Proctor test, ASTM D698 for Standard Proctor or ASTM D1557 for Modified Proctor). Specifications typically require fill to be compacted to at least 95% of maximum dry density — meaning the field density is at least 95% of what the laboratory says is achievable.

## Part 2: The Engineering Properties That Govern Foundation Design

From the basic science, we can now derive the specific engineering properties that a foundation designer needs to know.

### Shear Strength

The shear strength of soil is its ability to resist the sliding failure that occurs when a foundation overloads it. Soil shear strength has two components (the Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion):

**τ = c' + σ'n tan φ'**

Where:
- τ = shear strength (force per area)
- c' = effective cohesion (the component of strength that exists even with zero normal stress — primarily from clay mineralogy and cementation)
- σ'n = effective normal stress (the compressive stress on the failure plane, minus pore water pressure)
- φ' = effective angle of internal friction (the frictional component — analogous to the friction angle between sliding blocks)

For granular soils (sand and gravel), cohesion c' ≈ 0, and all strength comes from friction (φ' typically 30° to 45°). For clays, both c' and φ' contribute, but φ' is lower (15° to 30°) and c' can be significant in stiff clays.

The critical insight from this equation is the effective stress concept. Pore water pressure (u) reduces effective normal stress (σ'n = σn - u), which reduces shear strength. When soil becomes saturated and pore pressures rise — during heavy rain, flooding, or rapid loading — shear strength drops. This is why saturated soils fail more readily than dry ones, and why drainage is so critical for slope and foundation stability.

### Bearing Capacity

The bearing capacity of soil beneath a foundation is calculated using bearing capacity equations developed by Terzaghi (1943) and extended by Meyerhof, Brinch Hansen, Vesic, and others. The general form of Terzaghi's bearing capacity equation for a strip footing is:

**q_ult = c·Nc + q·Nq + 0.5·γ·B·Nγ**

Where:
- q_ult = ultimate bearing capacity (the load per unit area that causes shear failure)
- c = soil cohesion
- q = overburden pressure at the base of the footing (γ × depth of footing)
- γ = unit weight of soil
- B = width of the footing
- Nc, Nq, Nγ = dimensionless bearing capacity factors that depend on the friction angle φ

The **allowable bearing capacity** (what engineers actually use for design) is the ultimate bearing capacity divided by a factor of safety, typically 2.5 to 3.0 for foundations: q_allowable = q_ult / FS.

This equation tells us several important things:
1. Wider footings have higher ultimate bearing capacity (the Nγ term) — there is more soil mobilized to resist failure
2. Deeper footings have higher bearing capacity (the Nq term) — overburden pressure helps confine the soil and resist shear
3. Cohesive soils (clays) have bearing capacity even at zero depth (the Nc term)
4. Friction soils (sand and gravel) have no bearing capacity at zero depth if there is no overburden

This is why engineers specify both a minimum footing width and a minimum footing depth, and why footings generally perform better when they are deeper.

### Compressibility and Settlement

For calculating settlement, the key property is compressibility — specifically:

**For granular soils**: Settlement is usually rapid and relatively small. It can be estimated from SPT N-values or cone resistance using empirical methods. Most settlement in granular soils occurs during construction, before the building is occupied.

**For normally consolidated clay** (clay that has never been loaded beyond its current stress state): Settlement is calculated using the compression index Cc and the void ratio e₀:

**ΔH = (Cc / (1 + e₀)) × H × log(σ'final / σ'initial)**

Where ΔH is the settlement, H is the thickness of the compressible layer, and σ' values are the initial and final effective vertical stresses. The time to achieve that settlement is calculated separately using Terzaghi's consolidation theory, which involves the coefficient of consolidation cv. Settlement of normally consolidated clay is slow — it can take decades to centuries depending on layer thickness and drainage conditions.

**For overconsolidated clay** (clay that has been loaded more heavily in its geologic past than it is now — a common condition in glaciated regions): Settlement is much less, using the recompression index Cr (typically 1/5 to 1/10 of Cc), provided the new loading does not exceed the preconsolidation pressure.

**Preconsolidation pressure** (σ'p or Pc) is the maximum effective stress a clay has ever experienced. It is determined from laboratory consolidation tests (ASTM D2435). If the foundation pressure exceeds the preconsolidation pressure, the clay will consolidate significantly. Below it, compression is small and mostly recoverable.

### Permeability

Permeability (hydraulic conductivity, k) is the soil's ability to transmit water through its pore spaces. It ranges across 12 orders of magnitude:

- Clean gravel: k = 10⁻¹ to 10¹ cm/s
- Clean sand: k = 10⁻³ to 10⁻¹ cm/s
- Silty sand: k = 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻³ cm/s
- Silt: k = 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁵ cm/s
- Clay: k = 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻⁷ cm/s

This enormous range explains why drainage problems affect fine-grained soils so much more than coarse-grained ones. Sand drains quickly; clay barely drains at all. A perimeter drain installed next to a clay-walled basement will drain the gravel next to the pipe easily, but the clay farther away from the pipe will retain water for days or weeks after a rainstorm.

## Part 3: What Happens at a Building Site — From Raw Land to Foundation Ready

Before any geotechnical investigation happens, the site itself tells a story to the trained eye. Site reconnaissance — simply walking the site and looking carefully — can reveal a great deal about what lies below.

### Reading the Landscape

**Topography**: Low-lying areas collect water and may have poor drainage or high water tables. Slopes have different geologic conditions uphill and downhill. Flat terraces above streams are often old floodplain deposits — potentially deep soft soils.

**Vegetation**: Certain plants indicate wet soil (cattails, willows, certain sedges). Certain patterns of vegetation stress (trees with curved trunks — "pistol butt" trees — indicate soil creep on slopes). Absence of vegetation may indicate toxins or extremely dry, nutrient-poor soil.

**Existing structures**: Old buildings on the site have been sitting on the soil for years. Do they show signs of distress? Settlement, cracking, tilt? Their condition is free information about what the soil has been doing.

**Surface drainage**: Which way does water flow during rain? Are there areas of ponding? Are there signs of erosion — gullies, exposed roots, scoured channels?

**Fill areas**: Depressions, soft spots, irregular topography may indicate filled areas. Old aerial photographs (available through USGS, historical government archives, and services like Google Earth's historical imagery) can reveal what the site looked like decades ago — whether there was a pond, a low area that was filled, an old building, or other disturbance.

**Soil exposures**: Road cuts, stream banks, excavations on neighboring properties, or even a deep scratch in the surface can reveal soil stratigraphy — the layering pattern. Is the soil consistent to depth? Or does the character change dramatically a few feet down?

### Neighboring Information

Talk to neighbors. Long-term residents know which properties have water problems, which have had foundation work, which have cracked driveways or bowed basement walls. This is not scientific data, but it provides valuable context for where to focus investigation.

Check public records: permit databases often reveal whether the property or neighbors have had foundation repair work.

## Part 4: Geotechnical Investigation Methods — How We Actually Sample the Ground

A geotechnical investigation is the formal process of sampling the subsurface, testing what is found, and interpreting the results. The level of investigation appropriate for a given project depends on its scale, risk, and complexity.

### Level 1: Desktop Study

For very small or low-risk projects, a desktop study may be sufficient. This involves:

**USGS Geological Maps**: USGS maps geological units at 1:24,000 scale across the contiguous United States. A geologist can identify the bedrock geology and the typical Quaternary (surficial) deposit types from these maps. Available free at ngmdb.usgs.gov and the USGS National Map Viewer.

**NRCS Web Soil Survey**: The Natural Resources Conservation Service's Web Soil Survey (websoilsurvey.sc.egov.usda.gov) provides mapped soil series data for the entire United States. Each soil series has a description of its typical profile, drainage class, engineering properties, and limitations for construction. This is not a substitute for site-specific investigation, but it provides a starting point and a sanity check.

**State geological surveys**: Most states have geological surveys that publish maps, reports, and databases of boring logs from past projects. The Virginia Department of Mines, Minerals and Energy (DMME), the Texas Bureau of Economic Geology, and similar agencies maintain publicly accessible databases.

**FEMA Flood Maps**: FEMA's National Flood Hazard Layer (msc.fema.gov) shows flood zones that indicate areas of potential high water table and problem soils.

**Historical aerial photographs**: Google Earth's historical imagery, the USGS Earth Explorer (earthexplorer.usgs.gov), and state historical archives provide decades of aerial photographs that reveal site history.

### Level 2: Test Pits

For residential construction in straightforward conditions, hand-dug or machine-excavated test pits (trenches) are often the primary investigation method.

A test pit is typically 2 to 4 feet wide, 10 to 15 feet long, and excavated to the depth of interest (usually 5 to 8 feet for a shallow foundation, or deeper if soft or variable material is encountered). A person descends into the pit and:

1. Logs the soil profile: What soil types are present? At what depths? What is the color (dark color often indicates organic material; mottled gray-green indicates poor drainage)? What is the consistency (hard, stiff, firm, soft, loose)?

2. Identifies the depth to groundwater: Does water seep into the pit? At what depth?

3. Checks for fill: Does the material look natural (consistent color and structure, no debris) or does it look like it was placed (mixed colors, construction debris, inconsistent layers)?

4. Checks for soft zones: Pressing a thumb into the soil face gives a crude but useful indication of consistency. A very soft clay that takes a full thumb impression is around 500–1,000 psf unconfined compressive strength — marginal for direct bearing.

5. Collects samples: Bulk samples for moisture content and basic classification testing.

Test pits are cheap (a few hundred dollars to hire a small excavator for a day), but they have limitations: they cannot go very deep (10 to 15 feet is typically the limit of standard equipment), they cannot sample from below the water table, and they cover a limited area.

### Level 3: Soil Borings with SPT

For more complex or larger projects, or any project where the designer needs quantitative data beyond a few feet of depth, soil borings with Standard Penetration Testing (SPT) are the standard method.

**The boring**: A drilling rig advances a borehole into the ground using rotary drilling (washing out cuttings with water or drilling fluid) or hollow-stem auger drilling (the auger carries cuttings to the surface as it rotates). Boring rigs are typically truck- or track-mounted and can reach depths of 100 feet or more for typical residential investigations, and thousands of feet for major projects.

**The SPT**: At regular intervals (typically every 5 feet in the upper 20 feet, every 10 feet below), drilling stops and a 140-pound hammer is dropped 30 inches onto the drill rods. A split-spoon sampler at the bottom captures soil as it penetrates. The number of blows for the last 12 inches of a target 18-inch penetration is the SPT N-value.

SPT N-values are correlated to soil behavior through decades of empirical data:

For sand and gravel:
- N < 4: Very loose — essentially no bearing capacity, significant settlement
- N = 4–10: Loose — low bearing capacity, high settlement potential
- N = 10–30: Medium dense — moderate bearing capacity
- N = 30–50: Dense — good bearing capacity, low settlement
- N > 50: Very dense — excellent bearing capacity, effectively incompressible

For clay:
- N < 2: Very soft — less than 250 psf unconfined strength
- N = 2–4: Soft — 250–500 psf
- N = 4–8: Medium stiff — 500–1,000 psf
- N = 8–15: Stiff — 1,000–2,000 psf
- N = 15–30: Very stiff — 2,000–4,000 psf
- N > 30: Hard — greater than 4,000 psf

The SPT also produces a soil sample (the split-spoon sample) that can be visually classified and sent to a laboratory for further testing.

**Water table observation**: After drilling, or after allowing water levels to equalize (which can take 24 hours in clay), the depth to groundwater in the borehole is measured. In some cases, standpipe piezometers are installed in the boring to monitor long-term water table fluctuations.

### Level 4: Cone Penetration Test (CPT)

The Cone Penetration Test (CPT) has become increasingly popular, particularly for soft ground investigations, because it provides continuous, high-resolution data rather than point measurements at 5-foot intervals.

A steel cone with a base area of 10 cm² and a behind-the-cone friction sleeve is pushed into the ground at a constant rate of 2 cm/second by a hydraulic pushing system on a specialized truck. Two values are recorded continuously:

- **qc**: Cone tip resistance — how hard it is to push the cone through the soil
- **fs**: Sleeve friction — friction on the cylindrical sleeve behind the cone

The ratio fs/qc is the friction ratio, which is used to classify soil type. Low friction ratio → granular soil. High friction ratio → cohesive soil.

Modern CPT rigs also measure pore water pressure (piezocone or CPTU), which allows identification of clay layers, drainage conditions, and pore pressure dissipation rates.

CPT has several advantages over SPT:
- Continuous profile (no gaps between sampling points)
- Better precision in soft soils (SPT cannot accurately characterize very soft clay because the N-value is nearly always 0 or 1)
- No borehole required (less expensive per foot of penetration)
- Better repeatability (operator error is minimized)

Disadvantage: No physical sample is retrieved. CPT identifies and characterizes soil by inference from measured resistance, not by visual inspection of samples. For definitive classification, SPT borings or separate sampling boreholes are still needed.

### Level 5: Laboratory Testing

Samples retrieved from borings are tested in a geotechnical laboratory to determine engineering properties that cannot be measured in the field.

**Atterberg Limits (ASTM D4318)**: The Liquid Limit (LL) is the water content at which clay transitions from plastic to liquid behavior. The Plastic Limit (PL) is the water content below which clay becomes brittle. The Plasticity Index (PI = LL - PL) indicates the range of moisture content over which the clay is plastic and workable. High PI indicates expansive clay:
- PI < 10: Non-plastic to low plasticity — minimal swell
- PI = 10–20: Medium plasticity — moderate swell potential
- PI = 20–35: High plasticity — high swell potential
- PI > 35: Very high plasticity — very high swell potential; classic Dallas-type clay

**Grain size distribution (ASTM D422, D7928)**: The particle size distribution curve (gradation curve) classifies the soil and determines fines content.

**Moisture content (ASTM D2216)**: The natural water content of the soil compared to its Atterberg limits tells the engineer how close the clay is to its liquid limit (nearly liquid = very soft) or its plastic limit (stiff, dry).

**Unconfined compression test (UCT) (ASTM D2166)**: A cylindrical clay sample is loaded to failure in compression without lateral confinement. The ultimate load divided by sample area gives the unconfined compressive strength (qu). Undrained shear strength Su = qu/2. This directly measures what N-values estimate.

**Consolidation test (ASTM D2435)**: A thin clay disk is placed in a rigid ring and loaded in increments while drainage is allowed only from top and bottom. At each load increment, compression is measured over time until equilibrium. This yields the compression index Cc, the recompression index Cr, the preconsolidation pressure σ'p, and the coefficient of consolidation cv. These are the parameters needed to calculate settlement magnitude and rate.

**Swell test**: A sample is placed in the consolidation apparatus, loaded to simulate the foundation pressure, then allowed to absorb water. The amount of swell and the pressure required to prevent swell (swell pressure) characterize the expansive soil's behavior.

**Permeability test (ASTM D2434 for granular, D5084 for low-permeability soils)**: Measures hydraulic conductivity directly.

**Triaxial compression test (ASTM D2850, D4767)**: More sophisticated than UCT, measures shear strength parameters (c' and φ') under controlled drainage conditions. Used for slope stability analysis and more complex bearing capacity calculations.

## Part 5: Reading the Geotechnical Report

A geotechnical engineering report (or geotechnical investigation report, or soils report) synthesizes all the investigation data into recommendations for foundation design. If you are building or buying a property where one has been done, here is how to read it.

### Report Structure

A typical geotechnical report includes:

**1. Introduction**: Project description, authorization, purpose of the investigation.

**2. Site description and reconnaissance**: Summary of what was observed during the site visit.

**3. Subsurface exploration**: How many borings or test pits were done, to what depths, at what locations. Usually includes a boring location plan.

**4. Field and laboratory test results**: Tables of SPT N-values with depth, groundwater observations, laboratory test results. Often presented as boring logs — graphical representations of each boring showing soil type, N-value, and sample locations versus depth.

**5. Analysis and discussion**: The engineer's interpretation of the data. What is the stratigraphy? What are the engineering properties of each unit? What is the groundwater regime?

**6. Foundation recommendations**: The key section. The engineer recommends a foundation type (slab, crawl space, piers, etc.), minimum bearing depth, allowable bearing pressure, required footing dimensions, any special requirements (drainage, waterproofing, soil treatment), and pile or pier specifications if deep foundations are recommended.

**7. Earthwork recommendations**: Specifications for site grading, fill placement, and compaction.

**8. Limitations**: Every geotechnical report includes a statement of limitations — the investigation was limited to specific locations and depths; conditions elsewhere on the site may differ; the report is valid as of the investigation date and may not reflect future changes.

### Critical Numbers to Find

When reviewing a geotechnical report, look for:

**Allowable bearing pressure**: Expressed in psf or ksf (kips per square foot, where 1 ksf = 1,000 psf). Typical residential values range from 1,500 psf (marginal clay) to 4,000 psf (firm clay or dense sand) for shallow foundations. Higher values for deep foundations or rock.

**Depth to bearing stratum**: How deep the footing must extend to reach soil with the specified bearing capacity. This drives excavation cost.

**Depth to groundwater**: Critical for basement design, waterproofing requirements, and dewatering during construction.

**Frost depth requirement**: May or may not be in the geotech report (this is often from local code), but some reports address it.

**Swell potential**: If expansive soils are present, the report should characterize swell potential and swell pressure, and recommend mitigation measures.

**Settlement estimate**: The report should provide an estimate of expected settlement under the design loads, and indicate whether it will be uniform or potentially differential.

**Special conditions**: Slope stability concerns, liquefaction potential in sandy areas (this requires a specific analysis under ASCE 7 for higher seismic zones), presence of fill, proximity to karst features (sinkholes), corrosive soil (sulfates in the soil can attack concrete — the report should specify sulfate content and recommend appropriate cement type).

### What to Do When There Is No Geotechnical Report

For many existing homes and smaller projects, no formal geotechnical report was ever done, or it was done decades ago and is not available. What then?

1. **Use the NRCS Web Soil Survey**: It will tell you the mapped soil series and its engineering characteristics. This is county-level mapping, not site-specific, but it is far better than nothing.

2. **Look at the building record**: The original building permit file, if it can be retrieved from the local building department, may reference soil conditions or have the original engineer's notes.

3. **Inspect visible evidence**: How is the foundation performing? If a 30-year-old house has no cracks, level floors, and working doors, the foundation is probably adequate for continued use. If it shows distress, investigate.

4. **Commission a limited site investigation**: For $1,500 to $3,000, a geotechnical firm can typically do two or three soil borings or test pits on a residential lot, sufficient for basic foundation design on an infill project or evaluation of an existing foundation.

5. **Ask the neighbors**: Specifically, ask whether they have had foundation problems, what type of foundation their house has (if visible), and whether they know anything about historical site conditions.

## Part 6: Groundwater — The Foundation's Constant Adversary

Groundwater deserves its own section because its interaction with foundations is so multifaceted and consequential.

### The Water Table

The water table is the surface below which all soil voids are saturated. It is not a fixed plane — it fluctuates with seasons, rainfall, tidal influence (in coastal areas), and pumping (agricultural and municipal wells can lower water tables dramatically).

**Seasonal fluctuation**: In most of the eastern United States, the water table is highest in late winter and early spring (after winter rain and before summer evapotranspiration) and lowest in late summer and fall. The seasonal range can be a few inches to several feet.

**Tidal influence**: Within a few hundred feet of tidal water, the water table fluctuates with the tides — sometimes twice daily. This is highly relevant for coastal Virginia construction.

**Perched water tables**: Sometimes a layer of low-permeability material (a clay lens) sits above the regional water table, and water perched on top of it creates a local, temporary high water condition. Perched water tables are notoriously tricky — they may not show up during summer investigation and then appear dramatically in spring.

### Hydrostatic Pressure

Water exerts pressure proportional to depth: for every foot of water depth, the pressure is approximately 62.4 pounds per square foot (the unit weight of water). A basement wall with 8 feet of soil and water behind it experiences up to 8 × 62.4 = 499 psf of hydrostatic pressure at the base of the wall.

This pressure:
- Pushes horizontally against basement walls, requiring them to be designed as retaining walls with hydrostatic loading
- Pushes upward against basement slabs (uplift pressure), requiring the slab to be designed for this or the basement to be held down with anchors
- Can push upward with enough force to float an empty basement out of the ground (a scenario called buoyancy failure — if the upward water force exceeds the weight of the basement structure, the whole thing pops up)

Buoyancy failure is not rare in coastal areas and areas with high water tables. Lightweight basements (formed before the slab is placed, or before the structure above adds weight) are particularly vulnerable.

### Drainage as Foundation Protection

The single most cost-effective thing a homeowner can do to protect their foundation is ensure good site drainage:

1. **Gutters and downspouts**: Collect roof runoff and discharge it well away from the foundation (minimum 6 feet). A 2,000 sq ft house receiving 1 inch of rain produces about 1,245 gallons of runoff. Dumped against the foundation, that volume saturates the backfill and attacks the foundation wall. Directed away, it is harmless.

2. **Positive drainage away from the building**: The ground surface within 6 to 10 feet of the building should slope away at a minimum of 1/2 inch per foot (about 6 inches of drop in 10 feet). Over time, settling backfill and landscaping often reverse this slope.

3. **Window wells**: Below-grade windows require properly sized and drained window wells. A window well without a drain is a bathtub waiting to fill.

4. **Perimeter drains**: A perforated pipe at the base of the foundation footing, surrounded by gravel, collects water that reaches the footing level and directs it to daylight or a sump. These are standard for basements and are increasingly used for crawl spaces.

5. **Swales and French drains**: Site grading can direct surface water around the building. Interior yard drainage problems can be addressed with subsurface French drains (perforated pipe in gravel-filled trenches).

## Part 7: Site-Specific Conditions Across the United States

Soil conditions in the United States vary enormously by region, reflecting different geology, climate, and depositional history. Here is a regional summary of what builders and buyers typically encounter.

### The Northeast (Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and northern tiers)

The Northeast is dominated by glacial geology — the Wisconsin Glaciation (which ended about 12,000 years ago) left behind a complex patchwork of glacial materials:

**Glacial till**: A mixture of particles from clay to boulders, unsorted and deposited directly by the glacial ice. Till is generally dense and has reasonable bearing capacity (N-values of 15–50+ for typical New England till), but it is spatially variable and may contain buried boulders that complicate excavation.

**Glacial outwash**: Sandy and gravelly material deposited by meltwater streams. Excellent bearing material, often very well-graded and dense.

**Glaciolacustrine (lake) deposits**: Fine silt and clay deposited in glacial lakes. Highly compressible, variable, and sometimes structurally problematic. The varved clays of Lake Champlain and glacial Lake Hitchcock (now the Connecticut River Valley) are notoriously soft.

**Frost depth**: 4 feet in southern Connecticut, increasing to over 6 feet in northern Maine. All exterior footings must be below frost depth.

**Radon**: Glacial deposits in granite terrain can have elevated radon — not a foundation structural issue, but a consideration for basement design (sub-slab depressurization systems).

### The Mid-Atlantic (Maryland, Delaware, Virginia, North Carolina coastal plain)

The Mid-Atlantic coastal plain is underlain by thick sequences of marine sediments — alternating sand, silt, and clay deposited in ancient shallow seas over the last 70 million years. These sediments are found from the "fall line" (the inland limit of tidal rivers) eastward to the ocean.

**Chesapeake Formation**: In coastal Virginia, the Chesapeake Formation is a series of marine sands and clays. Some units contain artesian pressure — water under pressure that rises above the base of a boring when it is intersected. The Yorktown Formation, widely encountered in Hampton Roads, alternates sand and clay.

**Subsidence**: The Hampton Roads region (including Newport News) is experiencing land subsidence of 3 to 5 mm per year due to a combination of glacial isostatic adjustment (the land is still slowly rebounding from glacial unloading, but the rebound has been superseded by aquifer compaction) and sediment compaction. This subsidence affects long-term flood risk but not typically short-term foundation performance.

**Kaolinite clay**: The piedmont (west of the fall line) has deeply weathered residual soils derived from granite and gneiss — kaolinite-dominant clays with moderate to high plasticity but far less swell than smectite. Still require careful design.

**Frost depth**: 2 inches in southeastern Virginia coast, increasing to 8 inches inland to about 10 inches at the DC latitude. Very shallow by national standards — a significant advantage for foundation costs.

### The Southeast (South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Florida)

The Southeast's coastal plain has geology similar to the Mid-Atlantic — thick marine sediments — but with some regional peculiarities:

**Florida**: Underlain by thick limestone (Ocala Limestone, Suwannee Limestone), which is riddled with dissolution cavities from millions of years of freshwater dissolution. Sinkholes are common, unpredictable, and potentially catastrophic. Foundation design in Florida requires awareness of karst risk and, for higher-risk areas, special investigation and deep foundations.

**Gulf Coast**: River delta deposits — Mississippi River delta, Mobile-Tensaw delta — have extremely soft, deep clay and organic material. Buildings in New Orleans, Mobile, and the Mississippi Delta sit on foundations driven to depth through these materials.

**Frost depth**: Essentially zero (0 to 6 inches) throughout the deep Southeast. Slab-on-grade is the dominant foundation type.

### The Midwest (Ohio, Indiana, Michigan, Illinois, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa, Missouri, Kansas, Nebraska, the Dakotas)

The Midwest is a mix of glaciated terrain (similar to the Northeast in the northern tier) and unglaciated till plains and loess deposits.

**Loess**: Wind-deposited silt, found extensively in the Mississippi Valley, in Nebraska and Iowa. Loess can be surprisingly strong when dry (it has metastable structure maintained by suction) but can collapse dramatically when saturated. Loess is also highly frost-susceptible.

**Chicago clay**: The Chicago metropolitan area is underlain by glacial lake deposits — varved clay and silt left by ancient Lake Chicago. These are soft, compressible clays that have caused settlement of historic buildings and driven Chicago's tradition of deep foundation construction.

**Frost depth**: 3 feet in St. Louis, 4 feet in Chicago, 5 to 6 feet in Minneapolis and the Dakotas.

### The Southern Plains (Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas, and southern Colorado)

The Southern Plains is the heartland of the expansive soil problem. The Cretaceous marine shales that underlie much of Texas and Oklahoma are rich in smectite clay. They have been weathering for tens of millions of years, and their weathered residual soils — dark, heavy clays colloquially called "black cotton soil" or "gumbo" — are some of the most challenging foundation materials in the world.

We will devote an entire article to Dallas-Fort Worth specifically. But the general pattern throughout this region is:

- Slab-on-grade with post-tensioning is the overwhelming preference for residential construction
- Drilled piers to 10–15 feet or deeper are used when additional differential movement control is needed
- Maintaining consistent soil moisture is critical — irrigation, planting patterns, and drainage all matter
- Even well-designed foundations will move somewhat; the goal is to keep movement within acceptable limits

### The Desert Southwest (Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada, southern California)

The desert Southwest has its own peculiar soil challenges:

**Collapsible soils**: Desert soils that formed under low moisture conditions can have open, flocculated structure maintained by clay menisci. When first saturated (from irrigation, broken pipes, or ponding), this structure collapses and the soil undergoes sudden, large settlement — a process called hydrocompaction or soil collapse. This is a significant hazard for foundations in alluvial fan deposits in Arizona and Nevada.

**Caliche**: Cemented calcium carbonate layers common in the Southwest. Caliche can be extremely hard (difficult to excavate) but may have inconsistent thickness and can dissolve in acidic water.

**Expansive clay**: Despite the dry climate, the Southwest has expansive clay soils (the smectite in desert basin deposits), and the combination of deep desiccation during drought and saturation from monsoon rains creates dramatic moisture cycling.

**Frost depth**: Near zero in Phoenix and Las Vegas; 6 to 12 inches in Albuquerque (elevation 5,300 feet); 18 to 24 inches in parts of Colorado and northern New Mexico.

### The Pacific Northwest (Washington, Oregon, northern California)

The Pacific Northwest combines glacial geology in the north (similar to New England) with volcanic geology throughout (Mt. Rainier, Mt. Hood, Mt. St. Helens and their deep ash and lahar deposits) and some highly problematic soils:

**Seattle area**: Glacial till on the hills, soft lake and marine sediments in the lowlands. Liquefaction hazard along the Duwamish waterway and Lake Union. Strong seismic hazard (the Seattle fault and the Cascadia Subduction Zone).

**Expansive clay**: The Willamette Valley in Oregon has significant expansive clay in its black prairie soils.

**Coastal areas**: High rainfall and steep slopes mean landslide hazard is a significant consideration that intersects with foundation design.

## Part 8: When and How to Commission a Geotechnical Investigation

### When Is a Geotech Required?

Many jurisdictions require geotechnical reports for:
- Commercial construction of any size
- Multi-family construction
- Buildings in Special Flood Hazard Areas
- Buildings in mapped landslide hazard zones
- Construction on slopes exceeding a certain grade
- Custom home construction on unusual sites

Many jurisdictions do NOT require a geotechnical report for standard single-family residential construction using prescriptive IRC provisions. This does not mean it is not a good idea — it means the code is written to address the common, straightforward case and accepts some risk of failure for cost savings on simple projects.

Commissioning a geotech report voluntarily is strongly recommended when:

- You are building on a lot where the soil is unknown
- The lot has been filled, graded, or disturbed
- Neighboring properties show foundation distress
- The local soils are known to be problematic (expansive clay, soft organic deposits, karst)
- You are in a moderate to high seismic zone
- You are building an unusual or expensive structure
- You are building a structure with a basement in an area with a high water table

### How to Commission a Geotechnical Investigation

1. **Find a licensed geotechnical engineer**: Look for a PE (Professional Engineer) with geotechnical specialty, or a firm with GE (Geotechnical Engineer) licensure (available in some states). Professional organizations include ASCE's Geo-Institute and the Association of Geotechnical and Geoenvironmental Specialists (AGES). Word of mouth from structural engineers and contractors is often the best referral.

2. **Define the scope**: For a single-family residence on a standard lot with no known site complexities, a scope of two or three soil borings to 15 to 20 feet with SPT testing, Atterberg limits on clay samples, and a written report with foundation recommendations is typical. This typically costs $2,000 to $5,000 depending on region.

3. **Review the report with your structural engineer**: The geotechnical report provides soil data and general foundation recommendations. The structural engineer uses those recommendations to design specific foundation elements — thickness, reinforcement, dimensions.

4. **Communicate changes**: If conditions encountered during excavation differ from what the geotechnical report anticipated — different soil, unexpected water, buried debris — the geotechnical engineer should be notified immediately and the design adjusted.

### The Interaction Between Geotech, Structural, and Architect

In the best-case scenario, these three professionals work together from project inception:

- The **architect** designs the building program and envelope, establishing general size and layout
- The **geotechnical engineer** investigates the site and provides foundation type recommendations and soil parameters
- The **structural engineer** uses the architectural program and the geotechnical parameters to design the structural system, including foundation

In many residential projects, these roles are blurred or consolidated. Prescriptive IRC foundation design does not require a structural engineer. Geotechnical investigations are often skipped for standard lots. But when things go wrong — and they do — the absence of proper investigation and design is always part of the story.

## Part 9: Practical Checklist for Site Evaluation — What Every Buyer and Builder Should Know

Whether you are buying an existing home or breaking ground on new construction, these questions should be answered before proceeding:

**For existing homes:**
- Does the foundation show signs of distress? (Cracks, tilt, sticking doors, uneven floors)
- Has the foundation ever been repaired? (Ask the seller, check permit records, look for obvious repair signs like helical pier brackets or patched concrete)
- Is there water in the basement or crawl space? (Visit after rain; check for watermarks, efflorescence, mold)
- Does the drainage slope away from the building?
- Are gutters and downspouts functional and properly directed?
- What type of foundation is it? (Basement, crawl space, slab, piers)
- Has there been soil disturbance near the foundation (recent trenching for utilities, landscaping, pool installation)?
- Are there large trees within 20 to 30 feet of the foundation? (Their roots can extract moisture from clay and cause settlement)

**For new construction:**
- Has a geotechnical report been done? If so, what does it recommend?
- What are the local mapped soil types?
- Is the lot on fill? If so, what type, how old, and has it been compacted and tested?
- What is the frost depth requirement in this jurisdiction?
- What is the expected seasonal water table depth?
- Are there any special geological hazards (karst, liquefaction, expansive soil, landslide, coastal flooding)?
- What foundation type does the builder propose, and does it match the geotechnical recommendation?
- What are the drainage provisions?
- Will there be a structural engineer reviewing the foundation design?

## Conclusion: Soil Is Not a Background Detail

In the excitement of designing or buying a home, soil often feels like a background detail — something the contractor handles, something hidden beneath the concrete, something that just is. It is not. It is an engineering variable of the first importance, and the decisions made about it before the first shovel goes in the ground shape the performance of the building for its entire life.

The good news is that modern geotechnical science is extremely capable. We know how to measure soil properties. We know how to predict bearing capacity, settlement, and moisture behavior. We know how to design foundations that perform well in almost any soil condition. The tools are there. The challenge is simply making sure they are used.

Tomorrow, in Article 3, we begin the detailed exploration of the three most common residential foundation systems: the slab-on-grade, the crawl space, and the full basement. We will cover construction methods, advantages and disadvantages, cost comparisons, and code requirements for each.
