## Part 1: The Appliances Nobody Thinks About Until They Fail

There is a category of home maintenance that most people never perform — not because it is difficult, but because it requires thinking about what is happening behind, under, and inside appliances that appear to work fine. The dryer seems to be working. The washing machine completes cycles. The refrigerator keeps food cold. Why would you clean something that appears to work?

Because appearance and function are not the same. A dryer with a lint-packed vent duct is completing cycles and also building toward a fire. A washing machine with a mold-coated gasket is completing cycles and also depositing mold on your clothes. A refrigerator with coils buried in pet hair is keeping food cold while its compressor runs at 30% higher load than it should, silently shortening the appliance's life by years.

The tasks in this post are not glamorous. They do not produce visible improvement the way a freshly painted room does. But they prevent two of the most common causes of household fires (dryer fires and kitchen fires from neglected range hoods), extend the life of appliances that cost $800 to $3,000 each to replace, and prevent the mold and moisture problems that come from appliances that do not work correctly.

This post covers every major appliance in your home with a complete maintenance protocol for each.

## Part 2: The Dryer — Your Home's Biggest Hidden Fire Hazard

Clothes dryers are one of the leading causes of residential structure fires in the United States. The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) reports that dryers and washing machines caused an estimated 13,820 home fires per year during a recent reporting period, resulting in injuries, deaths, and hundreds of millions of dollars in property damage. Failure to clean was the leading contributing factor.

The mechanism is straightforward: lint — composed of cotton, polyester, and other fabric fibers — is extremely flammable. The lint trap catches much of it, but a significant fraction passes through the trap and accumulates in the exhaust duct, the duct joints, and around the blower and heating element inside the dryer. Given sufficient accumulation and a heat source, this lint ignites.

### The Lint Trap — After Every Load

Clean the lint screen after every single load. This is not optional and not subject to schedule — it is a habit built around every load of laundry.

Every 3 to 6 months, wash the lint screen with warm soapy water and a soft brush. Fabric softener and dryer sheet residue build up on the mesh and restrict airflow even when the screen looks lint-free. Hold the screen up to a light after washing — you should be able to see light through the mesh. If the mesh is coated, wash it again and let it dry completely before reinstalling.

### The Duct — Annually

The exhaust duct runs from the back of the dryer through the wall to the exterior. This duct accumulates lint regardless of how well you clean the lint trap — the trap does not capture 100% of the lint in every load.

**Annual duct cleaning procedure:**
1. Disconnect the dryer from the duct (typically by loosening a clamp on the flexible connector at the back of the machine)
2. Unplug the dryer (electric) or shut off the gas valve (gas)
3. From the laundry room, insert a dryer vent cleaning brush — a long, flexible brush on a series of extension rods — into the duct and work it back and forth
4. Vacuum up all dislodged lint with a shop vacuum
5. Go to the exterior exhaust vent, remove the cover, and brush and vacuum from that end as well
6. Reconnect the duct, ensuring no kinks or compression in the flexible connector
7. Replace the exterior vent cover and confirm the damper flap opens freely when the dryer runs

**Duct configuration matters enormously for fire risk:**
- The duct must be rigid metal or semi-rigid aluminum — not flexible plastic or foil accordion duct (these are fire hazards and code violations in new installations)
- The maximum total equivalent length (straight run plus deductions for elbows) varies by dryer manufacturer but is typically 25 feet of straight duct minus 5 feet per 90-degree elbow
- Every foot of duct beyond the maximum adds restriction and lint accumulation risk
- The exterior vent must be a proper dryer vent with a damper flap, not a screen (screens accumulate lint and block airflow)

### Inside the Dryer — Every 2–3 Years

Lint accumulates inside the dryer cabinet as well — behind the drum, around the heating element (electric) or burner assembly (gas), and in the blower housing. This interior lint is not accessible without disassembling the dryer and is the lint most likely to actually ignite.

Professional dryer cleaning — disassembly and cleaning of the interior — is available from appliance service companies for approximately $100 to $200 and is worth doing every 2 to 3 years for a heavily used dryer.

If you are mechanically inclined, dryers are relatively straightforward to disassemble. Search for your specific model number and "disassembly for cleaning" — most common dryer brands have YouTube tutorials. The process typically involves removing the front panel, the drum, and the lint chute to access and vacuum the interior.

### Gas Dryer — Additional Checks

For gas dryers, annually check the gas flex line connecting the dryer to the gas supply:
- The line should be a flexible stainless steel corrugated connector — not old rubber or copper tubing
- It should not be kinked, crimped, or pinched behind the dryer
- There should be no smell of gas (if you smell gas, shut off the gas valve and call your gas company)

## Part 3: The Washing Machine — Mold, Floods, and Laundry That Smells Wrong

The washing machine is arguably the highest flood risk appliance in the house. A ruptured washing machine supply hose can discharge 600 gallons per hour — enough to cause catastrophic structural damage in a very short time.

### Supply Hoses — Replace Every 5 Years

Standard rubber washing machine supply hoses should be replaced every 5 years regardless of condition. They degrade from the inside out; a hose that looks fine on the outside can fail catastrophically without warning. The $20 to $40 cost of new hoses is trivial insurance against a flood.

Better: replace them now with braided stainless steel hoses, which are far more resistant to failure. These cost $15 to $30 each (you need two — hot and cold) and should be replaced every 7 to 10 years.

Inspect the hoses every year: look for bulges, blistering, kinking, or corrosion at the fittings. Any of these signs means replace immediately.

**Consider a flood stop valve.** These devices attach to the supply connections and automatically shut off water flow if they detect a leak or if the washing machine door opens unexpectedly. Products like the Floodstop or similar devices cost $80 to $150 and can prevent a catastrophic flood from an unattended machine failure.

### The Washing Machine Drain

The washing machine drains through a pump into a standpipe or laundry tub. The standpipe should be 2 inches in diameter and 30 to 36 inches tall to prevent backup. If you notice the washing machine draining slowly or water backing up around the standpipe, the drain may be partially clogged — call a plumber to snake it.

### Front-Loading Washers — The Mold Problem

Front-loading washing machines are more energy and water efficient than top-loaders, but they have a well-documented design vulnerability: mold and mildew growth on the rubber door gasket (the large rubber seal around the opening of the drum) and inside the drum.

The cause: front-loaders seal so tightly that moisture cannot escape between uses. Detergent residue accumulates in the gasket folds. In warm, sealed conditions, this residue feeds mold and mildew, which produces the distinctive musty smell many front-loader owners know.

**Prevention:**
- After every wash cycle, wipe the door gasket thoroughly with a dry cloth, paying special attention to the folds at the bottom where water collects
- Leave the door ajar between uses to allow air circulation and drying
- Use HE (High Efficiency) detergent and use the correct amount — more detergent is not better, and excess detergent residue feeds mold
- Use the "Drum Clean" or "Tub Clean" cycle monthly (or run an empty hot cycle with no detergent and 2 cups of white vinegar in the drum)

**If mold is already present in the gasket:**
- Mix 1 tablespoon of bleach per quart of water
- Wearing gloves, wipe the gasket thoroughly with the bleach solution, working into all the folds
- Leave for 5 minutes, then wipe dry
- Run an empty hot cycle to rinse
- In severe cases, the gasket can be removed for deeper cleaning or replaced entirely (gasket replacement is an HVAC/appliance repair job, approximately $100 to $200 in parts and labor)

**Prevention for the drum itself:**
Monthly, run the Tub Clean cycle with an OxiClean or Affresh tablet (or with white vinegar) per the manufacturer's instructions. This is a maintenance cycle that sanitizes and deodorizes the drum.

### Top-Loading Washers — Balancing and Agitator Maintenance

Top-loading washers with a center agitator can develop off-balance loads that cause violent machine vibration during the spin cycle. If this is chronic:
- Check that the machine is level (there are adjustable feet — level the machine both front-to-back and side-to-side)
- Distribute laundry evenly around the agitator
- Do not overload — an overloaded top-loader is prone to imbalance

The agitator in a traditional top-loader contains agitator dogs (small plastic directional couplings) that allow the agitator to wiggle-wash. When these wear out, the agitator spins freely in both directions rather than oscillating. Replacement agitator dogs are available for most brands for $10 to $20 and the replacement is a DIY-friendly job.

High-efficiency top-loaders without an agitator have their own maintenance considerations — primarily the drum and gasket cleaning mentioned above for front-loaders, plus lint filter cleaning (HE top-loaders usually have a lint filter in the drum that should be cleaned periodically).

## Part 4: The Refrigerator — Coils, Drains, and Door Seals

The refrigerator runs 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, for 10 to 20 years. It is the one appliance in the house that is literally never off. Every incremental inefficiency — dirty coils, poor door seals, an ice maker with a slow leak — runs continuously.

### Condenser Coil Cleaning — Annually

The refrigerator removes heat from food by running a refrigerant cycle, and it has to dissipate that heat somewhere. The condenser coils — located either at the back of the unit (older models) or underneath behind the kick plate (most modern models) — are where that heat is rejected. As dust and pet hair accumulate on the coils, heat dissipation becomes less efficient, the compressor runs longer cycles, electricity consumption increases, and the compressor's lifespan shortens.

**Cleaning procedure:**
1. Unplug the refrigerator (important — do not work on the coils while powered)
2. For rear-mounted coils: pull the refrigerator away from the wall; use a vacuum with a brush attachment to clean the coils and the condenser fan behind the unit
3. For under-mounted coils: remove the kick plate at the bottom front; use a refrigerator coil brush (a long, flexible bristle brush, available for $10 to $15) to reach the coils, and vacuum up the debris
4. Plug the refrigerator back in
5. Push the refrigerator back (leave at least 1 to 2 inches of space at the back for air circulation)

Homes with pets should do this every 6 months rather than annually — pet hair accumulates on coils very rapidly.

### Door Gaskets — Inspect Annually

The door gaskets create the airtight seal that keeps cold in and warm air out. A failing gasket causes the refrigerator to run almost continuously, dramatically increasing electricity consumption and shortening compressor life.

**Test the seal:** Close the door on a piece of paper. If you can pull the paper out easily with the door closed, the gasket is not sealing properly at that point.

**Inspect visually:** Look for cracking, tearing, mold (especially in the fold of the gasket), or sections that have stiffened and lost flexibility.

**Cleaning the gasket:** Wipe the gasket monthly with a damp cloth. For mold, use a solution of baking soda and water. Do not use bleach on rubber gaskets — it dries them out and accelerates cracking.

**Replacement:** Replacement gaskets are available for most refrigerator models for $50 to $150 and are a DIY-friendly replacement — the gasket presses into a retaining channel in the door liner. Some models require unscrewing the door interior trim. If the gasket is failing, replace it rather than tolerating the inefficiency.

### The Defrost Drain — Clear It When It Clogs

Frost-free refrigerators have a defrost heater that periodically melts ice from the evaporator coil. The meltwater drains through a channel to a drain tube and into a drip pan under the refrigerator, where it evaporates.

This drain can become clogged with ice (if the drain freezes), mold, or debris. Signs of a clogged drain: water accumulating in the bottom of the refrigerator or freezer compartment, water puddles under the refrigerator, ice buildup on the floor of the freezer.

**To clear the drain:**
1. Locate the drain hole inside the refrigerator (usually at the back of the freezer compartment, sometimes under a cover panel)
2. Use a turkey baster filled with warm water to flush the drain — repeat until clear
3. Use a pipe cleaner or flexible drain brush to physically clear any blockage
4. Check that the drain tube at the bottom of the refrigerator is clear and the drip pan is in place

### The Refrigerator Water Supply Line

If your refrigerator has an ice maker or water dispenser, it connects to your home's water supply via a small supply line. This line is usually either copper tubing, braided stainless steel, or plastic.

**Check the connection at the wall shutoff valve annually** — look for drips, corrosion, or mineral buildup. If you have plastic supply line, replace it with braided stainless steel. A plastic supply line that fails — and they do — can deliver a significant water leak.

**If the ice maker is not used:** turn it off and turn off the supply valve. An unused ice maker with water supply connected can develop slow leaks that go unnoticed.

### Behind the Refrigerator

Pull the refrigerator out from the wall once a year when you do the coil cleaning. While you have it out, look at the wall and floor:
- Any water stains on the wall? (Indicates a supply line leak)
- Any floor discoloration, soft spots, or mold under the refrigerator? (Indicates a defrost drain problem or supply line issue)
- Any debris or rodent evidence? (Refrigerators create warmth and a dark, protected space — attractive to mice)

Also check the refrigerator's leveling: refrigerators should be slightly tilted back (front feet slightly higher than rear) so the doors close automatically. Level side-to-side. Adjust the feet as needed.

## Part 5: The Dishwasher — Filters, Sprayer Arms, and Door Seals

Dishwashers are one of the most frequently neglected appliances in the kitchen. Most homeowners run them until they stop cleaning well and then call an appliance repair person, when monthly maintenance would have prevented the problem entirely.

### The Filter — Monthly Cleaning

Modern dishwashers (most models made in the last 10 to 15 years) have a manual-clean filter rather than the self-cleaning (grinding) mechanism of older dishwashers. The filter — a cylindrical screen assembly at the bottom of the tub — traps food particles and prevents them from recirculating and redepositing on dishes.

When this filter is not cleaned, it restricts water flow, reduces cleaning performance, and produces odors (the accumulated food particles decompose). Many homeowners who think their dishwasher is failing simply have a filter that has never been cleaned.

**To clean the filter:**
1. Remove the bottom rack
2. Twist and lift the cylindrical filter assembly (the mechanism varies by brand — check your manual)
3. Rinse under warm running water
4. Use a soft brush (an old toothbrush works well) to scrub any buildup
5. Replace the filter assembly, ensuring it clicks into position

Do this monthly. It takes three minutes.

### Sprayer Arms — Inspect Quarterly

The rotating sprayer arms have small holes (jets) through which water is sprayed onto the dishes. These holes can become clogged with hard water mineral deposits, food particles, or detergent residue.

**To inspect and clean:**
1. Remove the lower sprayer arm by unscrewing the nut (or pressing the release clip) at the center
2. Hold it up to the light and look through each jet hole — they should all be clear
3. Use a toothpick or thin wire to clear any clogged holes
4. Rinse thoroughly and reinstall
5. Repeat for the upper sprayer arm

### Door Seal and Door Interior

The door seal collects food residue, detergent buildup, and moisture that feeds mold. Wipe it monthly with a damp cloth. Check for cracking or tearing — a failing door seal causes leaks.

The door interior itself — the inside surface of the door — is often coated in residue that builds up over time. Wipe it monthly as well.

### Monthly Run with Dishwasher Cleaner

Once a month, run the empty dishwasher on the hottest cycle with a dishwasher cleaner tablet (Affresh, Finish, or similar). This removes buildup from the interior surfaces, the pump, and the spray arms. Alternatively, place a cup of white vinegar in the top rack and run the hot cycle.

### Hard Water Management

If you have hard water, mineral deposits — primarily calcium and magnesium carbonates — will accumulate on dishwasher surfaces, spray arms, and heating elements. This is visible as white or grey film. A monthly acidic cleaning (vinegar, citric acid, or commercial dishwasher cleaner) prevents excessive buildup. In areas with very hard water, a whole-house water softener or an inline water softener for the dishwasher significantly extends appliance life.

## Part 6: The Range and Oven — Burners, Grease, and Hood Maintenance

The cooking area is the highest fire-risk zone in most homes. Cooking is the leading cause of home fires in the United States, according to the NFPA. Understanding and maintaining the equipment involved is essential.

### Gas Burner Maintenance

Gas burner ports — the small holes through which gas flows and burns — can become clogged with boil-over food residue. Clogged ports cause the burner to burn unevenly, with some ports not igniting, producing yellow flames (incomplete combustion) rather than blue.

**To clean gas burners:**
1. Remove the grates and burner caps
2. Soak the grates and caps in hot soapy water for 30 minutes
3. Use a toothpick or thin wire to clear any clogged ports (the small holes around the burner head)
4. Do NOT use water to clean the burner igniter — the ceramic igniter is delicate and moisture can cause intermittent ignition problems. Clean it with a dry cloth or very lightly with a soft toothbrush.
5. Reassemble when dry

The igniter clicks when you turn a burner on. If a burner won't ignite despite the clicking, the igniter or the burner cap alignment is the likely cause. Check that the burner cap is seated correctly (slightly off-center caps prevent ignition). If the cap is seated and the burner still won't ignite, the igniter itself may need replacement.

### Electric and Induction Burner Maintenance

Electric coil burners: remove them and clean underneath the drip pans regularly. The drip pans (the reflective metal bowls under each coil) should be washed with hot soapy water. Replace them when they are permanently stained — they are inexpensive and a clean drip pan reflects heat better.

Smooth-top electric or induction ranges: clean spills immediately while the surface is warm (not hot). Once food burns onto a glass-ceramic surface, removal requires a specialty cooktop cleaner (Cerama Bryte, Weiman, or similar) and a razor scraper. Never use abrasive pads or cleaners — they scratch the surface permanently.

### Oven Maintenance

**Self-cleaning cycles:** Most modern ovens have a self-cleaning function that heats the oven to approximately 900°F and turns food residue to ash. Run this cycle when there is significant buildup — not more frequently than necessary, as the high temperatures stress oven components (control boards, door hinges, and oven elements have all been reported to fail during or after self-cleaning cycles). Remove the oven racks before running the cycle (the high heat can discolor and warp them). After the cycle, wipe out the ash residue with a damp cloth.

**Steam clean:** Some newer ovens offer a steam clean option (lower temperature, uses water) that is gentler on the oven components. It does not clean as thoroughly as the high-temperature self-clean, but it is appropriate for light buildup.

**Manual cleaning:** For spot cleaning, a paste of baking soda and water applied to the interior surfaces and left for several hours dissolves most grease buildup without harsh chemicals. Wipe clean and rinse.

**Door seal:** The oven door gasket (the braided fiberglass seal around the oven door opening) should be inspected annually. A damaged or compressed gasket allows heat to escape, affecting cooking performance and increasing energy consumption. Replacement gaskets are available for most oven brands.

### The Range Hood — Your Most Neglected Kitchen Item

The range hood is, for many homeowners, the most neglected maintenance item in the kitchen. And yet it is one of the most fire-critical items in the house.

The range hood removes grease-laden air from over the cooking surface and either recirculates it through filters (recirculating hood) or exhausts it outside through a duct (vented hood). In both cases, the filters — which trap grease — require regular cleaning.

**Grease filters — Monthly cleaning:**

Most range hoods use aluminum mesh grease filters that can be washed and reused. These filters are saturated with grease and cooking residue and should be cleaned monthly.

1. Remove the filters (they usually slide or click out)
2. Place them in the dishwasher (if they fit) and run the hottest cycle — this is the most effective cleaning method
3. Alternatively, soak in very hot water with a degreasing dish soap (Dawn original works well) for 30 minutes, then scrub with a stiff brush
4. Rinse thoroughly and dry before reinstalling

Grease filters that have turned brown or cannot be cleaned should be replaced. Replacement filters are inexpensive ($10 to $30).

**Carbon filters (recirculating hoods only) — Every 3–6 months:**

Recirculating hoods that do not exhaust outside use activated carbon filters to remove odors. These cannot be washed — they must be replaced every 3 to 6 months depending on use. (Vented hoods that exhaust outdoors do not use carbon filters.)

**The duct and exterior vent:**

For vented range hoods, the duct that connects the hood to the exterior accumulates grease over time. This grease is flammable — a range hood duct fire can spread to the wall cavity and attic.

The exterior vent cover should be cleaned annually: remove the cover, clean the grease buildup from the damper and surrounding surfaces, ensure the damper opens and closes freely.

The duct itself, if accessible, should be cleaned every 2 to 3 years for heavy cooks, more often for commercial-style high-BTU ranges. This may require a professional if the duct is not easily accessed — some professional kitchen exhaust cleaning services do residential work.

**The hood interior and light fixtures:**

Wipe the interior surfaces of the hood monthly with a degreaser. Do not let grease accumulate on the inside surfaces — it is a fire hazard.

## Part 7: The Garbage Disposal — Care, Feeding, and Odor Prevention

### What Garbage Disposals Can and Cannot Handle

**Can handle:** Most soft food scraps, small fruit pits, small bones (chicken, fish), cooked food scraps, ice (for sharpening/cleaning).

**Cannot handle:** Fibrous materials (celery, artichokes, asparagus — these wrap around the impellers), starchy foods (potato peels, pasta, rice — swell and create paste that clogs), large bones, nuts, shells, fruit pits (large ones), grease or oil (solidifies in the drain lines), eggshells (the membrane can wrap around the impellers), coffee grounds (accumulate in the trap).

### Monthly Maintenance

**Cleaning the disposal:** The interior of the disposal and the rubber splash guard accumulate food residue and bacteria that produce odors. Monthly:
1. With the disposal off, reach up under the splash guard and scrub the underside with an old toothbrush and dish soap — this is where most odors originate
2. Put a cup of ice and a half cup of rock salt in the disposal and run it with cold water — the ice and salt scour the inside of the grinding chamber
3. Cut a lemon into wedges, feed them in while running with cold water — the citric acid deodorizes

**Baking soda and vinegar:** Pour a half cup of baking soda followed by a cup of white vinegar into the disposal, let it fizz for 5 minutes, then flush with hot water. This is effective for deodorizing.

### Clearing a Jammed Disposal

A disposal that hums but does not grind is typically jammed — an obstruction is preventing the impellers from rotating. Do not leave the motor running when jammed — it will burn out.

1. Press the reset button on the bottom of the disposal unit (there should be a small red or black button — press until it clicks)
2. Use the hex key (usually a 1/4-inch Allen key) provided with the disposal and insert it into the hex socket at the center of the bottom of the unit. Turn it back and forth to work the jammed impellers free.
3. Use a long wooden spoon (never your hand) to reach down through the drain opening and dislodge any obstruction
4. Run water and try the disposal again

If the disposal trips the reset button repeatedly, the unit may be overloaded or failing. A disposal typically lasts 10 to 15 years.

## Part 8: The HVAC System's Hidden Cleaning Jobs

The HVAC system has maintenance components that live between full professional service visits and deserve attention on their own.

### The Return Air Grilles and Supply Registers

Dust accumulates on return air grilles and supply registers. This dust narrows the opening, restricts airflow, and gets drawn into the system when the HVAC runs. Clean them with a vacuum and a damp cloth every few months.

Remove the return air grilles occasionally and look into the return duct: a thick coating of dust on the walls of the duct near the grille indicates either a severely overloaded filter history or bypassing around the filter. The duct itself should be professionally cleaned if there is visible heavy dust contamination.

### The Condensate Drain Pan and Line

As discussed in Post 3, the air conditioner's evaporator coil drips condensation into a drain pan that empties through a drain line. This drain line can grow algae and become clogged, causing the pan to overflow.

Monthly during the cooling season, pour a cup of diluted bleach or white vinegar into the drain line clean-out (if accessible — a capped pipe near the air handler). This inhibits algae growth and keeps the drain clear.

### Humidifiers — If Your HVAC Has One

Whole-house humidifiers connected to the HVAC system are common in dry climates and in cold climates where winter heating air is very dry. They require specific maintenance:

- Replace the humidifier pad (also called a water panel or evaporator pad) annually at the beginning of the heating season. The pad becomes coated with mineral scale that prevents proper evaporation and can harbor bacteria.
- At the end of the heating season, close the humidistat to the summer setting (most whole-house humidifiers should be turned off in summer), shut off the water supply to the humidifier, and drain any standing water.
- Inspect the humidifier water supply tubing annually for mineral buildup or kinks.

## Part 9: The Water Softener — If You Have One

Water softeners use ion exchange to remove calcium and magnesium ions from hard water, replacing them with sodium ions. They require periodic attention:

**Salt replenishment:** Check the brine tank every 1 to 2 months and refill with water softener salt when the level falls below half. Use pellet or crystal salt as specified by your system — do not use rock salt.

**Salt bridges:** Occasionally, the salt in the brine tank forms a hard crust (salt bridge) above a void, making it appear that salt is present when the actual brine level is low. Break up the bridge with a broom handle and stir the salt.

**The resin bed:** The ion exchange resin in the softener tank eventually becomes fouled with iron, manganese, or other contaminants. A resin cleaner additive (Iron Out or similar) run through the system periodically can restore capacity. The resin itself has a lifespan of 10 to 20 years.

**The settings:** The regeneration cycle (backwash and brine rinse that recharges the resin) should be set appropriately for your water hardness and household usage. Most modern softeners have demand-initiated regeneration that regenerates only when needed — if yours has a timed regeneration, verify the settings are appropriate.

## Part 10: The Master Appliance Maintenance Schedule

Use this summary to build your appliance maintenance habits:

### After Every Load
- Dryer: clean the lint screen

### Monthly
- Washing machine (front-loader): wipe door gasket; leave door open between uses
- Dishwasher: clean filter; wipe door seal
- Garbage disposal: clean splash guard and grinding chamber; run ice and citrus
- Range hood grease filters: wash or inspect
- Refrigerator: wipe door gaskets; check under sink for any supply line drips

### Every 3 Months
- Dryer vent lint trap: wash the lint screen with soap and water to remove coating
- Dishwasher: run cleaning cycle with descaling agent or vinegar
- Range hood carbon filter (recirculating only): replace or check
- Water softener brine tank: check and replenish salt

### Every 6 Months
- Washing machine: run drum clean or sanitize cycle
- Water heater: check anode rod condition (every 2–3 years is minimum)
- HVAC condensate drain: treat with bleach or vinegar solution

### Annually
- Dryer vent duct: full cleaning (brush and vacuum from both ends)
- Refrigerator condenser coils: vacuum clean
- Refrigerator door gasket: inspect and test seal
- Dishwasher sprayer arms: clear jet holes
- Range/oven: deep clean; inspect door seal
- Range hood duct (vented): inspect and clean if accessible
- Gas range burner ports: clear with toothpick; clean igniter housings
- Water softener: add resin cleaner; inspect settings
- HVAC humidifier: replace pad; inspect and clean

### Every 2–3 Years
- Dryer interior: professional disassembly and interior lint cleaning
- Washing machine supply hoses: inspect and replace if rubber; replace braided stainless at 7–10 years
- Refrigerator water supply line: inspect; replace plastic with braided stainless if not already done
