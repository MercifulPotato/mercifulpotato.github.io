# The Roots of the Tree: Tolkien's Real-World Sources, Influences, and the Life That Made the Mythology

*This is Part Ten — the final article — of the comprehensive history of Middle-earth. The previous nine articles told the story from inside: the in-universe chronological history, the unresolved questions, the language systems. This final article tells the story from outside: what Tolkien was drawing on, what he was working through, what he was arguing against, and how the specific circumstances of his life shaped the mythology he spent sixty years building.*

*Understanding the sources does not diminish the secondary world. It deepens it. Knowing that the Rohirrim are Anglo-Saxons tells you something about what Tolkien found worth preserving in that culture. Knowing that Gandalf is partly Odin does not make Gandalf less Gandalf; it tells you what Tolkien was doing when he built Gandalf. The roots of the Tree and the Tree itself are one thing.*

---

## Part 1: The Man Before the Mythology — Tolkien's Early Life

John Ronald Reuel Tolkien was born on January 3, 1892, in Bloemfontein, in what was then the Orange Free State of South Africa, where his father Arthur Tolkien had taken a position with Lloyd's Bank. Arthur Tolkien died of rheumatic fever in 1896, when Ronald was four years old. His mother Mabel brought Ronald and his younger brother Hilary back to England, settling eventually in Sarehole, a small village then on the rural edge of Birmingham.

Sarehole matters. The years between four and eight that Tolkien spent there — in a genuinely rural English setting, with a mill and a pond and open fields and an awareness of the edge of the village where the countryside began — gave him something he would seek to preserve for the rest of his life. It gave him the Shire. Not metaphorically: the Shire is Sarehole, scaled up and made Hobbitish, preserving the specific quality of that particular kind of English village life — its smallness, its self-sufficiency, its complete irrelevance to the affairs of the wider world and its complete rightness in its own terms.

Mabel Tolkien converted to Roman Catholicism in 1900, a decision that cost her the financial support of her Baptist family. In 1904, she died of diabetes. Ronald was twelve. She had been his primary teacher, had taught him Latin and Greek and French and had begun teaching him Old English and Welsh. Her death left him and Hilary as orphans in the care of Father Francis Morgan, a Catholic priest who became their legal guardian and genuine parental substitute.

The loss of his mother — and the connection Tolkien drew between her death and her conversion to Catholicism, which he believed his family had punished her for by withdrawing support — shaped his faith. He was a Catholic for the rest of his life, but a Catholic marked by awareness of what faith costs. His Catholicism was not comfortable or triumphalist; it was the faith of someone who had seen what belief can cost a person who holds it genuinely.

## Part 2: Edith — Lúthien

Tolkien fell in love with Edith Bratt when he was sixteen and she was nineteen. They were both lodgers in the same Birmingham boarding house, both orphans, both somewhat adrift. Father Francis Morgan discovered the relationship and forbade Tolkien from any contact with Edith until he was twenty-one — a three-year prohibition that Tolkien honoured scrupulously, writing no letters, making no contact.

When he turned twenty-one, the first thing he did was write to Edith. She had become engaged to someone else in the interim, but she broke the engagement and returned to Tolkien. They married in 1916, just before Tolkien left for France.

In 1917, convalescing from trench fever in a cottage in Yorkshire, Tolkien wrote the first version of the Tale of Tinúviel — the story that would become Beren and Lúthien. He had watched Edith dance among the hemlock flowers in a woodland glade, and the image became the founding image of the most important love story in his mythology. He wrote to his son Christopher near the end of his life:

> "I never called Edith Lúthien — but she was the source of the story that in its final form is *The Tale of Beren and Lúthien*. She was beautiful, petite and dark-haired."

He instructed that her gravestone should bear the name Lúthien. His own bears Beren. They lie in Wolvercote Cemetery in Oxford.

The emotional weight of Beren and Lúthien in the mythology — the depth of feeling, the genuineness of the love, the specific anguish of mortality as a theme — comes from here. Tolkien was a mortal man deeply in love with a woman who had danced among the flowers, and he knew he would lose her, and he made a story about it. The story was a way of holding what could not be held. That is what Lúthien's song in the halls of Mandos is about: holding on by the power of the thing itself, the music, the love expressed in its fullest form, when nothing else is left.

## Part 3: The First World War — The Cataclysm and Its Mark

Tolkien was commissioned as a second lieutenant in the Lancashire Fusiliers and went to France in 1916. He was present at the Battle of the Somme, which began on July 1, 1916, and was one of the most catastrophic single days in British military history: approximately 57,470 British casualties on the first day alone, including nearly 20,000 killed. By the time the Somme offensive ended in November 1916, there had been roughly 420,000 British casualties.

Tolkien contracted trench fever — a louse-borne bacterial infection common in the trenches — in November 1916 and was sent back to England to recover. He spent much of the rest of the war in and out of hospital, which likely saved his life. By the war's end, almost all of his close friends from his Oxford days — a group called the T.C.B.S. (Tea Club and Barrovian Society), four young men who had literary ambitions and great mutual affection — were dead. Rob Gilson was killed on July 1, 1916. G.B. Smith died of wounds in December 1916. Christopher Wiseman survived. Tolkien survived.

The effect of this on the mythology is direct and documented. Tolkien told an interviewer late in life that the Lord of the Rings was "a fundamentally religious and Catholic work." But it was also, at a deeper level, a work about the specific kind of loss that the First World War inflicted: the loss of a whole generation of young men of promise, the waste of it, the sense that greatness had been cut off before it could achieve what it might have.

The Dead Marshes in The Two Towers — the bog through which Frodo and Sam and Gollum pass, where the faces of the dead can be seen under the water, the bodies of Elves and Men and Orcs from the Battle of Dagorlad — are the trenches. The specific visual of dead faces preserved under water, lit by ghost-lights, too close to touch and utterly unreachable — this is the image of soldiers in no-man's-land, of the dead that could not be retrieved, of the landscape of the Somme where the ground swallowed what was left.

The fellowship of the Fellowship of the Ring — the specific intimacy of a small group of different people bound together by a shared ordeal and a shared purpose — is the fellowship of the trenches, where men from entirely different social backgrounds became brothers because they were going through the same thing and only each other could fully understand it. The T.C.B.S. became the Fellowship. The loss of Rob Gilson and G.B. Smith became the losses of the War of the Ring.

Tolkien was not writing about the war. He rejected that reading explicitly. He was writing from within the experience of someone who had survived a catastrophe that most of his contemporaries had not survived, and the mythology was partly the work of processing that survival.

## Part 4: Beowulf and the Northern Heroic Tradition

Tolkien's 1936 lecture "Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics" is one of the most important works of literary criticism in the twentieth century. Before it, the Old English poem Beowulf was studied primarily as a historical document — as evidence of Anglo-Saxon culture and language — and the monsters were regarded as embarrassing, primitive, mythological intrusions into what would otherwise be a more serious text. Tolkien argued that this had it backwards: the monsters were the point. Beowulf was a meditation on heroism in the face of inevitable defeat, and the monsters were the form that inevitable defeat took.

His reading of Beowulf's structure — the old king dying in his last battle against the dragon, knowing he would die, going anyway — informs the shape of the Northern heroic tradition as Tolkien understood it. This is not the heroism of the classical tradition, where the hero triumphs and is rewarded. This is the heroism of a tradition where the hero knows doom is coming and faces it with courage anyway, not because it will help but because the alternative — giving in, running, failing to face it — is not bearable.

This is Fingolfin at the gates of Angband. This is Théoden riding to the Pelennor Fields at seventy years old, knowing he will not return. This is Boromir dying on the hillside, holding off the Orcs to buy time for Hobbits he has just tried to rob of their Ring. The Northern tradition says: you are going to lose, and you go anyway, and the going is the heroic act.

The specific Norse mythology that Tolkien drew on most directly:

**The Völsung Saga and the Nibelungenlied:** The story of Sigurd/Siegfried — the dragon-slayer, the man of great gifts who was nonetheless destroyed by fate and treachery — is the direct model for Túrin Turambar. The cursed gold (in the Norse myth, Andvari's gold; in Tolkien, the Silmarils and then the Nauglamír), the doomed hero, the incest tragedy (in the Norse tradition, Sigurd's wife Gudrun's brother Gunnar marries the hero's lover Brynhildr; in Tolkien, Túrin unknowingly marries his own sister) — these structural parallels are direct borrowings. Tolkien translated portions of the Völsung Saga and wrote his own alliterative version of it (the Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún, published posthumously in 2009). He knew the material with scholarly intimacy.

**The Edda and Norse Cosmology:** The Nine Worlds, the World-Tree Yggdrasil, the Twilight of the Gods (Ragnarök) — these are the structural precedents for Tolkien's Ages of the World and for the concept of the Dagor Dagorath. The One Ring owes something to the Eddic cursed rings. Odin — the wandering god, one-eyed, disguised, going among men in a grey cloak and wide-brimmed hat, imparting wisdom and stirring up heroism where he found it — is the direct model for Gandalf. Tolkien acknowledged this and also transformed it: Gandalf is not morally ambiguous in the way Odin is (Odin is a trickster, ultimately focused on his own ends); Gandalf serves something genuinely larger than himself.

**Fenrir the Wolf:** Glaurung, Ancalagon, and the dragon tradition in Tolkien draw on the Norse monster tradition — Níðhöggr gnawing the roots of Yggdrasil, Jörmungandr the World Serpent, Fenrir the Wolf who will swallow Odin at Ragnarök. Tolkien's dragons are not the treasure-hoarding beasts of fairy tale but weapons of cosmic significance, beings whose deaths shake the world.

**The Dwarves of the Eddas:** As noted in the language article, Tolkien's Dwarf-names come directly from the Dvergatal of the Völuspá. The Norse dwarves are smiths and makers of divine weapons (Gungnir, Mjölnir, Gleipnir). Tolkien's Dwarves inherit this tradition while adding his own moral and theological dimension.

## Part 5: The Finnish Kalevala — The Other Foundation

If the Norse tradition provided the heroic tradition, the Finnish Kalevala provided the love story tradition — and the sound of Quenya.

The Kalevala is the Finnish national epic, compiled by Elias Lönnrot from Finnish oral tradition and published in its final form in 1849. It tells the story of the hero Väinämöinen and his companions through a series of rune-songs — poems in a trochaic tetrameter that gives Finnish epic its distinctive rhythm. Key stories in the Kalevala:

**Kullervo:** The most direct source for Túrin Turambar. Kullervo is a man cursed from birth, who grows up as a slave, who accidentally causes his sister's death through an incestuous encounter (he seduces her without knowing who she is; when she discovers the truth, she throws herself into rapids), and who then kills himself with his own sword. The sword speaks to him before he dies. Tolkien translated portions of the Kullervo story into English in 1914-15 (published posthumously as The Story of Kullervo in 2015). He described Kullervo as the inspiration for Túrin and acknowledged the parallel directly.

**The Sampo:** A magical artifact of great power that everyone wants and that causes catastrophe through the wanting of it. The Silmarils owe something to the Sampo. More broadly, the pattern of the created object that is both enormously valuable and enormously destructive — that becomes the focus of obsessive possession and conflict — runs through both the Kalevala and Tolkien's mythology.

**The sound of Finnish:** Tolkien encountered Finnish as a student reading the Kalevala in the original, and the aesthetic experience of the language — its open syllables, its long pure vowels, its musical quality — was immediate and overwhelming. He described it in letters as being like discovering a wine of a kind he had not known existed. He began learning Finnish and modelled Quenya on it. The Quenya *Namárië* poem reads with a Kalevala-adjacent rhythm.

The Kalevala is also, like Tolkien's mythology, a mythology for a people who felt they lacked one — Lönnrot compiled the Finnish oral tradition specifically to give Finland a national epic comparable to those of other European nations. Tolkien's stated purpose in creating the mythology — to give England a mythology, to fill the gap left by the Norman Conquest that had destroyed so much of the native English mythological tradition — parallels Lönnrot's project exactly. Both men were making something that felt found rather than invented, recovering rather than constructing.

## Part 6: Medieval Literature — Sir Gawain, Pearl, and the Alliterative Tradition

Tolkien's professional speciality was medieval English literature, and his work on specific medieval texts left specific marks on the mythology.

**Sir Gawain and the Green Knight (14th century):** The great Arthurian poem that Tolkien edited and translated. Its themes — the testing of a knight's courage and integrity, the covenant that must be kept even when keeping it seems suicidal, the Green Knight who is not what he appears — resonate throughout Tolkien's mythology. The Ents have something of the Green Knight's unsettling not-quite-humanness. The theme of the kept covenant runs through the Dead Men of Dunharrow (oath-breakers who must fulfil their oath) and through Tolkien's treatment of promise-keeping generally.

**Pearl (14th century):** An elegy in the alliterative tradition, in which a father grieves for his dead child and receives a vision of her in a Christian paradise. The poem's treatment of grief, its acceptance of loss through spiritual trust, its vision of beauty in the midst of mourning — these are themes Tolkien returned to throughout his life. The *Leaf by Niggle* short story (discussed below) handles the same material in a different register.

**Beowulf:** Already discussed, but its specific formal features shaped Tolkien's prose. The kennings of Old English poetry — compound words that carry metaphors within them, like "whale-road" for sea or "word-hoard" for vocabulary — are the ancestors of Tolkien's compound names and epithets. "Bag End" as a kenning-like place-name, or "Wolfsbane" and "Kingsfoil" as plant-names in the athelas tradition.

**The Welsh Mabinogion:** The collection of medieval Welsh tales that Tolkien knew both in the original Middle Welsh and in English translation. The otherworldly quality of the Mabinogion — its particular kind of fairy-land that is adjacent to the real world and overlaps with it in disconcerting ways — is one source for how Tolkien handles Faërie in his secondary world. The woods of Mirkwood and the dreamlike quality of Lothlórien have Mabinogion roots.

## Part 7: Catholic Theology — The Deep Structure

Tolkien described The Lord of the Rings as "a fundamentally religious and Catholic work," and this requires understanding in some depth, because the Catholicism in question is not obvious — there is no church, no mass, no priest, no explicitly Christian character. The religious quality is structural and theological rather than confessional.

**The theology of the Fall:** The Ainulindalë is Tolkien's version of the Fall — the moment when the world is damaged by the free choice of a creature who was given the freedom to choose otherwise. Like the Christian doctrine of Original Sin, Tolkien's Arda Marred is a world that carries within itself the damage of a foundational wrong choice. Unlike some Calvinist traditions, Tolkien's Arda Marred is not a world in which the damage is total — the creation is still good, the beauty is still real, the damage is a distortion rather than a corruption of the fundamental nature. This is a Thomistic understanding of evil as privation rather than substance.

**Providence and free will:** The mythology consistently maintains both divine providence (Ilúvatar's design encompasses all, including Melkor's rebellion) and genuine free will (Melkor's choice was real, Men's choices are real, the Oath of Fëanor was freely sworn). Tolkien held the Catholic position that these two are compatible — that genuine freedom and divine foreknowledge are not contradictory — and built it into the mythology's structure.

**Grace:** The theology of grace — of divine assistance extended to beings in need, assistance that does not override free will but works with and through it — is most visible in the fate of Gollum. Frodo's mercy to Gollum is an act of grace extended beyond what justice requires. Ilúvatar's turning of Gollum's last act into the instrument of the Ring's destruction is providence working through what appears to be failure. The pattern is Augustinian: God writes straight with crooked lines.

**The Incarnation as model for the Istari:** The five Wizards, who are Maiar taking physical form and entering the world in diminished power to assist its peoples, have an obvious structural parallel to the Incarnation — the divine entering the created world in limited physical form for the benefit of those within it. Tolkien denied this was allegory, which is correct: the Istari are not Christ. But the structural parallel is real, and it reflects Tolkien's Catholic understanding of how divine assistance enters the created order.

**Purgatory and the Halls of Mandos:** The Halls of Mandos, where Elvish souls wait after death for eventual rehousing, has structural similarities to the Catholic doctrine of Purgatory — a state after death in which purification occurs before the soul can proceed to its final destination. Tolkien was presumably aware of this parallel, though he did not draw it explicitly in his published texts.

**The Eucharist and lembas:** Tolkien described the waybread of the Elves — lembas — in terms that suggest a eucharistic parallel in at least one letter (Letter 210, to Forrest J. Ackerman). Lembas sustains beyond what its physical substance should be capable of, feeds the spirit as well as the body, is given only to those judged worthy. The host of the Mass, which in Catholic theology is the literal body of Christ and nourishes the soul as well as marking the communicant's identity as part of the community, is not an allegory Tolkien is pushing — but the resonance is there.

## Part 8: The Inklings — The Community That Made the Work Possible

Tolkien did not create his mythology in isolation. The community of friends and colleagues known as the Inklings — who met in Oxford, typically at the Eagle and Child pub (called "the Bird and Baby" by regulars) on Tuesdays and at Tolkien's or Lewis's rooms on Thursdays — provided the audience, the criticism, and the encouragement that kept the work going through the decades when it might otherwise have stalled.

The Inklings were not a formal literary group with membership criteria and published manifestos. They were a loose collection of friends who shared an interest in fantasy and mythology and Christianity, who read their work aloud to each other and responded with honest criticism. The core members included:

**C.S. Lewis (1898–1963):** Tolkien's closest friend and the person most responsible for The Lord of the Rings existing at all. Lewis converted from atheism to Christianity partly through conversations with Tolkien, and he became one of the most enthusiastic readers of the developing mythology. It was Lewis who, when Tolkien lost heart with The Lord of the Rings in the early 1940s and nearly abandoned it, urged him to finish. Lewis's own Narnia series and his Space Trilogy were developed partly in parallel with and in response to Tolkien's work. The two were also engaged in a long and productive argument about what mythology is and how it relates to truth — an argument that made both of them more precise about what they were doing.

**Charles Williams (1886–1945):** The third major figure of the Inklings, a mystical thriller writer whose novels (Descent into Hell, All Hallows' Eve, The Place of the Lion) blend everyday contemporary settings with supernatural intrusions. Williams's theology influenced both Lewis and Tolkien, and his death in 1945 was a significant blow to the group. Tolkien wrote in a letter that he had expected Williams to outlive them all.

**Hugo Dyson:** A lecturer at Reading and Oxford who was instrumental in Lewis's conversion. Less significant as a creative figure but important to the group's dynamics.

**Owen Barfield:** A philosopher and one of Lewis's oldest friends, whose ideas about language and mythology — particularly the concept of "original participation," the idea that ancient peoples experienced language and reality as unified rather than divided — influenced both Lewis and Tolkien.

The specific contribution of the Inklings to Tolkien's mythology is documented. We know from Tolkien's letters and from Lewis's diaries that Tolkien read extensive portions of the mythology aloud at Inklings meetings — including early versions of what would become the Silmarillion, and the entire developing text of The Lord of the Rings. Lewis's encouragement after each reading session was direct and important: when Tolkien read the new chapters, Lewis was enthusiastic, and that enthusiasm kept Tolkien writing.

The famous conversation between Tolkien, Lewis, and Dyson on the night of September 19, 1931 — a long walk along Addison's Walk in Magdalen College, Oxford, during which Tolkien argued that myths were "the splintered fragments of the true light" and that the Incarnation was the myth that was also fact — was the crucial push that completed Lewis's conversion. It also sharpened Tolkien's understanding of his own project. If myths carried fragments of truth, and if his mythology was myth in the genuine sense, then it was not escape from reality but a way of approaching reality from another angle. This was the philosophical grounding for the mythology's seriousness.

## Part 9: On Fairy-Stories — Tolkien's Poetics

In 1947, Tolkien published "On Fairy-Stories" — an extended essay that began as the Andrew Lang Lecture delivered at the University of St Andrews in 1939. It is the closest thing to a poetics of the mythology, a statement of what he thought he was doing and why it mattered.

The key concepts of the essay:

**Secondary World:** Tolkien distinguishes between the Primary World (reality as it is) and the Secondary World (a created world within a story). The author of a Secondary World is a sub-creator — making, in Tolkien's theological framework, an act parallel to and derived from the Creator's original making of the Primary World. Human creativity is not merely self-expression; it is the expression of the Image of God in human beings, the imaginative capacity that reflects the divine creative nature.

**Spell:** The quality of a Secondary World that makes its internal laws feel real — that produces the "enchantment" of genuine imaginative engagement, where the reader believes in the world's reality for the duration of the reading. This is not suspension of disbelief but something more active: the reader's own imagination participates in making the Secondary World real.

**Eucatastrophe:** The term Tolkien coined for the sudden joyous turn in a story that was otherwise going toward catastrophe — the unexpected rescue, the unexpected grace, the moment when everything seems lost and is not. Tolkien argued that this was the highest achievement of fairy-story: not the happy ending as a comfortable reassurance, but the happy ending as a glimpse of something real. He connected eucatastrophe explicitly to the Resurrection — the Resurrection was, in his framework, the greatest eucatastrophe in history: the one that was not merely narrative but fact.

**Escapism reconsidered:** Tolkien defended escapism against its critics (who in his time included several influential literary voices who regarded fantasy as infantile evasion). His argument was that there was a crucial difference between the Escape of the Deserter and the Escape of the Prisoner. The soldier who escapes from the battlefield is a deserter; the prisoner who escapes from prison is not. If the Primary World is a prison — a world of pain and mortality and diminishment — then fantasy's escape is not cowardice but the legitimate seeking of another window on a reality that the Primary World's limitations obscure.

This essay is essential for understanding what Tolkien thought The Lord of the Rings was and what he was trying to do with the mythology. He was not writing entertainment (though entertainment was a welcome side effect). He was making a Secondary World serious enough to function as a lens on the Primary World — to see in it things that could not be seen directly, to feel in it possibilities that daily life had obscured.

## Part 10: Leaf by Niggle — The Autobiographical Parable

Among Tolkien's shorter works, the short story "Leaf by Niggle" (written around 1943, published 1945) is the most directly autobiographical and the most revealing about his understanding of his own project.

Niggle is a painter who has a vision of a great Tree — extraordinarily detailed, beautiful, containing within it a whole world — but who can never finish the painting because his neighbours keep interrupting him with small tasks and small demands. He is eventually forced to go on a Journey (death, clearly) leaving the painting unfinished. In Purgatory (called a workhouse), he is purified and learns patience. And then he is led to a place where his Tree actually exists — complete, more fully realized than he had ever managed on canvas, real rather than imagined.

The story is an extended meditation on the relationship between artistic creation and the divine. The Tree Niggle paints is his mythology — vast, never quite finished, always interrupted by the demands of daily life and family and health. The Journey is death, which Tolkien knew would come before the mythology was completed. And the reward — the Tree made real, the Secondary World become Primary World — is Tolkien's expression of faith that the sub-creator's work is not lost, that what the imagination makes truly is a discovery of something real and not merely an invention.

This is not a comfortable or self-congratulatory parable. Niggle is not presented as a great artist; he is presented as a rather selfish one who neglects his neighbours for his painting. The purgatorial correction is real. But the vision is ultimately validated — the work mattered, the Tree was real, the creation was not merely self-indulgent.

Tolkien's own sense of his mythology's incompleteness — the sixty years of revision and the still-unfinished texts that Christopher Tolkien spent decades editing — is the anxiety behind this parable. He was Niggle. He could see the Tree but could not paint it in full. He died with much of it unpainted.

## Part 11: England and the English — The Mythological Project

Tolkien stated repeatedly that he wanted to create "a mythology for England" — to provide his country with the mythological tradition that the Norman Conquest of 1066 had disrupted. After 1066, the literary tradition of England shifted toward French models, and the native English mythological tradition was largely lost or relegated to fragments: Beowulf survived, but as a scholarly text rather than a living cultural heritage. The Arthurian cycle was English but had been so thoroughly Frenchified through Chrétien de Troyes and later through Malory that it no longer felt specifically English.

What Tolkien was arying to recover was something pre-Norman: a mythology that felt specifically of the land, of the grey skies and green fields and ancient hills of England, that had the quality he found in Beowulf and in the fragments of Anglo-Saxon verse — a melancholy beauty, a sense of something ancient and half-lost, the elegiac note that runs through the best of Old English poetry.

He achieved this most completely in the Shire. The Shire is England — specifically the rural England of his childhood in Sarehole, the England of small villages and green fields and a suspicion of anything "outlandish" (foreign), the England that was already disappearing in the face of industrialization when he was young and that had largely disappeared by the time he was writing The Lord of the Rings. The nostalgia for the Shire is explicitly a nostalgia for a lost England.

But the mythology is also specifically English in its theology: the combination of the Northern heroic spirit (which Tolkien found best preserved in Old English poetry) with the Catholic theological framework is a distinctively English combination, reflecting the history of a country that was once Germanic-pagan, then became Christian, and then had its Christianity shaped by Celtic and Roman and Norse influences into something distinctively its own.

The Rohirrim are Anglo-Saxons, and their positioning in the mythology — as a people of simple virtues and great courage, at the edge of the sophisticated political world — reflects Tolkien's view of what the Anglo-Saxons were: not the dominant culture of the era he was mythologizing, but a crucial people of nobility and endurance who would otherwise be overlooked in the great stories of Elves and Númenóreans.

## Part 12: The Industrial Age — What the Mythology Refuses

Tolkien was not simply in favour of the past and against the present. But he was specifically against industrialization in its most characteristic form: the reduction of the natural world to resource, the substitution of mechanical production for craft, the severing of the relationship between people and the land they lived on.

This critique runs through the mythology in several places:

**Saruman's Isengard:** The clearest symbol. Isengard under Saruman is described as a place of smoke and machinery, of Orcs working day and night in forges, of trees felled and burned for fuel, of a great wheel and pits. It is explicitly industrial — a nineteenth-century factory complex transplanted into a medieval setting. The Ents' destruction of it — the flooding, the trees breaking the machinery, the old world of living things reasserting itself over the dead world of mechanism — is Tolkien's most explicitly anti-industrial passage.

**The Scouring of the Shire:** Saruman's takeover of the Shire involves the replacement of the old mill with a bigger, noisier, more productive but uglier one. Small-scale Hobbit craft is displaced by industrial output. The Ruffians enforce rules that displace local autonomy with centralized control. Sam's restoration of the Shire — planting Lórien-seeds, restoring the old mill — is a restoration of craft and living growth over mechanism.

**Mordor:** The landscape of Mordor — ashen, sterile, poisoned by industry, the air smelling of sulphur, nothing growing — is a nightmare version of an industrial landscape. The fires of Orodruin are explicitly forge-fires, and Barad-dûr is built with the same kind of mastery-through-manufacture that Tolkien associated with the desire to dominate rather than the desire to make.

This is not a simple rejection of technology. Tolkien used a typewriter. He drove a car (briefly, and apparently badly). He was not a primitivist. His critique was specifically of what he saw as the destructive relationship between the industrial mode of production and the natural world, and between the desire for domination (which industrialization expressed in its worst forms) and the desire to make (which craft expressed in its best).

## Part 13: The Similarities to Other Mythologies — And the Differences

Tolkien's mythology has been compared to many existing mythological traditions, and the comparisons are always partly right and always incomplete. Understanding what is borrowed and what is transformed tells us a great deal about what Tolkien was doing.

**Greek mythology:** The Valar have been compared to the Olympian gods, and there are structural similarities — powerful beings who govern specific domains, who are not omnipotent, who have personalities and conflicts. But the crucial difference is monotheism: the Olympians are ultimate in themselves; the Valar are subordinate to Ilúvatar. Tolkien was a Catholic and could not make his gods genuinely divine. The Valar are the greatest servants in a theistic system; the Olympians are the top of the hierarchy in a polytheistic one.

**Norse mythology:** The deepest single influence. But the Norse mythology ends in Ragnarök — the twilight of the gods, the defeat of the divine order by chaos. Tolkien's mythology does not end this way. The War of Wrath is a victory. The Last Alliance is a victory. Even the ambiguous end of the Third Age — the Elves fading, Men inheriting a diminished world — is not Ragnarök. Tolkien could not accept the purely tragic ending of the Northern tradition because his Catholic faith required a conviction that the world's story was not ultimately tragedy. The eucatastrophe was always available.

**Celtic mythology:** The fairy-world of Celtic tradition — the Otherworld that exists alongside and overlapping with the ordinary world, the fairy-folk who are beautiful and dangerous and other — is present in Tolkien's Elves, particularly the Lothlórien Elves and Galadriel herself. But Tolkien's Elves are not the morally ambiguous fairy-folk of Celtic tradition; they are dignified and committed to the good in a way that the Celtic fairy-folk, with their capriciousness and their alien ethics, are not.

**Hindu mythology:** Tolkien was aware of the Hindu mythological tradition through his scholarly knowledge of Indo-European philology, and there are structural similarities — a creator god, subordinate powers, cycles of ages, a mythology that encompasses vast spans of time. But there is no specific borrowing of the kind that characterises his engagement with Norse and Finnish material.

## Part 14: The Scholarship — How Professional Knowledge Shaped the Mythology

Tolkien's professional scholarly work shaped the mythology in ways that go beyond the obvious (he knew Beowulf; Grendel influenced his monsters; etc.). Specific scholarly projects had specific mythological effects:

**The A, B, C manuscripts of Beowulf:** In his editorial work on Beowulf, Tolkien spent years with the specific texture of an early medieval heroic poem, absorbing its rhythms and its narrative strategies. The "matter of fact" way Beowulf treats its extraordinary events — the matter-of-fact enormity that Tolkien admired, the way great events are described with understatement — is directly present in the narrative style of The Lord of the Rings.

**The Ancrene Wisse:** Tolkien worked on this Middle English devotional text for women recluses, a 13th-century guide to the anchoritic life. This immersion in medieval religious life — its specific vocabulary for spiritual struggle, its metaphors of the soul under siege — influenced the spiritual texture of the mythology.

**The Gothic language:** Tolkien's enthusiasm for Gothic, the earliest attested Germanic language, gave him phonological material for the construction of Khuzdul (the hard consonants of Gothic influenced the Dwarvish sound palette) and for the broader Germanic linguistic tradition he drew on for the Rohirrim.

**The mystery of the "genuine" English tradition:** Tolkien's scholarly conviction that England had lost its mythological tradition in the Norman Conquest — that something had been genuinely cut off — was not merely a professional observation but a personal grief. He spent his career studying what had been lost (Old English literature, the older mythological patterns preserved in Beowulf and the fragments) and his non-professional life trying to recover it through creation.

## Part 15: The Great War's Language — What Tolkien Preserved and What He Refused

The literary response to the First World War produced, in England, a distinctive poetic tradition: Wilfred Owen, Siegfried Sassoon, Rupert Brooke — poets who responded to the industrial slaughter of the trenches by either romanticizing it (Brooke, early in the war) or by the bitter, direct, irony-soaked verse of Owen's "Dulce et Decorum Est." The dominant literary response to the war was disillusionment: heroism was a lie, patriotism was a lie, the old stories of glory and sacrifice were cover for industrial murder.

Tolkien did not make this response. He went through the same experience — the Somme, the losses, the mud and the lice and the disease — and came out of it not disillusioned with heroism but convinced that heroism was real and that the stories of heroism were not lies. He believed that the courage of the soldiers in the trenches — the courage of ordinary people doing terrible things with dignity — was genuinely heroic. The heroism was not in the cause (he was sceptical of political causes) but in the person, in the doing.

This is why the mythology is not ironic. It is not a debunking of the heroic tradition. It is an affirmation of it — not of the political and military institutions that deploy heroism for their purposes, but of the individual human capacity for courage and sacrifice and love in terrible circumstances. Tolkien knew those circumstances from the inside, and what he had seen in the trenches was not evidence that heroism was a lie but evidence that it was real even in the worst possible conditions.

Sam Gamgee carries Frodo up Mount Doom. This is not a debunking. It is an affirmation: ordinary people, in the worst possible circumstances, do extraordinary things not because they are heroes in the classical sense but because they love someone and won't leave them. Tolkien saw this in the trenches — the batmen, the ordinary soldiers, the men who died for each other — and put it in his mythology.

## Part 16: The Oxford Landscape — Physical Sources

Oxford and its surrounding countryside are physically present in the mythology in ways that Tolkien scholarship has mapped.

**The River Cherwell:** The winding, tree-lined river that flows through the Oxford University Parks and Addison's Walk has the quality of the rivers of Rivendell — slow, clear, bordered by willows and alders. Tolkien walked along it throughout his Oxford years.

**Addison's Walk:** The path in Magdalen College grounds where Tolkien and Lewis and Dyson walked the night of Lewis's conversion. It has the quality of Elvish woodland paths — ancient trees, dim light filtering through leaves.

**The Inklings meetings at the Eagle and Child:** "The Bird and Baby," 49 St Giles' Street, where the Tuesday morning Inklings gathered. The back room where they met — small, wood-panelled, warm — has something of the quality of hobbit-hole interiors.

**The Berkshire Downs:** The rolling chalk downlands south of Oxford, with their ancient earthworks and barrows, are the landscape behind the Barrow-downs of the Old Forest region. Tom Shippey has traced specific Berkshire landmarks to specific mythological locations.

**The West Midlands and Birmingham:** Tolkien's childhood landscape — the Sarehole Mill, the twin towers of the Edgbaston waterworks that some have suggested are the origin of Minas Morgul and Minas Tirith, or alternately of the Two Watchers at Cirith Ungol — is present in the mythology. He described himself as a "West Midlander by blood and by choice."

## Part 17: The Problem of Race — The Honest Examination

Any honest accounting of Tolkien's sources and influences must address the question of race in the mythology, because it is genuinely present and genuinely problematic.

Tolkien's mythology draws on a Northern European mythological tradition in a way that privileges Northern European peoples and cultures. The peoples who fight on the side of the West in the War of the Ring are primarily Elvish (clearly Nordic/Celtic aesthetic), Numenorean/Dúnedain (clearly aristocratic Northern European), Rohirrim (explicitly Anglo-Saxon), and Dwarves (Norse). The peoples who fight on Sauron's side are primarily Orcs (presented as irredeemably evil), Easterlings (vaguely Central Asian aesthetic), and Haradrim (vaguely African/Middle Eastern aesthetic in the films, though Tolkien's text is less specific). The geographic distribution — the good guys in the northwest, the bad guys in the east and south — maps uncomfortably onto real-world racial geography.

Tolkien was not a racist in the deliberate, ideological sense — he despised the Nazis and their racial ideology, wrote pointedly about this in a famous draft letter to a German publisher who had asked him to confirm his "Aryan descent" (Tolkien rejected the question with some heat and pointed out that he wished he had some Elvish blood but that he had no knowledge of "Aryan" forebears, the term being linguistically meaningless). He was not writing a racial allegory.

But he was working within a tradition — the Northern heroic tradition, the Romantic nationalist mythology project — that had racial assumptions built into it, and those assumptions are present in the work. The darkness of Mordor, the darkness of the Orcs and their allies, the western/northwestern orientation of the "good" cultures — these are not innocent of the racial imaginaries of Tolkien's time and place.

The fairest reading, which is also the most honest one, is this: Tolkien's mythology contains real racial problems that are the result of unexamined assumptions rather than deliberate ideology, that are present despite his personal decency, and that are visible to readers who bring different perspectives to the text. Acknowledging this does not require dismissing the mythology or denying its value; it requires reading it with the critical awareness that all great works require and that their greatness does not exempt them from.

## Part 18: The Question of Women — Lúthien, Éowyn, Galadriel

The women of Tolkien's mythology are fewer in number than the men, and several of the most memorable are discussed in the mythology primarily as objects of desire (Lúthien) or as exceptions to a rule of masculine heroism (Éowyn). This is a legitimate observation and a legitimate critique.

And yet: Lúthien is the most powerful individual in the entire mythology when she is at her full strength. She defeats Sauron in single combat. She walks into Morgoth's throne room and enchants the most terrible being in Arda. She moves the most implacable of the Valar to tears. She is not a passive object; she is the most active agent in the Tale of Beren and Lúthien, and Beren is frequently the one being rescued.

Galadriel is one of the two or three most powerful beings remaining in Middle-earth in the Third Age, the wisest Elf in Middle-earth (by most accounts), the one who refuses the Ring when offered it and thereby demonstrates a strength of will that Boromir and Saruman and Denethor could not match.

Éowyn's arc is explicitly about the constraints placed on women's heroism in a patriarchal society and her breaking of those constraints. She is not presented as exceptional for being a woman who fights; she is presented as a person who has been told that her capacities are not welcome in the arena where they are most needed, who refuses that verdict, and who turns out to be the person the specific crisis required.

The women in the mythology are fewer than the men, and their fewer number is a real limitation. But within the framework of a medieval-inspired heroic tradition, the women who do appear are consistently among the most powerful and most admirable figures in the entire mythology. Tolkien's understanding of women was shaped by his time and his culture, and the mythology reflects that. It also contains, within those limitations, portraits of female power and courage that many modern readers find genuinely compelling.

## Part 20: What Remains — The Mythology as It Lives in the World

The Lord of the Rings has been continuously in print since 1954. It has sold more than a hundred and fifty million copies, by various estimates making it the third or fourth best-selling novel in history. It has been translated into more than sixty languages. It has generated an entire genre of commercial fiction (the secondary world fantasy), an academic field (Tolkien Studies), and a cultural phenomenon (the Peter Jackson films, The Rings of Power television adaptation, the extended gaming tradition, the language communities) that shows no signs of diminishing.

Why?

Part of the answer is accessibility: The Lord of the Rings tells a clear story with identifiable heroes and a satisfying resolution. It can be read as pure adventure and enjoyed on that level.

But the works that have merely accessible stories do not remain in print for seventy years. The deeper reason is that Tolkien built into his Secondary World something that functions as genuine myth functions in a culture: it provides a set of images and stories for thinking about things that are hard to think about directly. Mortality and the fear of it. The relationship between love and loss. What courage means when it is not rewarded. What ordinary people are capable of when everything depends on them. The nature of evil as distortion rather than as a separate power equal to good.

These are questions that every human being in every generation has to answer, and Tolkien's mythology provides not answers (it is too honest for pat answers) but a set of stories that make the questions visible and bearable. This is what myths do. This is what they have always done.

The world of Middle-earth is not England and it is not any specific place, but it is made from England and from the Norse north and from the medieval Catholic tradition and from the trenches of the Somme and from a man dancing with a woman among hemlock flowers in a Yorkshire wood while he was recovering from trench fever and trying to stay alive. It is made from all of that, and it gives all of that back to readers who encounter it — the longing for the Shire, the grief at the Grey Havens, the joy of the eucatastrophe, the particular courage of continuing to put one foot in front of the other when everything suggests that stopping would be reasonable.

The mythology is not finished. Tolkien never finished it, and in a different sense, no mythology is ever finished. It continues in the readers who carry it, in the writers who respond to it, in the scholars who study it, in the musicians and painters and game designers who find in it images and stories they need. It continues in the nine-year-old who reads The Hobbit for the first time and feels, for the first time, that there is a world as large as they suspected the world might be.

The Tree is not done growing.
