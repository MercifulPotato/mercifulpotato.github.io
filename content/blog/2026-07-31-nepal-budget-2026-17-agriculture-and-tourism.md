We have spent recent posts on the parts of the budget that make headlines — infrastructure, energy, health, the safety net. Now we turn to the economy that most Nepalis actually live in, the one beneath the headlines: agriculture. For the majority of Nepal's people, the fundamental economic facts of life are not tax rates or hydropower exports but the land, the monsoon, the harvest, and whether the farm can feed and support the family. Agriculture is how most Nepalis make their living, and yet — in a paradox that lies at the heart of the country's economy — it produces relatively little of the nation's wealth and cannot even reliably feed the country, which imports food despite being an agricultural nation. Understanding this paradox, and what the budget does about it, takes us to the real economic condition of most of the population.

This post examines the budget's agriculture allocation of about **NPR 73.12 billion (around USD 490 million)** and its centrepiece agricultural-investment grant, along with its tourism ambitions, anchored by the planned Visit Nepal Year campaign, funded through the Ministry of Culture, Tourism and Civil Aviation's allocation of about **NPR 10.53 billion (around USD 70 million)**. And it confronts a recurring problem in agricultural and other subsidies that this budget illustrates vividly: the tendency for support that is presented as helping ordinary people — farmers — to be designed in ways that actually channel the benefit to the *well-connected and the already-prosperous*, while the smallholders who form the mass of the rural population are left out. By the end you will understand the productivity puzzle at the heart of Nepal's agricultural economy, and you will be able to read an agricultural subsidy — or any subsidy — and ask the question that reveals who it really helps: not whom it names, but who can actually qualify for it.

## The agricultural paradox

Start with the paradox, because it frames everything. Agriculture occupies a strange and central place in Nepal's economy, defined by a mismatch between *people* and *output*.

On the *people* side, agriculture is overwhelmingly dominant. A large majority of Nepal's population — historically well over half — depends on agriculture for their livelihood, working the land as farmers, mostly on small plots, growing food for their families and for sale. For most Nepalis, agriculture is not a sector of the economy; it is *life* — the source of their food, their income, and their way of living. So agriculture is, by far, the largest *employer* and the foundation of most people's economic existence.

On the *output* side, however, agriculture is far less dominant. Despite employing the majority of the population, agriculture produces only around a quarter of the country's total economic output (its GDP) — a much smaller share than its share of employment. This mismatch is the essence of the paradox: agriculture employs most of the people but produces a disproportionately *small* share of the wealth. And the mismatch has a precise meaning, which is the key to understanding rural poverty in Nepal: it means that agricultural *productivity* — the output produced per worker, per unit of land — is *low*. A sector that uses most of the workers to produce a small share of the output is, by definition, a low-productivity sector, where each worker produces relatively little. And low productivity translates directly into low incomes: because each agricultural worker produces little, each earns little, which is why rural agricultural poverty is so pervasive — the majority of Nepalis work in a low-productivity sector that cannot generate good incomes. This is the economic root of much of Nepal's poverty and of the migration that defines it: people leave the land, and leave the country, precisely because the low-productivity agriculture that employs most Nepalis cannot provide a good living.

The paradox has a second, almost shocking dimension: Nepal, an agricultural country where most people farm, *imports food*. A nation of farmers cannot reliably feed itself and must buy food from abroad — importing rice, vegetables, and other foods that it might be expected to grow. This is the productivity problem made concrete: Nepali agriculture is so low in productivity that it cannot produce enough even to feed the country, let alone generate a surplus for prosperity, so food must be imported, adding to the import bill we discussed in the revenue post and deepening the dependence on imports that characterises the whole economy. An agricultural country importing food is a vivid symptom of an agriculture that is not productive enough.

So the central challenge for agriculture, and the test of any agricultural policy, is *raising productivity* — enabling the agricultural sector to produce more per worker and per unit of land, so that it can feed the country, generate better incomes for the majority who depend on it, reduce food imports, and perhaps even produce a surplus. This is the productivity question, and it is the lens through which the budget's agricultural measures should be judged: do they actually raise productivity, particularly for the smallholders who form the mass of the rural population, or do they not?

## The agriculture allocation and the investment grant

This budget allocates about NPR 73.12 billion (around USD 490 million) to the Ministry of Agriculture and the related areas of forestry and environment. This funds a range of activities — agricultural programmes, support, research, irrigation-related work, and the centrepiece measure we will examine: a grant for agricultural investment.

The centrepiece is a grant in which the government will cover *up to 40 percent of the initial capital* for agricultural investments — but with a crucial condition: the investment must be **at least NPR 2 crore**, which is NPR 20 million, or around USD 133,000. Let us understand exactly what this means before we examine who it helps. The government is offering to subsidise large agricultural investments by paying up to 40 percent of their initial capital cost — so for a qualifying investment of NPR 2 crore (NPR 20 million), the government might contribute up to NPR 8 million (around USD 53,000). The stated intention is presumably to encourage investment in agriculture, to modernise and commercialise farming, to bring capital and scale into a sector that needs them — which is, in the abstract, a reasonable goal, since raising productivity does require investment in better methods, equipment, and scale.

But now apply the question that this post exists to ask, the question that reveals who a subsidy really helps: *who can actually qualify for this grant?* The condition is that the investment must be at least NPR 2 crore — NPR 20 million, around USD 133,000. So to access this grant, a person must be able to make an agricultural investment of at least NPR 20 million. Who, in Nepal, can do that? Recall the agricultural reality we just described: the mass of Nepali farmers are *smallholders* — people farming small plots, with little capital, often barely above subsistence, for whom an investment of NPR 20 million is utterly inconceivable, a sum many times their entire wealth, far beyond anything they could ever invest. The smallholders who form the majority of the rural population, and who are the poorest and most in need of support, *cannot possibly qualify* for this grant, because they cannot make an investment of NPR 2 crore. The only people who *can* qualify are those who already have, or can mobilise, capital of at least NPR 20 million to invest in agriculture — that is, the relatively wealthy, large commercial farmers, agribusinesses, and well-connected investors. So a grant presented as support for agriculture is, by its design — its NPR 2 crore floor — accessible *only to the already-prosperous*, and entirely *inaccessible to the smallholders* who are the mass of farmers and the people in greatest need.

This is a textbook case of a subsidy whose design determines a distribution very different from its presentation, and it deserves to be stated plainly. The grant is, in effect, a transfer of public money — up to 40 percent of large investments — to those wealthy enough to invest NPR 2 crore in agriculture, funded by the public purse, while the smallholders who cannot meet the threshold receive nothing from it. It may be defensible on certain grounds — one might argue that encouraging large-scale commercial agriculture raises overall productivity and food production, creates jobs, and modernises the sector, benefiting the country broadly even if the direct grant goes to the wealthy. That argument has some force, and we should acknowledge it: large commercial farms can be more productive, and supporting them might raise output and employment. But the argument must be weighed against the reality that this is *public money flowing to the prosperous*, that it does *nothing directly for the smallholders* who are the poorest and most numerous, and that it may *worsen* rural inequality by subsidising large operations that could even out-compete and displace the smallholders. And it should be weighed against the alternative uses of the same money — support designed to actually reach smallholders, to raise *their* productivity, which would address rural poverty more directly. So the grant is not necessarily indefensible, but its design — the NPR 2 crore floor — means that, whatever its stated purpose, it channels public money to the wealthy and excludes the smallholders, and a citizen should see that distribution clearly rather than accepting the framing of "support for agriculture" that obscures who actually benefits.

## The deeper question: what would actually help smallholders?

Step back and ask the productivity question directly: what would actually raise the productivity and incomes of the smallholders who form the mass of Nepali agriculture? This matters because it is the test against which the budget's agricultural spending should be judged, and because it reveals what the NPR 2 crore grant does *not* do.

Raising smallholder productivity requires a different kind of support than a grant accessible only to large investors. It requires things like: *access to better inputs* (improved seeds, fertilizer, tools) at affordable prices; *irrigation* (so that farming does not depend entirely on the erratic monsoon — and recall that the agriculture ministry shares responsibility for irrigation); *agricultural extension* (the provision of knowledge and advice to farmers about better methods); *access to credit* on terms smallholders can use (small loans, not grants requiring NPR 2 crore investments); *access to markets* (the roads and connections to actually sell produce profitably — connecting back to the infrastructure post); *storage and processing* (to reduce the waste of produce that spoils before it can be sold); and *fair prices* for what farmers produce. These are the kinds of support that actually reach smallholders and actually raise *their* productivity and incomes — and they are largely *different* from a grant covering 40 percent of NPR 2 crore investments, which reaches none of them. So the question for the budget's agricultural spending is whether, beyond the headline grant for large investors, it actually provides the irrigation, inputs, extension, credit, market access, and price support that would raise smallholder productivity — because that, not subsidies to large investors, is what would address the agricultural paradox and rural poverty. A budget that spent on smallholder support would be tackling the real problem; a budget whose centrepiece is a grant accessible only to the wealthy is, at best, addressing a different and narrower goal (large-scale commercialisation) while leaving the mass of farmers and the core productivity problem largely untouched. A citizen evaluating the agriculture allocation should look beyond the headline grant to ask whether the spending actually reaches and helps the smallholders who are the majority — and should be skeptical of agricultural support that, by its design, flows to the few who least need it.

## Tourism and Visit Nepal Year

The budget also addresses *tourism*, through the Ministry of Culture, Tourism and Civil Aviation (about NPR 10.53 billion, around USD 70 million) and through the promotion of a *Visit Nepal Year* campaign — a dedicated effort to attract international tourists, planned around the Bikram Sambat year 2085. Tourism deserves attention because it is, like hydropower and IT, one of the genuine economic opportunities suited to Nepal's particular endowments.

The logic of tourism for Nepal is strong. Nepal possesses extraordinary tourism assets: the Himalayas, including Everest and the great peaks that draw mountaineers and trekkers from around the world; rich cultural and religious heritage, including sites of profound significance to Hindus and Buddhists; stunning natural beauty; and a reputation as a destination for adventure and spirituality. Tourism brings *foreign currency* — exactly what Nepal needs, earned by providing services to visitors rather than by exporting labour — and it creates *jobs* across the economy (in hospitality, guiding, transport, crafts, and more), often in areas and for people that other sectors do not reach. So tourism is a genuine and well-suited economic opportunity, and promoting it — through a Visit Nepal Year campaign aimed at boosting visitor numbers — is a reasonable strategy that plays to Nepal's strengths. The aspiration deserves credit.

The cautions, as always, are about effectiveness and about the difference between promotion and the underlying conditions. A *Visit Nepal Year* campaign — a promotional push to attract tourists — can raise awareness and visitor numbers, but its success depends heavily on factors beyond the campaign itself: on the *infrastructure* to receive tourists (airports, roads, the connectivity we discussed — and recall the capital-absorption problems that plague that infrastructure), on the *quality and safety* of the tourism experience, on *air connectivity* (which is why aviation shares the ministry), and on *value for money* and reputation. A promotional campaign that is not backed by the actual infrastructure and quality to receive and satisfy the tourists it attracts may disappoint — and Nepal's previous tourism-promotion campaigns have had mixed results, sometimes affected by external shocks (and notably, a previous Visit Nepal Year campaign was severely disrupted by the global pandemic, a reminder that tourism is vulnerable to forces entirely outside Nepal's control). So tourism promotion is a reasonable and well-suited strategy, but it should be judged by whether it is backed by the infrastructure and quality to actually deliver a good tourist experience, and by actual visitor numbers and tourism earnings, rather than by the announcement of a campaign. And tourism, like remittances, is a source of foreign currency that depends partly on external conditions (global travel, the absence of disruptions) beyond Nepal's control, so it is a genuine but not wholly reliable opportunity. The right stance is to welcome the tourism focus as playing to a real strength, while watching whether the supporting conditions are in place and whether the campaign actually delivers visitors and earnings.

## A worked example: the smallholder and the agribusiness

Make the subsidy-exclusion problem concrete by following two very different farmers as they encounter the agricultural-investment grant, because the contrast reveals exactly who the grant helps.

Consider first **Hari**, a smallholder farmer in a hill district — representative of the mass of Nepali farmers. Hari farms a small plot, growing food mostly for his family with some surplus to sell. His total wealth — his land, his few tools, his modest savings — amounts to a fraction of what would be needed. When Hari hears that the government is offering a grant covering 40 percent of agricultural investment, he might, for a hopeful moment, imagine help. But then he encounters the condition: the investment must be at least NPR 2 crore — NPR 20 million, around USD 133,000. Hari could never invest NPR 20 million; the sum is many times everything he owns, utterly beyond his reach. So the grant, despite being "agricultural support," offers Hari *nothing* — he cannot qualify, because he cannot make an investment of the required size. The support announced for agriculture does not reach the smallholder who is the typical Nepali farmer and who is in genuine need.

Consider next **a well-capitalised agribusiness** — perhaps a company, or a wealthy individual investor, with access to substantial capital. This investor *can* mobilise NPR 2 crore to invest in a large commercial agricultural operation. So this investor *qualifies* for the grant, and the government will cover up to 40 percent of the investment — up to NPR 8 million (around USD 53,000) of public money — supporting the investor's large commercial venture. The grant, despite being "agricultural support," flows to the prosperous investor who could well have made the investment anyway, subsidised by public funds, while Hari and the millions of smallholders like him receive nothing from it.

The contrast reveals the distribution precisely. The grant excludes the smallholder Hari (and the mass of farmers like him, the poorest and most numerous and most in need) by its NPR 2 crore floor, and includes only the well-capitalised investor (the prosperous few). So the public money flows to the wealthy and away from the poor, despite the framing of "support for agriculture" — exactly the elite-capture pattern this post warns about. And the example illuminates the deeper point: a subsidy's *design* — here, the investment floor — determines its *distribution*, and a subsidy presented as broadly helpful can, by its design, actually channel benefit to the few while excluding the many. A citizen who follows the two farmers sees that the grant helps the agribusiness and not Hari, and understands that to judge a subsidy one must ask not whom it names ("farmers," "agriculture") but who can actually qualify for it — because the qualification conditions, not the name, determine who really benefits. The lesson generalises far beyond agriculture: any subsidy or support should be examined for the conditions that determine who can actually access it, because those conditions, often buried in the design, reveal the true distribution that the generous-sounding name conceals.

## For the technically minded: the allocations and the eligibility filter

For readers who work with numbers, here are the agriculture and tourism allocations in schematic form, followed by the eligibility logic of the grant — which reveals, in code, exactly who it excludes:

| Sector / item | NPR | USD (≈) | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agriculture, Forestry & Environment (ministry) | 73.12 billion | 0.49 billion | Most Nepalis depend on agriculture |
| — Agricultural investment grant | up to 40% of capital | — | Requires investment ≥ NPR 2 crore (USD ~133k) |
| Culture, Tourism & Civil Aviation (ministry) | 10.53 billion | 70 million | Tourism, heritage, aviation |
| — Visit Nepal Year campaign | (within tourism) | — | Promotion toward Bikram Sambat 2085 |

Now the eligibility filter, showing in code who can and cannot access the grant:

```text
# The agricultural investment grant: covers up to 40% of capital,
# but ONLY for investments at or above the NPR 2 crore floor.
INVESTMENT_FLOOR = 20_000_000   # NPR 2 crore (~USD 133,000)
GRANT_RATE       = 0.40         # up to 40% of the investment

def can_access_grant(investor_available_capital):
    if investor_available_capital >= INVESTMENT_FLOOR:
        grant = min(investor_available_capital, /* up to */ investor_available_capital) * GRANT_RATE
        return True, grant      # the wealthy investor qualifies; gets up to 40% subsidised
    else:
        return False, 0         # everyone below NPR 2 crore is EXCLUDED -- gets nothing

# A typical smallholder's available capital is a tiny fraction of NPR 2 crore:
smallholder_capital = 200_000   # illustrative -- far below the floor
can_access_grant(smallholder_capital)   # -> (False, 0): EXCLUDED

# A well-capitalised agribusiness can meet the floor:
agribusiness_capital = 25_000_000
can_access_grant(agribusiness_capital)  # -> (True, up to ~10,000,000 of public money)

# The FLOOR is the filter. It is presented as "agricultural support" but, by design,
# admits only the prosperous and excludes the smallholders who are the mass of farmers.
# To judge any subsidy: find the eligibility condition, and see who it admits and excludes.
```

The code makes the exclusion undeniable. The NPR 2 crore floor is an *eligibility filter* that, by its design, admits only those with capital at or above NPR 20 million — the prosperous — and excludes everyone below, which is the mass of smallholders. The grant is presented as agricultural support, but the filter determines that it flows to the wealthy and excludes the poor. A developer or analyst evaluating any subsidy would look not at its name or stated purpose but at its *eligibility conditions* — the filters that determine who can actually access it — because those conditions, expressed precisely, reveal the true distribution. And a citizen, equipped with the same logic, knows to ask of any subsidy: what is the eligibility condition, and who does it admit and exclude? The NPR 2 crore floor, examined this way, reveals a subsidy that excludes the very farmers it is presented as helping — and that revelation, available to anyone who examines the eligibility condition rather than the name, is exactly what the framing of "support for agriculture" is designed to obscure.

## How budgets deceive: subsidies that exclude the people they name

The technique of deception for this post is one of the most common and most consequential in all of public spending, because it lets a government appear to help ordinary people while actually channeling money to the prosperous: **subsidies and support that are presented as helping a broad, sympathetic group, but whose design — through eligibility conditions, thresholds, or requirements — actually excludes the mass of that group and channels the benefit to the well-connected few.**

Here is the move, and the agricultural grant is its textbook case. A subsidy or support measure is announced for a sympathetic, broad group — farmers, small businesses, the rural population — generating goodwill and the appearance of helping ordinary people. But the *design* of the measure — the eligibility conditions, the thresholds, the requirements that determine who can actually qualify — restricts the benefit to a narrow, often prosperous or well-connected subset, while the mass of the named group is excluded. The deception is in the gap between the *presentation* (broad support for a sympathetic group) and the *design* (a benefit accessible only to a privileged few): the government banks the political credit of appearing to help farmers, while the public money actually flows to the wealthy investors who can meet the conditions, and the smallholders who are the face of the policy receive nothing. The deception is especially effective because the eligibility conditions are buried in the design, not the headline — the announcement says "support for agriculture" or "help for farmers," and one must dig into the conditions (the NPR 2 crore floor) to discover that most farmers are excluded. So the public hears "help for farmers" and credits the government with helping farmers, while the reality is a transfer to the prosperous, the exclusion invisible behind the generous-sounding name. This pattern — subsidies and support that exclude the people they name — is pervasive far beyond agriculture, appearing wherever a benefit is presented as broad but designed to be narrow: tax breaks "for business" that only large firms can use, support "for the poor" that the poorest cannot access, programmes "for ordinary people" whose conditions admit only the privileged.

The defence is to *examine the eligibility conditions of every subsidy and ask who they actually admit and exclude*. Whenever a subsidy or support measure is announced for a sympathetic group, do not stop at the name; find the *conditions* — the thresholds, the requirements, the qualifications — that determine who can actually access it, and ask: who can meet these conditions, and who cannot? Does this actually reach the broad group it names — the mass of farmers, the small businesses, the poor — or do its conditions restrict it to a privileged subset while excluding the many? In the agricultural grant, the NPR 2 crore floor is the condition, and examining it reveals that the grant excludes the smallholders who are the mass of farmers and admits only the prosperous — a transfer to the wealthy wearing the name of help for farmers. A citizen who examines the eligibility conditions of every subsidy, who refuses to credit a measure as broadly helpful merely because it is named for a broad group, and who asks always who can actually qualify, cannot be deceived by subsidies that exclude the people they name. And such a citizen reads this budget's agricultural grant for what it is: not support for the farmers it names, but a subsidy to the prosperous few who can meet its threshold, the smallholders excluded by design. The deception thrives on the public crediting the name; the defence is to examine the conditions and see who actually benefits — because in subsidies, as in so much of the budget, the design determines the distribution, and the name is chosen to obscure it.
