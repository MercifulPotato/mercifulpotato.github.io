# The Land Ceiling Manifesto: A Case for Universalist Land-Cap Socialism — From Kathmandu to Every City on Earth

> *"The problem is not that there are too many capitalists, but too few."*
> — G.K. Chesterton

> *"Why start a business when you can make more trading land? It has killed entrepreneurship."*
> — Former government economist, Kathmandu (2025)

---

There is a peculiar kind of madness that grips a city when land becomes its primary religion. It does not happen overnight. It starts as prudence — a family buys a plot, another plot, perhaps one more. It accelerates when the bank loan for that second plot earns more in twelve months than the factory floor pays in three years. And it metastasizes when the entire financial system, the entire political class, and the entire social imagination of a nation converges on a single proposition: *land always goes up.*

In Kathmandu, that proposition stopped being an investment thesis long ago. It became a geological fact, as immovable and as terrifying as the mountain ranges that ring the valley. A plot in Baneshwor that sold for NPR 20 lakhs per *aana* in 2010 now demands NPR 80–90 lakhs for the same patch of earth in 2025. Land prices in the valley have been growing at 27.7% per year according to Nepal Rastra Bank data — doubling in value approximately every three and a half years — while salaries have crept upward at a modest 7.45% annually. The arithmetic is not merely unfair. It is catastrophic.

But Kathmandu is not a singular tragedy. It is a particularly vivid expression of a global pathology: the transformation of the most fundamental human need — a place to stand, a place to shelter — into the most prized speculative asset class in human history. Seoul did it. Sydney did it. San Francisco did it. London did it decades ago, and is still doing it. The mechanisms differ in their details; the outcome is interchangeable: an ever-smaller class of landowners extracting ever-larger rents from an ever-larger class of people who will never own the ground beneath their feet.

This article proposes a solution. Not a tweak. Not a pilot programme. A structural reimagination of the relationship between citizens, land, and the state — one that draws on the intellectual traditions of Henry George, G.K. Chesterton, Hilaire Belloc, the lessons of Singapore's Housing Development Board, and the hard-won knowledge of every failed land reform that came before it. We call this framework **Universalist Land-Cap Socialism**, and its central mechanism is the **Ratcheting Land Ceiling**.

This is a long article. It needs to be, because the problem is long, the history is long, and the solutions need to be examined from every angle. If you are here for a quick take, you are in the wrong place. If you are here because you have looked at the cost of a house and felt something that sits between despair and fury — keep reading.

## Part 1: The Anatomy of a Land Crisis — Kathmandu Valley as Case Study

### 1.1 Geography as Destiny

The Kathmandu Valley is, in the most literal sense, a bowl. Ringed by five mountain ranges — Shivapuri to the north, Phulchowki to the south, Nagarjun to the northwest, Champadevi and Chandragiri to the southwest — the valley covers approximately 570 square kilometres of flat and gently sloping terrain at an elevation of around 1,400 metres. This geography, which made it the perfect site for an ancient urban civilisation and a crossroads of Himalayan trade routes, also makes it physically incapable of expanding outward. When a city grows in Kathmandu, it has nowhere to go but up — or it cannibalises the agricultural land and forests along its margins.

The valley contains three major districts — Kathmandu, Lalitpur, and Bhaktapur — with a combined census-measured population of just under three million as of Nepal's 2021 census. Population density in the Kathmandu district alone stands at approximately 5,169 people per square kilometre. The metropolitan city proper, covering around 50.7 square kilometres, has a density approaching 20,000 to 30,000 people per square kilometre in its most congested zones. The valley as a whole is growing at approximately 4% per year — one of the fastest urbanisation rates in South Asia — and the annual demand for new housing units in the valley is estimated at around 59,000 units (42% of Nepal's national demand of 140,000 units per year), while actual construction manages only around 25,000.

The math, in short, is broken by design.

### 1.2 The Price Numbers That Should Shock You

Numbers lose their power when they are large enough. So let us make them concrete.

A piece of land in Durbarmarg, Kathmandu's prime commercial strip, was sold for NPR 90 million — approximately USD 750,000 — for a plot whose price per square metre exceeded USD 22,000. That places it among the most expensive real estate on earth by per-square-metre cost, in a country whose GDP per capita was a meagre USD 1,447 in 2025. Nepal is one of the poorest nations in Asia by income. It has some of the priciest land on earth by square metre. That sentence is not a typo.

In Bhaktapur's Suryabinayak area — not prime Kathmandu, not Durbarmarg — land that cost NPR 10 lakhs per *aana* in 2012 has risen above NPR 60 lakhs per *aana* in 2025. An average three-bedroom house in Kapan, a mid-ring district of Kathmandu, that sold for NPR 1.2 crores in 2016 now commands NPR 3 to 3.5 crores. Apartments in Baneshwor demand monthly rents of NPR 35,000 to NPR 50,000 — far above the average Nepali household monthly income. Households in Kathmandu now spend between 20 and 25% of their monthly income on rent, compared with under 10% in rural areas.

The rental yield on a central Kathmandu property is a paltry 1.7% — the lowest in the South Asian region, comparable only to a handful of Chinese cities — while mortgage rates hover between 8% and 12%. In any rational market, this would represent a catastrophic misallocation of capital. In Kathmandu, it represents business as usual, because buyers are not purchasing for rental income. They are purchasing because they believe prices will continue to rise. And they have been right, with extraordinary consistency, for decades.

### 1.3 Remittances: The Rocket Fuel

Nepal's land crisis cannot be understood without understanding remittances. In 2023, Nepal received approximately USD 11 billion in remittances — more than 26% of the country's GDP, according to the World Bank. To put that in context: remittances exceeded the combined inflow of official development assistance and foreign direct investment. Approximately 56% of all Nepali households receive remittances. The country's economic lifeline, the money that feeds children and educates them and pays medical bills, arrives from approximately 3.5 million Nepali migrant workers toiling in the Gulf states, Malaysia, South Korea, and India.

Here is the mechanism by which remittances become a land crisis: When money arrives in a country with a volatile stock market, distrusted government bonds, a weak formal financial system, and a cultural tradition that views land as the ultimate store of value — it flows into land. Seventy-five percent of remittance flows globally are used for essential household expenditures, including housing. In Nepal, much of what remains goes into property. Every year, approximately USD 9 to 10 billion flows through this channel, and a significant portion of it chases the same finite bowl of Kathmandu valley land.

This creates what economists would recognise as a self-reinforcing speculative loop. Remittances push prices up. Rising prices validate the investment thesis. More remittances flow into land. Prices rise further. The loop tightens. Capital-gains tax on property falls from 7% to 5% after five years, compared with income-tax rates that can reach 39%. The incentives are, in the words of one economist quoted in the *Kathmandu Journal*, "clear": why start a business when land trading is more profitable? The result has been a documented destruction of entrepreneurship — a diversion of the country's primary capital source away from productive investment and into passive asset appreciation.

### 1.4 The Banking System as Accelerant

Nepal Rastra Bank, the central bank, has at various points attempted to cap real estate lending. In 2011, it directed banks and financial institutions to limit their real estate and housing lending to 25% of their total loans. The banks largely ignored this, or found creative ways around it — masking real estate loans as agricultural credit (which is why there was a 60% increase in agricultural loans with no corresponding increase in farm production or productivity), or as general business loans with land collateral.

The consequences: approximately 11–12% of Nepal's banking sector loan portfolios — well over Rs 427–516 billion — is tied up in real estate lending. More than two-thirds of all loans in the system use property as collateral. In a system this concentrated, a significant property correction does not merely harm landowners. It threatens the solvency of the financial system itself. The bubble has, in a phrase that deserves to be heard more widely, become too big to pop.

There have been cases of outright fraud. In one widely reported incident, 21 banks loaned a combined Rs 2.41 billion to members of the land mafia who presented property inside the grounds of the Prime Minister's residence in Baluwatar as collateral. The boundary between the banking system, the political class, and the land speculation industry has, at various points, simply ceased to exist.

### 1.5 What All of This Produces

The downstream effects of a land crisis at this scale are not abstract. They are measured in human lives and human dignity.

The Nepal Land Housing Developers Association estimates a housing shortfall of approximately 115,000 units per year in Kathmandu valley alone — the gap between annual demand of 59,000 units and actual supply of around 25,000. Middle-class and lower-middle-class families are systematically pushed to the urban periphery, lengthening commutes, straining infrastructure, and separating people from the economic opportunities that drove them to Kathmandu in the first place. The poorest families — squatters, day labourers, domestic workers — are pushed entirely off the map, into flood plains and hillside settlements that are among the most seismically vulnerable places to live in the world.

The 2015 earthquake killed approximately 9,000 people in Nepal, and destroyed or severely damaged over 600,000 homes. The earthquake did not discriminate between rich and poor by seismic preference; it discriminated by building quality. And building quality, in a city where 60% of structure costs are land, is the first thing cut. Haphazard, code-violating construction in one of the world's most seismically active cities is not a failure of engineering culture. It is a rational response to a land market that consumes most of the available capital before a single brick is laid.

The gap between land price growth (27.7% per year) and income growth (7.45% per year) is, over a decade, not merely an inequality. It is a chasm. Those who owned land in 2010 are, in 2025, separated from those who did not by a wealth gap that no salary increment will ever bridge. This is the defining feature of what we might call the *rentier trap*: a society in which economic position is determined not by productivity, skill, or effort, but by the accident of whether one's ancestors happened to hold title to a piece of the valley floor.

## Part 2: The Intellectual Architecture — What Ideas Built This Model

Before proposing a solution, we need to understand the intellectual raw materials from which it is constructed. Universalist Land-Cap Socialism is not a novel idea minted in isolation. It is a synthesis of several distinct and well-developed traditions, each of which captures something true, and each of which fails in isolation.

### 2.1 Henry George and the Single Land Tax

In 1879, an American journalist and political economist named Henry George published a book called *Progress and Poverty*. It sold three million copies — an extraordinary number for an economics book in any era — and in 1886, George narrowly missed being elected Mayor of New York City, finishing second in a three-way race in which the third-place finisher was a young Theodore Roosevelt. More than 100,000 people paid their respects at George's funeral in 1897. For a few decades, he was arguably the most influential economic thinker in the English-speaking world.

George's central observation was this: as societies become wealthier and more productive, the gains flow not to workers (whose wages remain at subsistence) or to capital (whose returns are competed down), but to landowners, whose holdings appreciate simply because society around them is improving. The value of land in a city centre is not created by the landowner. It is created by the roads, the schools, the hospitals, the commercial activity, the social infrastructure — all the investments of society at large. Yet the landowner captures this value as private wealth.

George's solution was elegant in its simplicity: tax the *unimproved* value of land at a rate high enough to capture the socially created "economic rent," while exempting improvements (buildings, factories, farms) from taxation entirely. Because the supply of land is perfectly inelastic — you cannot produce more of it — taxing its value does not reduce its availability or distort production. It simply transfers the unearned increment from private pockets to the public treasury. George argued that this single tax would be sufficient to fund all public goods, eliminating the need for taxes on labour or capital that reduce productivity.

George's framework is now implemented, in partial forms, across a surprisingly wide range of jurisdictions. Denmark, Estonia, Lithuania, Russia, Singapore, Taiwan, parts of Australia, Germany, Mexico, and Pennsylvania all levy some form of land value tax. In 2011, Altoona, Pennsylvania became the first American city to rely *exclusively* on a land value tax, eliminating taxes on buildings. The research consensus is broadly that land value taxes encourage development, reduce speculative vacancy, and are highly efficient — Milton Friedman called it "the least bad tax."

But George's framework has a gap, which is what motivates the extension proposed in this article. A land value tax, however well designed, operates at the *margin*. It can slow speculation, redirect capital, and fund public goods. What it does not do, on its own, is structurally limit the concentration of land ownership. A sufficiently wealthy individual or corporation can absorb a land value tax and continue holding vast tracts of land, simply passing costs on to tenants or writing them off against other income. The Georgist mechanism is necessary but not sufficient for a society that wants to eliminate the rentier class, not merely tax it.

### 2.2 Chesterton, Belloc, and Distributism

In the early twentieth century, G.K. Chesterton and Hilaire Belloc developed a framework they called Distributism. Where Georgism focused on a single elegant tax mechanism, Distributism was a broader social philosophy: the ownership of productive property should be *distributed as widely as possible* throughout society, rather than concentrated in the hands of a few capitalists (as in liberal capitalism) or in the hands of the state (as in socialism).

Chesterton's most famous summary: "The problem is not that there are too many capitalists, but too few." Belloc, in *The Servile State* (1912), argued that capitalism and socialism were two faces of the same coin — both produced a society in which most people owned nothing productive, and were therefore wholly dependent, either on an employer or on a bureaucrat. Distributism sought a society of owners: people who held enough productive property to be genuinely economically independent.

The Distributist tradition has been criticised for its romanticism — for its preference for small farms and artisan workshops in an age of industrial economies — and for its historical association with a specifically Catholic social theology that may not translate across cultures. These are fair criticisms, and we do not adopt Distributism wholesale. What we take from it is its *diagnosis*: the fundamental problem of modern capitalism is not exploitation in the Marxist sense, but displacement — the systematic exclusion of the majority from ownership of productive assets. And the *remedy* it points toward: not nationalisation, but wide ownership.

Resource-Based Distributism, as we develop it here, updates the Chestertonian insight for a twenty-first century urban context. The productive asset we are most concerned with is not the farm or the workshop, but the land itself. And we are not seeking to distribute it equally to every household — a logistically impossible and socially disruptive goal — but to *cap* it, so that no single agent can accumulate so much of the finite and socially created asset of urban land that others are excluded from access to it.

### 2.3 The Universalist Welfare Tradition: Why Means Testing Fails

The third intellectual strand is the debate between means-tested welfare and universal provision. This is a debate with a long history in social policy, and it has a reasonably clear empirical resolution that is nonetheless politically difficult to act on.

Means-tested programmes — welfare benefits awarded only to those who fall below income or asset thresholds — create what policy researchers call the "welfare cliff" or "benefit cliff." This is the point at which earning an additional dollar of income triggers the loss of benefits worth more than a dollar. A single mother in Missouri receiving Medicaid, SNAP, childcare subsidies, housing assistance, and TANF faces a situation in which a modest raise — moving her from USD 12 to USD 15 an hour — can trigger the simultaneous loss of multiple overlapping benefits, reducing her *total* resources by more than the wage increase added. The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta's CLIFF Tools research has documented these cliffs across American states; they are not edge cases. They are the normal operating condition of means-tested welfare.

The cliff effect produces perverse incentives: it traps people in poverty by making it financially rational to turn down work or to underreport income. It requires vast bureaucratic infrastructure to administer eligibility checks, income verification, asset tests, and appeals processes. It is stigmatising. And it is deeply regressive in its actual distribution: as Brookings scholars have documented, an increasing share of means-tested welfare spending goes to middle-class recipients rather than those most in need, because programmes have expanded upward while leaving extreme poverty insufficiently addressed.

The universalist counter-tradition — best exemplified by Beveridge's NHS, universal state pensions, and universal child benefit in the UK tradition — argues that services which everyone uses are politically durable, economically efficient, and socially integrative. Citizens Network's analysis of UK welfare policy makes the key observation: it is not *services* that are better protected than *cash benefits*; it is *universal* entitlements that are better protected than *means-tested* ones. The NHS and state pension, both universal, have survived decades of austerity politics that gutted means-tested disability and housing benefits.

The mechanism for this political durability is straightforward: when everyone uses a service, everyone votes to defend it. When only the poor use a service, it becomes a target for politicians who want to signal fiscal rectitude by cutting "welfare." Universal provision creates a constituency of defence that is, by definition, the entire citizenry.

Universal provision also eliminates the administrative apparatus of means testing — the armies of case workers, the income verification requirements, the appeals processes, the fraud detection units — replacing them with a simple, universal entitlement. The administrative savings can be substantial, though the precise magnitude depends on the programme design.

### 2.4 Singapore: The Existence Proof

Any serious discussion of public housing as a universal good must contend with Singapore. Because Singapore has done, essentially, what we are proposing — and it has worked.

When the Housing Development Board was established in 1960, more than 70% of Singapore's population was living in slums and squatter settlements. Within three years, the HDB had built 21,000 flats. By 2025, more than 80% of Singapore's residents — including 84% of the poorest 10% of households — live in HDB-provided housing, and more than 90% of them *own* the flat they live in (on 99-year leases). The homeownership rate is approximately 88% of all resident households. There is, as every analyst of the Singapore model notes, no stigma attached to HDB housing, because the majority of Singaporeans — ministers, professionals, millionaires — grew up in HDB towns.

How did Singapore do it? Several interacting mechanisms, all of which are relevant to our model.

First, the Land Acquisition Act of 1966, which gave the government broad powers to acquire land for any public purpose, at compensation rates fixed at pre-development values. This effectively ended speculative windfall profits from land located in the path of development. The government could acquire land cheaply, build public housing, and sell or lease the flats at below-market rates. The unearned increment from urban development flowed to the public, not to private landowners. This is the most direct expression of Georgist principle ever implemented at scale.

Second, the Central Provident Fund mechanism, which allowed Singaporeans to use their compulsory retirement savings to purchase HDB flats. This eliminated the need for commercial mortgage markets in the public housing sector, avoided the interest-rate exposure that destroys housing affordability in mortgage-dependent systems, and integrated housing equity into the broader system of retirement security.

Third, the explicit rejection of the income-segregation model that produced the housing project failures of American cities and the *banlieues* of Paris. HDB estates are mixed-income by design, with quotas ensuring ethnic and income diversity at the block and precinct level. They are built with hawker centres — Singapore's beloved open-air food courts where all social classes eat side by side — integrated transit, schools, and commercial space. Public housing in Singapore is not a poverty warehouse. It is a community.

Fourth, and most relevant to our proposal: the government's retention of land ownership. HDB flats are sold on 99-year leases; the government retains the freehold. This means that the enormous uplift in land values generated by Singapore's economic development over the past six decades has remained, substantially, in public hands. It has not created a class of property millionaires. It has created a society of comfortable, secure, largely debt-free homeowners who live in public infrastructure that would shame most private developments in richer countries.

Singapore's GDP grew by approximately 6% per year on average over the last 50 years. The country's economists attribute a significant portion of this to the Georgist structure of land policy: by removing land as the primary speculative asset class, Singapore redirected capital into productive investment. The comparison with Kathmandu — where land speculation has, in the frank assessment of local economists, "killed entrepreneurship" — is stark.

## Part 3: The Model — Universalist Land-Cap Socialism in Detail

We now have the intellectual raw materials. Let us build the model.

Universalist Land-Cap Socialism (ULCS) rests on four interlocking pillars:

1. **The Ratcheting Land Ceiling** — a legislative cap on private land ownership that tightens over time
2. **Permanent Public Housing as Infrastructure** — surplus land converted into non-market, non-speculative public housing
3. **Universal Social Floors** — replacing means-tested welfare with universal entitlements that eliminate the welfare cliff
4. **Resource-Based Financing** — funding the system through land value taxation and a progressive land holding charge on holdings approaching the ceiling

Each of these pillars is examined in detail below.

### 3.1 The Ratcheting Land Ceiling

The central mechanism of ULCS is the **Ratcheting Land Ceiling** — a legal limit on the maximum amount of land a single private person or legal entity may own, which decreases over time on a published, predictable schedule.

The starting parameters in the Kathmandu context might look like this:

**Phase 1 (Years 1–5):** Maximum private landholding set at 5,000 m² per individual or household. This is a permissive starting point, intentionally designed to avoid disrupting the vast majority of Nepali families. By land area, 5,000 m² — approximately 1.5 *ropani* in Nepali measurement — is already substantially more land than most urban families in Kathmandu hold. The ceiling in Phase 1 is primarily targeted at large commercial landholders, absentee speculators, and the land mafia.

**Phase 2 (Years 6–15):** Maximum reduced to 2,500 m². This begins to affect mid-scale landholders with multiple urban plots.

**Phase 3 (Years 16–30):** Maximum reduced to 1,500 m².

**Phase 4 (Year 31 and beyond):** Maximum of 1,000 m² — approximately one *ropani*, enough for a comfortable urban house with a garden in the Nepali tradition. This is the steady-state ceiling.

The schedule is published in law, not subject to administrative discretion, and not adjustable without a supermajority parliamentary vote. This predictability is not a design quirk; it is the entire point. Landholders need time to make rational economic decisions about what to do with their surplus land. They need to be able to plan. Surprise expropriation — the historical failure mode of land reform, from Zimbabwe to the Soviet Union — is precisely what the ratcheting schedule is designed to avoid.

**The Surplus Land Protocol**

When a landholder's holdings exceed the applicable ceiling, what happens? Here is where the model diverges critically from earlier land reform efforts.

Surplus land is not sold on the open market. It is not auctioned. It is not redistributed to individual beneficiaries. It is acquired by a public body — let us call it the Land Trust Authority — at a fair but non-speculative price, determined by a formula: the average of the land's assessed value in the three years prior to the ratchet taking effect, plus a fixed percentage (e.g., 10–15%) as a transition premium.

The Land Trust Authority does not then resell the acquired land. It holds it permanently, as the Singaporean government holds the freehold of HDB sites. It designates the land for one of three uses:

1. **Permanent public housing** — non-market, non-speculative units allocated by need, not by ability to pay
2. **Community infrastructure** — schools, clinics, parks, transit corridors
3. **Community land trusts** — a hybrid model in which the land is held in trust by a community organisation, and individual households may build or purchase homes on long leases at below-market prices, with resale gains capped (so that the land remains affordable for future residents)

The critical distinction from all prior land reforms is this: **the land never re-enters the speculative market**. Once it is in public hands, it stays in public hands. This is not communism — households living in public housing units or CLT leasehold homes have secure, inheritable tenure, full rights of occupancy, and genuine stability of home. What they do not have is the right to sell the underlying land at a speculative profit to a private buyer. The land is permanently decommodified.

This distinction matters enormously. One of the recurring failures of land reform is that acquired land, distributed to beneficiaries, is sold back into the private market within a generation. The original beneficiaries sell under economic pressure — medical emergencies, school fees, business failures — and the land re-concentrates. Within thirty years, the pre-reform distribution is substantially restored. The only way to prevent this is to retain the land in public or community trust permanently, while securing the occupancy rights of residents as completely as if they owned the freehold.

**The Compensation Mechanism**

No model that does not address compensation for expropriated land will survive political contact with reality. We must take seriously the objection that the Ratcheting Land Ceiling constitutes a taking, and that the state owes landholders fair compensation.

ULCS accepts this obligation, with nuance. The key distinction, developed in Singapore's Land Acquisition Act and in various strands of Georgist theory, is between the value the landholder *created* — through improvements, maintenance, and legitimate economic activity — and the value society *created* — through public investment, population growth, infrastructure, and urban agglomeration. The former is genuinely the landholder's property, and should be compensated fully. The latter is the economic rent in George's sense — value created by society — and there is a strong philosophical argument that it was never genuinely the landholder's property to begin with.

In practice, ULCS proposes the following: compensation for land acquired above the ceiling is paid at assessed value, minus a progressive "social increment" discount that increases with holding size. For small surplus holdings just above the ceiling, 100% of assessed value is paid. For very large holdings — the top 1% of landholders — the discount on assessed value is larger, reflecting the larger proportion of rent in the total value. This is not double taxation. It is a recovery of value that the market itself acknowledged was never the owner's product.

Compensation is paid partly in cash and partly in government bonds — specifically, bonds that can be invested in productive enterprises (manufacturing, services, technology, agriculture) but cannot be used to purchase replacement land. This is the crucial lever: the capital liberated from land speculation by the ceiling is redirected, by design, into productive economic activity. The wealthy landholder who loses 3,000 m² of urban land above the ceiling and receives NRs 50 million in mixed cash and bonds is not impoverished. They are *redirected* — pushed from the passive accumulation of land rents into active participation in the productive economy.

This is, we argue, good for them as well as for society. The 1.7% rental yield on Kathmandu land is, after all, a catastrophically poor return on capital. A properly functioning economy with competitive factor markets should be able to offer productive investment returns of 8–12% to entrepreneurs and savers. The land ceiling does not take wealth; it *mobilises* it.

### 3.2 Permanent Public Housing as Infrastructure

The second pillar of ULCS is the construction, on the land acquired through the ceiling mechanism, of permanent public housing — shelter treated not as a commodity, but as infrastructure.

The distinction between housing as commodity and housing as infrastructure is not semantic. Infrastructure is the argument for a road, a water pipe, a power grid. No one suggests that access to roads should be rationed by ability to pay, or that electricity should be denied to those who cannot afford a market rate. Roads and pipes and grids are understood as the preconditions for all other economic activity, and they are therefore provided universally and funded collectively.

Housing is as much a precondition for productive economic participation as a road. A person without stable shelter cannot maintain employment, cannot educate their children, cannot maintain their health, cannot plan for the future. The entire edifice of human capital — the investment in education, in health, in professional development, in the social networks that generate economic opportunity — depends on a stable address. This is not a liberal claim or a conservative claim. It is an observation about the structure of human cognition and social organisation.

ULCS public housing, modelled on the best elements of the Singapore HDB system and adapted for the Nepali context, would have the following characteristics:

**Non-market allocation.** Units are allocated on the basis of need — household size, income, years on a waiting list, current housing precarity — not on ability to pay. The waiting list is universal: any citizen may apply. There are no income *ceilings* on eligibility (the classic means-testing failure mode). The formula is: do you need a home? You are eligible. The income-based component is only in the *subsidy level*, not in the *eligibility* for housing itself.

**Long-term leasehold tenure.** Residents hold 50-year renewable leases on their units. The lease is inheritable by immediate family members. It grants full rights of occupancy, modification (within structural limits), and subletting under defined rules. What it does not grant is the right to sell the unit at market price, or to access the underlying land value as a financial asset. This is the trade-off: security and affordability, in exchange for decommodification.

**Mixed income by design.** Following Singapore's model, ULCS estates are not poverty silos. Market-rate rental units and below-market-rate leasehold units coexist in the same buildings and precincts. This is not tokenism; it is the mechanism by which stigma is eliminated and community resources — commercial activity, social services, political voice — are shared.

**Urban integration.** ULCS housing is not built in peripheral locations where land is cheap. It is built *in the city*, on the acquired land that has been returned to public ownership. The goal is proximity to employment, education, and transit — the essential goods that make urban housing worth having. Building public housing far from employment centres, as was done repeatedly in European postwar housing policy, simply moves the poverty and adds the commute.

**Seismic and environmental standards.** In the Kathmandu context specifically, all ULCS housing is constructed to stringent seismic standards — the standards that speculative private construction routinely ignores because land costs crowd out construction budgets. Public housing, financed collectively rather than squeezed from commercial margins, can afford to be built properly.

### 3.3 Universal Social Floors — Replacing the Welfare Cliff

The third pillar of ULCS is the replacement of means-tested welfare programmes with a set of universal social floors — minimum guarantees available to all citizens without income testing, asset testing, or bureaucratic adjudication.

This is not universal basic income in the cash transfer sense, though it is compatible with UBI. It is, more precisely, what some theorists call Universal Basic Services: a guarantee that certain fundamental goods will be available to everyone, regardless of income, so that no citizen ever faces the welfare cliff effect of losing more in benefits than they gain in earnings.

The universal social floors we propose are:

**Universal Healthcare.** This is the most tractable and most broadly accepted of the universal floors. Nepal's constitution already formally guarantees universal healthcare; the challenge is in implementation and financing. ULCS's land value taxation provides the fiscal base to move this from constitutional aspiration to operational reality.

**Universal Education.** Pre-school through secondary school, genuinely free and genuinely universal — not merely nominally free with hidden costs of uniforms, books, and informal payments. The financing mechanism is again the land value tax.

**Universal Housing Access.** As described in the previous section — not a cash transfer, but a direct provision. The state guarantees that no citizen sleeps rough or lives in a genuinely unsafe structure for want of affordable alternative. The guarantee is met through the ULCS housing stock.

**Universal Nutritional Floor.** A food security programme providing a basic caloric guarantee through a combination of direct provision (community kitchens, school meals) and a modest food credit usable at registered retailers. The critical design choice is universality: the credit is available to all, not means-tested, so there is no cliff and no stigma.

**Universal Childcare.** Heavily subsidised, universally available childcare is the social policy with arguably the strongest evidence base for long-term economic returns. Every rigorous cost-benefit analysis of universal early childhood education finds returns exceeding 10:1 in reduced criminal justice costs, improved educational outcomes, and increased female labour force participation. It is, on the numbers, the cheapest thing a government can do. The universality is essential: means-tested childcare creates cliffs that specifically punish single parents returning to work.

These floors are floors, not ceilings. They define the minimum; private alternatives remain available and legal. A citizen who chooses private healthcare, private school, or private housing is free to do so. But they will never be *forced* to do so by inability to access the universal alternative. And crucially: they will never lose the universal floor by earning an additional rupee.

This design feature — the elimination of the cliff — is not merely a kindness. It is an economic efficiency gain. The behavioural distortions created by means-tested welfare — the decision to work fewer hours, to decline promotions, to hide income — are not moral failures. They are rational responses to a perverse incentive structure. ULCS eliminates the structure by making the social floor unconditional.

### 3.4 Resource-Based Financing

The fourth pillar is the financing of the system. Where does the money come from?

ULCS is financed primarily through three mechanisms, each of which captures a form of economic rent — value created by society rather than by individual effort — and returns it to public use.

**Land Value Tax.** Following the Georgist blueprint, a recurring annual charge on the assessed unimproved value of land, separate from any tax on buildings or improvements. The rate is progressive by land area held: households holding less than 100 m² (the majority of the population) are exempt or pay minimal rates. The rate rises steeply for holdings approaching and exceeding the ceiling, creating a direct financial incentive to use land productively rather than hold it speculatively. The evidence base for land value taxation's economic efficiency is robust: it is one of the few taxes that all schools of economic thought — from Keynesians to Friedmanites — agree is non-distortionary.

**Land Holding Surcharge.** A supplementary charge, above the standard LVT rate, on holdings within 20% of the applicable ceiling. This charge is designed to tip the marginal calculation toward voluntary sale (to the Land Trust Authority) rather than ceiling violation. It makes the tax cost of holding land near the ceiling high enough that most rational landholders choose to sell proactively, rather than waiting for mandatory acquisition.

**Development Value Capture.** When public investment — a new metro line, a ring road extension, a school, a park — raises the value of nearby land, ULCS captures a portion of that value uplift for the public. This is sometimes called "betterment levy" or "developer contribution." The mechanism is simple: at the point of land transaction, a percentage of the difference between pre-improvement value and post-improvement value is paid to the Land Trust Authority. Singapore's entire HDB system was substantially financed by this mechanism in its early decades.

**Administrative Savings.** Replacing multiple overlapping means-tested programmes with universal floors eliminates enormous administrative overheads. Nepal's welfare bureaucracy — the case workers, the eligibility assessors, the fraud investigators, the appeals tribunals — is not as large as in OECD countries, but the savings from simplification are real and should be counted against the cost of universal provision.

The financing question is legitimate and important. We are not proposing something for nothing. We are proposing a reallocation of value — specifically, the economic rent of urban land, which is currently being captured by private landholders, and which under ULCS would be captured by the public. The question is not whether there is enough value to fund the system; the Kathmandu land market is generating hundreds of billions of rupees in capital gains annually that currently accrue to a small number of owners. The question is whether the political will exists to redirect it.

## Part 4: Nepal's Land Reform History — The Lessons That Make Us Humble

### 4.1 The Pre-1950 Landscape

Before proposing solutions, we owe the history of those who tried before us. Nepal has been attempting land reform for more than a century, with a consistent pattern: reform announced, reform diluted, reform evaded, reform reversed. Understanding why is essential to designing something that does not repeat the pattern.

In the pre-modern Nepali state, land tenure was organised around a series of grant systems that concentrated ownership with the ruling class. *Birta* land was granted by the king, tax-free, to soldiers, nobles, and favoured families who had aided the expansion of the Gorkha kingdom under Prithvi Narayan Shah. *Jagir* land was assigned to state functionaries in lieu of monetary salary — a system that made landownership a direct precondition of state service. *Rakam* was a temporary assignment lasting until death or termination of employment. *Guthi* land was held by religious and cultural institutions.

The result was a tenure system entirely organised around the interests of the ruling class. The vast majority of the rural population were tenants, sharecroppers, or landless labourers with no legal rights to the land they cultivated. In the hills, the Limbu people held *Kipat* land — communal tenure under which land belonged to the community rather than to individuals — and this represented one of the few significant concentrations of non-elite land control in the country.

The Rana regime (1846–1950) took land as the primary instrument of political reward and control, increasing the concentration of ownership through systematic expansion of the Birta and Jagir systems. By the mid-twentieth century, the distribution of land in Nepal was among the most unequal in South Asia.

### 4.2 The Reform Era: 1950–1964

The restoration of democracy in 1950 opened the first serious window for land reform. The first major legislative attempt was the *Land Act 1957*, which focused on security of tenure for tenants. The *Birta Abolition Act 1957* converted all Birta holdings into taxable *Raikar* land — land on which taxes are payable to the government — eliminating the feudal tax-free status of elite holdings. This was, on paper, a significant reform.

In practice, implementation was slow, corrupt, and systematically undermined by the political power of those whose interests it threatened. The *Lands Act 1964*, promulgated under King Mahendra after he had abolished the multiparty system and instituted the Panchayat system, was the most comprehensive land reform legislation Nepal had seen. It abolished the Jimidari system of tax-collecting intermediaries, introduced land ceilings for the first time, established tenancy rights, and created mechanisms for acquiring excess land.

But even Mahendra's reform illustrates the central structural problem: the Land Act 1964 was announced but "not imposed at once." The king "made some loopholes to make necessary arrangements for Jamindars" — the landholding class. Compulsory saving provisions were abandoned due to corruption. The fourth amendment's provision for registering tenancy was underused because "a large number of tenants were not able to register due to lack of education and information." Around 450,000 unregistered tenants were left without protection.

The ceiling provisions of the Lands Act 1964 specified maximum landholdings that varied by geographic location. In Kathmandu and other municipalities, the ceiling was set at 25 *ropani* (approximately 12,000 m²). In the hills, it was higher. But landholders had advance notice of the reform, and the most common response was a variant of a theme that has played out in every land reform from India to Brazil: *benami* transactions, transferring excess holdings to family members, shell entities, and trusted associates in order to remain formally below the ceiling while retaining effective control.

### 4.3 The Maoist Period and After

The Maoist insurgency that began in 1996 and ended with the 2006 Comprehensive Peace Agreement was driven, in large part, by land grievances in the hills and Terai. The Maoists established "people's governments" in rural areas that carried out their own land redistributions, often forcibly. Prime Minister Sher Bahadur Deuba, during the insurgency period in 2002, announced radical land reform proposals — in part to counter Maoist mobilisation. The Jana Aandolan II of 2006, which ended the monarchy and established Nepal's federal democratic republic, produced a new constitution with strong property rights provisions and extensive social rights guarantees, including the right to food, housing, and education.

None of this produced a functioning land reform. The fundamental obstacles remained: the political class was too closely intertwined with the landholding class; the implementation machinery was too weak, too corrupt, and too easily captured; and no legislation addressed the urban land speculation problem that has emerged as the dominant form of land concentration in twenty-first century Nepal.

About 1 million families — approximately a quarter of Nepal's households — remain landless or face serious land access issues, according to land rights organisations. Even after sixty-plus years of nominal reform efforts, the Birta system is not fully resolved: in Nuwakot district alone, there were families still ploughing Birta land as late as 2020.

The lesson is not that land reform cannot work. It is that land reform without a permanent institutional structure — a Land Trust Authority that holds acquired land in perpetuity rather than redistributing it into the private market — will be undone within a generation. The ratchet must have teeth. The land, once acquired, must not re-enter the speculative market. And the political coalition for the reform must include enough people with a direct personal stake in its success that it cannot be reversed.

### 4.4 Why the Current Ceiling Fails

Nepal's current Land Act still technically contains ceiling provisions. They are widely ignored. The Kathmandu Valley's land market has continued to inflate at 27.7% per year without meaningful intervention. Why?

First, the current ceiling is too high for the Kathmandu context. A 25-*ropani* ceiling (approximately 12,500 m²) encompasses almost no urban landholders. The richest urban speculator in Kathmandu holds hundreds of small plots, each individually below the ceiling, rather than a single large holding above it. The current law has no mechanism to aggregate related-party holdings, no beneficial ownership register that would make *benami* transactions traceable, and no anti-avoidance provisions adequate to modern commercial law.

Second, compensation for acquired land has historically been set at government "assessed" value — which in Nepal, as in many South Asian countries, is systematically understated relative to market value. This creates two problems simultaneously: it is insufficiently attractive to incentivise voluntary sales, and it generates political opposition from landholders who correctly perceive that they are being undercompensated relative to what the market would pay.

Third, the acquired land has typically been redistributed into private ownership rather than retained in public trust. Distribution to individual beneficiaries — while equitable in intention — simply replicates the problem one generation later.

ULCS addresses all three of these failures: it aggregates related-party holdings, sets compensation at market-related values with a progressive social increment discount, and retains acquired land permanently in public trust.

## Part 5: The Universality Argument — Why This Is Not Just a Nepal Story

### 5.1 The Universal Mechanism

The pathology we are diagnosing in Kathmandu is not specific to Kathmandu. It is a general mechanism, operating in every city that combines finite land supply with growing population and a speculative financial system. The specific numbers differ. The cultural context differs. The political history differs. But the underlying logic — land as a perfectly inelastic asset being bid up by a self-reinforcing speculative loop — is universal.

Seoul's apartment prices have increased by more than 50% in nominal terms since 2017, with a particularly sharp spike during 2020–2021. The Moon Jae-in government introduced more than twenty separate policy interventions in the housing market between 2017 and 2021, most of them having the opposite effect of what was intended: restrictions on supply, combined with demand-stimulating low interest rates, produced price increases rather than decreases.

Auckland, New Zealand, had one of the world's most severe housing affordability crises in the early 2020s, with house price-to-income ratios reaching 10:1. The causes — restricted land supply through exclusionary zoning, speculative investment by domestic and offshore investors, and a tax system that treated housing gains as capital gains and taxed them more lightly than income — are structurally identical to Kathmandu's, despite the vastly different income levels.

Vancouver and Toronto in Canada have experienced similar dynamics, driven by the same combination of land supply constraint, speculative investment, and a financialised mortgage market that treats housing as the primary savings vehicle for the middle class. A 2024 study by the Bank of Canada found that a significant proportion of housing demand in major Canadian cities was coming from investors rather than owner-occupiers — people buying property not to live in it, but to hold as an appreciating asset.

London has been in a housing crisis for at least three decades. The mean home price in London is approximately £600,000, or roughly thirteen times the median London salary. The waiting list for social housing in London contains over 300,000 households, with average waiting times measured in years or decades. The city spends enormous sums on temporary accommodation for homeless families — often in poor-quality private rentals at rates that exceed the cost of building permanent public housing — because the political will to build public housing has been absent since the Thatcher government's Right to Buy programme began dismantling the public housing stock in the 1980s.

San Francisco's housing crisis is so well documented that it has become a global shorthand for the failure of progressive-leaning governance to address housing affordability. Median rents in San Francisco in 2024 exceeded USD 3,500 per month for a one-bedroom apartment. The city has chronically underbuilt housing for decades, hemmed in by exclusionary zoning, NIMBYist opposition to new development, and a planning process that makes construction of new homes extraordinarily slow and expensive. Thousands of people sleep rough on the streets of one of the wealthiest cities in human history.

The common thread is not poverty. San Francisco is not poor. London is not poor. Auckland is not poor. The common thread is the treatment of land and housing as investment vehicles rather than as the infrastructure of human settlement, combined with a failure to capture the economic rent of land for public use, combined with a means-tested welfare system that creates housing cliffs rather than universal housing security.

### 5.2 Adapting ULCS to Different Contexts

We have developed the ULCS model using Kathmandu as the primary case study, because the Kathmandu land crisis is particularly acute and because Nepal's history of attempted land reform provides unusually clear lessons about what works and what does not. But the model is explicitly designed to be universally applicable, with local adaptation.

**In high-income cities (London, San Francisco, Sydney):**

The ceiling numbers would be vastly different — in London, a ceiling of 5,000 m² for private residential land would affect essentially no one; a more appropriate ceiling might be 2,000 m² with progressive charges on anything above 500 m² for a single household. The land value tax rate would be higher in absolute terms, because land values are higher, generating correspondingly greater public revenue. The public housing stock would be built on acquired land in the most desirable urban locations — the explicit rejection of the peripheral-siting failure of postwar council estates.

The universality of the social floors is more important in high-income countries than in low-income ones, counterintuitively, because the welfare cliffs in high-income countries are often more severe in their behavioural distortions. A single mother in London faces benefit cliff effects that are as financially devastating, relative to her marginal income, as those faced by her counterpart in Kathmandu — even though her absolute income is many times higher.

**In middle-income cities (Seoul, Istanbul, Cairo, Nairobi):**

The ceiling numbers and compensation formulas need to account for different levels of financial market development, different property registration systems, and different levels of informal land tenure. In many African and Asian cities, a significant proportion of urban land is held under customary tenure without formal registration. ULCS must engage with informal tenure — it cannot simply ignore it and claim to be a universal model. The approach here is to convert informal tenure into formal leasehold tenure *at the time of ceiling application*, establishing legal rights for existing occupants before imposing the ceiling, so that the ceiling does not inadvertently dispossess the very people it is meant to help.

**In low-income contexts (rural Nepal, sub-Saharan Africa, South Asian villages):**

Agricultural land raises different questions than urban land. The productive value of agricultural land — its capacity to feed families and generate livelihoods — is not the same as the speculative value of urban plots. The ceiling for agricultural land should be set differently: high enough to allow genuinely productive farming at scale (excluding smallholders from any burden), with the primary target being absentee landlordism and feudal holdover tenures like the Birta system. The public use of acquired agricultural land should prioritise redistribution to landless cultivators under secure long-term leases — not full freehold ownership, to prevent the one-generation re-concentration problem, but secure enough that farmers will invest in the land's productivity.

## Part 6: The Political Economy — How This Gets Built and How It Survives

### 6.1 The Coalition Problem

Any political proposal must answer the question: who supports it, and is that coalition large enough and durable enough to implement and sustain the reform?

The coalition for ULCS is, in principle, large. The majority of any urban population does not own significant land. The median household in Kathmandu, in Seoul, in London, in Auckland is a renter or a small owner-occupier holding well below any reasonable ceiling. These people have a direct, material interest in a policy that lowers housing costs, provides universal security, and transfers land value from speculative to productive uses.

The coalition is also inclusive across what are often assumed to be ideological divides. Conservatives in the Chestertonian tradition should be attracted to ULCS: it promotes widespread ownership (every household in a ULCS public housing unit has a secure, inheritable leasehold), it eliminates bureaucratic dependency (the welfare cliff and the degrading means test), and it champions the productive entrepreneur over the passive rentier. Progressives should be attracted to its explicit redistribution of wealth from the asset-owning class to the rest of society, and to its provision of universal social floors that the most vulnerable members of society can rely on without stigma. Libertarians of the Georgist variety should be attracted to the land value tax component, which is among the most economically efficient fiscal instruments available.

In practice, the coalition for ULCS faces one enormously powerful opponent: the existing landowning class, which in most societies is also the politically connected class, the donor class, and the class with the greatest capacity for sustained political mobilisation. This is not a new observation. It explains why every land reform in history has either been driven by genuinely revolutionary political change (the American occupation of Japan and South Korea, which broke the feudal landlord class; the founding of Singapore, which gave the PAP government the authority and urgency to act decisively) or has been diluted to irrelevance by the political power of those it threatened.

ULCS does not pretend that this obstacle is easy to overcome. It acknowledges it directly and proposes several design features intended to manage it.

### 6.2 The Ratchet as Political Technology

The ratcheting schedule — the published, legally committed, multi-decade timeline for reducing the ceiling — is not merely an economic design feature. It is a *political* design feature.

By giving landholders a long time horizon — twenty or thirty years — to adjust their holdings, ULCS reduces the political intensity of the opposition at each individual phase transition. A landholder who knows that the ceiling will reach 1,000 m² in thirty years has time to make rational economic choices: to sell, to develop, to reinvest. They are not being expropriated tomorrow. Their opposition, while real, is distributed over time rather than concentrated at a single crisis point.

This is the mechanism that has made progressive income tax politically sustainable despite its redistributive nature. No government announces that it will immediately tax all incomes above a certain level at 90%. Rates are adjusted incrementally, with debate, with advance notice, with adjustment mechanisms. The ratchet applies the same logic to land.

### 6.3 The Universality as Political Technology

The universal social floors are not merely good policy. They are smart politics.

When every citizen has a direct stake in the ULCS public housing stock — because they might need it, because their children might need it, because their parents might be in it — the political coalition for maintaining and improving it is the entire citizenry, not just the poor. The Singapore experience demonstrates this with almost experimental clarity: the ruling party's political durability is directly tied to the quality and availability of HDB housing. Politicians who improve public housing get credit from the majority of the population. Politicians who cut public housing face opposition from the majority. The incentive structure is the opposite of means-tested housing: instead of incentivising politicians to cut benefits to signal fiscal virtue, it incentivises them to improve services because most voters use them.

### 6.4 The Institutional Design

The Land Trust Authority must be designed to be as insulated from political interference as the central bank. This is not because its activities are technocratic in the way that monetary policy is — land policy involves genuine value choices that require democratic legitimacy — but because the primary threat to its mission is capture by the interests it is meant to constrain.

Institutional safeguards for the LTA should include: fixed terms for board members appointed through a supermajority parliamentary process; a constitutional mandate that acquired land may not be sold to private parties; annual public reporting with independent audit; and a mandatory public consultation process before any change to the ceiling schedule.

The community land trust component of the LTA's portfolio provides an additional institutional safeguard: CLTs are governed by their residents, who have a direct personal interest in maintaining affordability. Unlike state housing bodies, which can be captured by bureaucratic inertia or political pressure from above, CLTs represent a distributed, decentralised power base for the maintenance of the ULCS mission.

## Part 7: Case Studies — Stories from the Ground

### 7.1 The Speculator's Reckoning: A Fictional Account Grounded in Documented Reality

Consider a composite character — let us call him Ram Prasad Shrestha — who represents a type that is real in Kathmandu, documented in the news coverage, and numerically significant in the valley's economy.

Ram Prasad is sixty-two. He inherited 3 *ropani* of land in Lalitpur from his father in 1995, when the family had farmed it for generations. In 1995, the land was worth about NPR 500,000 — enough for a modest house, but not a fortune. Ram Prasad is an educated man, a former civil servant who took early retirement in 2003. He had watched, through the 1990s and 2000s, as the land values around him rose. In 2003, he took a bank loan against his land's collateral value and bought two more plots in Bhaktapur. In 2007, he bought three more in Kirtipur. He has not built on any of them. He rents two of the plots to parking operators and collects the modest income. The rest are vacant.

By 2025, Ram Prasad's six plots are worth, in aggregate, approximately NPR 200 million. His annual rental income is approximately NPR 1.5 million — a yield of 0.75%, meaningfully below even the 1.7% average. He pays minimal land revenue tax, calculated at historical assessed values that are a fraction of market prices. He is "rich" in the sense that his balance sheet shows NPR 200 million in assets and NPR 30 million in remaining bank debt. He is also, in functional economic terms, a drain: his land sits unproductive, priced beyond what any developer can build on at returns that justify the land cost.

Under ULCS, Ram Prasad's experience over the next thirty years would be as follows:

Phase 1 (years 1–5): The ceiling is 5,000 m². Ram Prasad holds approximately 6,300 m² across all plots, including the ancestral Lalitpur land. He is above the ceiling by approximately 1,300 m². He receives formal notification from the Land Trust Authority. He has five years to reduce his holding to 5,000 m² by voluntary sale to the LTA, at assessed value plus a 15% transition premium. The land holding surcharge on his holdings above 4,000 m² begins accumulating at 3% of assessed value annually.

Ram Prasad calculates: the surcharge is growing. The assessed values are being updated annually to market-reference levels. Over five years, holding onto the excess land will cost him approximately NPR 12 million in surcharges. The LTA's offer of assessed value plus premium works out to approximately NPR 25 million for the 1,300 m² he needs to divest. He sells to the LTA in year three.

The NPR 25 million arrives partly as cash (NPR 10 million) and partly as government productive investment bonds (NPR 15 million). Ram Prasad, nudged by his accountant, invests the bonds in a local manufacturing cooperative — a textile firm that his nephew has been trying to capitalise for years. The bonds pay 9% interest.

Phase 2 (years 6–15): The ceiling reduces to 2,500 m². Ram Prasad's remaining holding is 5,000 m², still significantly above the new ceiling. The process repeats. He divests another 2,500 m² to the LTA, receives compensation, and redirects it into productive investment. By year 15, Ram Prasad holds exactly 2,500 m² — his ancestral Lalitpur plot and one of the smaller Kirtipur parcels.

Phase 4 (year 31 onward): The final ceiling is 1,000 m². Ram Prasad's son, who has inherited his holdings, holds 2,500 m². He divests down to 1,000 m² — keeping the ancestral home — and reinvests the proceeds.

What has been lost? Ram Prasad no longer holds NPR 200 million in unproductive land. What has been gained? He and his family have received fair compensation, progressively redirected into productive enterprise. The ten plots he formerly held have been developed into 240 ULCS housing units — modest but seismically sound, connected to transit, occupied by 240 families who now have secure, affordable housing. The LTA's land value tax on the remaining private landholdings in the neighbourhood generates NPR 80 million annually in public revenue, funding the universal healthcare centre that opened in Lalitpur last year.

This is not a fairy tale. It is an arithmetic exercise. The numbers work. The question is whether the political will is present to run them.

### 7.2 The Migrant's Return: The Housing Cliff Eliminated

Consider Sita Tamang, thirty-four, who returned to Kathmandu in 2023 after six years working in Qatar. She sent home NPR 2 million in remittances during those six years — money that fed her parents, put her younger brother through engineering school, and paid for her mother's cataract surgery. She has come home to build a life.

In the current system, Sita faces the following situation: she has NPR 300,000 in savings. A two-room apartment in a safe part of Kathmandu requires a deposit equivalent to two months' rent (NPR 30,000–50,000), plus rent of NPR 25,000–35,000 per month. Her job search has produced an offer at NPR 35,000 per month — a reasonable professional salary in Kathmandu. After rent, utilities, food, and transport, she has approximately NPR 2,000–5,000 left over each month. She is one medical emergency, one vehicle repair, one job loss from destitution.

She qualifies, nominally, for a government housing programme — but the waiting list is five to seven years. She qualifies for a government health insurance scheme — but the nearest participating clinic is forty-five minutes away by bus. She does not qualify for food assistance because her income is just above the means-test threshold.

Under ULCS, Sita's situation is transformed not by giving her more money, but by changing the *floor* beneath her:

She applies for the universal housing waiting list — not a means-tested programme but a universal one. She waits eighteen months — still too long, but the wait time is falling as the ULCS housing stock grows. She moves into a two-bedroom ULCS unit in a mixed-income estate in Koteshwor. Her monthly housing cost is NPR 8,000, representing approximately 23% of her income. (The target ratio under ULCS is no more than 25% of net income for housing, which is internationally standard.)

The universal healthcare floor means she registers at the neighbourhood health centre — walking distance from her flat — without insurance verification or income testing. Her first health check in six years is free. A preventive care visit she would have delayed under the old system happens on schedule.

The universal nutritional floor provides a food credit worth NPR 5,000 per month, available regardless of her employment status. This is not means-tested; it does not cliff if she gets a raise. It is a permanent feature of citizenship, like access to a public park.

The net effect on Sita's economic behaviour is significant. She does not decline the NPR 40,000 job that was offered to her, as she might have done under the old system (where the income increase would have pushed her off various benefits). She takes it. She saves. She starts saving for the small tailoring business she has been planning. Within three years, she employs two other women.

This is the welfare cliff eliminated by universal design. The floor does not fall when she earns more. It stays where it is. And because it stays where it is, she behaves like a person with a floor beneath her — she takes risks, she invests, she contributes.

### 7.3 The Earthquake That Doesn't Kill as Many People

The 2015 earthquake killed approximately 9,000 people and displaced millions. The rebuilding process — still incomplete a decade later — was hampered by the same land market dynamics that produced the tragedy: land costs that crowded out building quality, informal tenure that made reconstruction financing impossible for squatters and informal settlers, and a lack of public land on which to build transitional or permanent housing.

Under ULCS, the valley's housing stock would, over thirty years, be progressively shifted toward publicly owned, seismically certified structures. The Land Trust Authority would, by construction, own significant quantities of land distributed throughout the urban fabric — not concentrated on the periphery, not in flood plains, but in the places where people actually need to live and work. When the next major earthquake comes — and it will come; the Kathmandu Valley sits atop one of the most seismically active zones on earth — the death toll in ULCS housing will be measurably lower than in the speculative private stock.

This is not a marginal benefit. It is a life-and-death difference, materialised in the bodies of people who will not die because they were not living in a structure whose construction quality was sacrificed to land cost.

## Part 8: Objections and Responses

No serious policy proposal can avoid its critics. We engage here with the strongest objections to ULCS.

### 8.1 "This is socialism / communism / expropriation"

The objection takes various forms, but its core is the claim that ULCS represents an illegitimate taking of private property.

The response begins with a philosophical distinction that Henry George made clearly in 1879, and which has been refined by subsequent legal theory: there is a difference between property rights in the *products of one's labour and investment*, and claims over the *unearned increment of land value*. A building is the product of labour and investment. The value of the land beneath the building is, in a city, primarily the product of the surrounding society — of public investment in roads, schools, transit, and commerce.

A landholder in Kathmandu who paid NPR 5 million for a plot in 2010 and has watched it grow to NPR 30 million by 2025 without doing anything has not *earned* that NPR 25 million increase. They have *collected* it — collected the value produced by Nepal's public infrastructure spending, by the labour of the migrants who built the roads, by the teachers who educated the workforce that made Kathmandu a desirable location, by the entirely unrelated economic growth of South Asia. The collection of this unearned value is what George called "economic rent," and ULCS proposes to progressively recapture it for the public.

This is not expropriation in the sense of taking what someone has built or earned. It is the correction of an existing structural expropriation: the ongoing private capture of publicly created value.

Furthermore, ULCS pays compensation. Singapore's Land Acquisition Act paid compensation; it was specifically designed so that the compensation did not include the speculative uplift — the portion of value created by public investment — and this was its genius. ULCS follows the same logic: compensate the genuine private contribution to land value; reclaim the publicly created increment.

### 8.2 "The ceiling will be evaded, just like the 1964 Act"

This is the strongest empirical objection, and it deserves the most serious engagement. Nepal's history of land reform evasion — the *benami* transactions, the family partitions, the shell companies — is well documented and sobering.

ULCS addresses evasion through several mechanisms that were not available in 1964:

**Beneficial ownership registration.** Modern financial regulation has developed sophisticated techniques for piercing corporate veils and identifying beneficial owners. Nepal's recent anti-money laundering reforms, driven partly by FATF pressure, have begun building the institutional capacity for beneficial ownership registers. ULCS mandates a comprehensive beneficial ownership register for all land holdings, aggregating holdings across related parties (family members, affiliated companies, trust beneficiaries) for the purposes of applying the ceiling. What is spread across twenty plots and twelve family members counts as one holding for ceiling purposes.

**Digital land records.** Nepal's land administration has been moving toward digitisation of land records, with a national digital cadastre project underway. Digital records make evasion harder — not impossible, but significantly more costly — than the paper-based system in which the 1964 Act operated.

**Financial transaction monitoring.** Land transactions above a certain threshold are already required to be reported to Nepal Rastra Bank. ULCS extends this reporting requirement and links it to the beneficial ownership register, so that any attempt to fragment a holding by selling to a related party triggers automatic scrutiny.

**Whistleblower incentives.** ULCS establishes a reward mechanism for information leading to the detection of ceiling evasion — a percentage of the surcharge or penalty collected. This creates a distributed enforcement network that complements the state's own enforcement capacity.

None of these mechanisms is perfect. Evasion will occur. The question is whether evasion can be limited to a level where the reform still achieves its primary objectives. The answer depends on political will as much as institutional design — but the institutional design is, at minimum, vastly stronger than anything Nepal has attempted before.

### 8.3 "Universal provision is unaffordable"

The affordability objection is important and requires a genuine quantitative response.

The land value tax on Kathmandu valley land, if levied at 2% annually on assessed market values, would generate revenues in the range of NPR 40–80 billion per year, depending on the accuracy of the assessed values relative to market prices. This is before counting development value capture, which historically generates substantial revenues in rapidly urbanising contexts. This is before counting administrative savings from the elimination of means-tested programmes.

NPR 40–80 billion is a large number in the Nepali fiscal context: Nepal's total government expenditure in FY 2024/25 was approximately NPR 1.79 trillion. The LVT revenues alone would represent 2–4% of national expenditure — enough to fund a meaningful expansion of universal healthcare and education, though not everything we have proposed simultaneously.

The scaling argument is important: ULCS is not proposed as a big-bang transformation. It is a phased reform whose fiscal capacity grows as the land value tax base is progressively improved (assessed values brought to market levels), the ceiling ratchet reduces the concentration of land in tax-exempt or undertaxed hands, and the development value capture system matures. By Phase 4 (year 31+), the steady-state ULCS fiscal system should be generating revenues sufficient to fund all four universal floors at a level comparable to what Singapore spends on HDB and the basic social safety net — approximately 3–5% of GDP.

The comparison to the status quo is also important. Nepal currently spends enormous sums accommodating the consequences of its land failure: temporary housing for earthquake-displaced families, healthcare costs for preventable diseases associated with housing precarity, social assistance for the urban poor that is inefficient, stigmatising, and inadequate. ULCS replaces all of these with a more efficient and more effective universal system. The *marginal* cost of ULCS relative to the status quo is lower than the gross cost suggests.

### 8.4 "Public housing means bad housing"

The objection is empirical: public housing in many countries has been a disaster. The American housing projects. The French *banlieues*. The brutalist towers of British local authorities that became crime-ridden poverty warehouses. Is there any reason to believe Nepal's public housing would be different?

The Singapore answer is direct: yes. Not because Singapore has some unique cultural magic, but because Singapore made a specific set of design choices that its failed counterparts did not:

Mixed income by design. No income ceiling on eligibility. Investment in common amenities and community spaces. Integration with transit and employment. Professional management with adequate resources. And, crucially: a political system in which the ruling party's legitimacy was directly staked on the quality of public housing, creating a powerful ongoing incentive for continuous improvement.

ULCS adopts each of these design choices explicitly. The income-eligibility universality is not incidental to the model; it is load-bearing. If ULCS housing becomes housing only for the very poor — if middle-class and professional households opt out into the private market en masse — the political coalition for maintaining and improving it collapses, and the quality degrades in the predictable direction. Universality is what keeps politicians accountable and what keeps the buildings maintained.

### 8.5 "This concentrates too much power in the state"

The concern about state power is legitimate, particularly given Nepal's history of political instability, corruption, and institutional capture. Giving a Land Trust Authority the power to acquire and hold vast quantities of urban land is a concentration of power that carries risks.

ULCS addresses this through several institutional design features: the constitutional mandate preventing land sale (which limits the LTA's power to give land away or sell it under political pressure); the community land trust governance structure (which distributes decision-making to resident communities); the mandatory public audit and parliamentary oversight; and the long ratchet timeline (which prevents any single government from rushing through acquisitions that benefit its allies).

More fundamentally: the *current* system is also a concentration of power — in the hands of the land-owning class, the land mafia, and the banking sector. The choice is not between concentrated power and dispersed power. It is between power concentrated in ways that serve the many (the public institution, subject to democratic accountability) and power concentrated in ways that serve the few (the speculative class, accountable to no one).

## Part 9: The Transition Path — From Here to There

### 9.1 The First Five Years

The first five years of ULCS implementation are the most politically sensitive. The legislation establishing the Land Trust Authority, the ceiling schedule, and the land value tax must be passed in a single parliamentary act — not incrementally, because incremental passage invites amendment and delay at each stage. The full package, with the ratchet schedule inscribed in the law rather than left to administrative discretion, must be put to a vote as an integrated whole.

The political window for such legislation is opened by crisis: an earthquake, a banking sector crisis triggered by the property bubble, a wave of evictions following a market correction, or a youth-led political movement whose primary demand is housing. Nepal has experienced all of these in the past decade. Youth-led anti-corruption protests in September 2025 resulted in the resignation of the prime minister and snap elections in March 2026. Political instability can be the enemy of reform — but it can also be the window through which reform becomes possible, if a coalition is ready.

In the first five years, the LTA focuses on: building the beneficial ownership register; updating land assessments to market reference values; beginning the collection of the land holding surcharge on above-ceiling holdings; and using the land acquired in the earliest voluntary sales to break ground on the first ULCS housing estates. The first estates are built in high-visibility locations — not peripheral sites but inner urban land — to demonstrate that public housing need not mean second-class housing.

The universal healthcare floor is the first of the social floors to be operationalised, because it has the broadest political support and the most developed existing institutional infrastructure. Universal education follows, because it builds on Nepal's existing public school system. Housing access is third, and grows as the LTA's housing stock accumulates. Nutritional and childcare floors come last, as the fiscal revenues grow.

### 9.2 The Role of International Models and Technical Assistance

Nepal does not need to invent this system from scratch. Singapore's HDB has received numerous international visitors and provides training to housing practitioners globally. The Singaporean government, UNHABITAT, and the World Bank have all documented the HDB model in extensive technical detail.

Community land trust models — the hybrid ownership structure that combines public land retention with individual security of tenure — have been developed extensively in the United States, the United Kingdom, Kenya, and Australia, and the technical templates are freely available. The Community Land Trust Network (UK) and the National Community Land Trust Network (US) both provide detailed model legislation, governance frameworks, and operational guidance.

The land value tax is arguably the most studied fiscal instrument in economics, with implementation experience across dozens of jurisdictions ranging from tiny Altoona, Pennsylvania to the entirety of Estonia. The Institute for Fiscal Studies, the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy, and numerous academic research centres have produced detailed implementation guides.

There is no technical mystery here. The knowledge exists. The models exist. The only missing ingredient is political will — and as we have argued, political will can be created by the right combination of crisis, coalition, and institutional design.

## Part 10: What Success Looks Like — A 30-Year Vision

### 10.1 Kathmandu in 2056

In thirty years, if ULCS is successfully implemented, Kathmandu valley looks like this:

The land market still exists. Private land ownership is still legal, under the 1,000 m² ceiling. There are still wealthy Nepalis who own beautiful homes and commercial properties. The private economy is more vibrant, not less, because the capital that formerly circulated in speculative land transactions has been redirected into manufacturing, technology, agriculture, and services. The entrepreneurship that was "killed" by the land market has been revived.

Approximately 35–40% of the valley's housing stock is ULCS public housing or community land trust leasehold housing. This represents hundreds of thousands of units distributed throughout the city. They are not identifiable by their shabbiness or their isolation — they sit in the same buildings and neighbourhoods as private housing, maintained to the same standards, occupied by teachers and engineers and taxi drivers and civil servants alongside the very poor. There is no "public housing address" stigma.

Kathmandu's skyline is denser than it was in 2026 — but denser in the right places, driven by public development rather than by speculative extraction. The ring road does not have the sprawl of informal settlements that currently presses against it, because those families are housed. The Bagmati River corridor, which currently accommodates a grim succession of informal settlements in one of the city's most flood-prone and earthquake-vulnerable zones, has been cleared and replaced with riverfront public space and well-built public housing on the adjacent higher ground.

The seismic risk profile of the city has improved, measurably, because the proportion of the housing stock built to modern earthquake standards has risen. When the next major earthquake hits — and the geologists are clear that it will — the death toll will be lower than it would have been in the counterfactual. This is not a metaphor. This is measurable in lives.

The welfare cliff is gone. A worker who gets a raise does not lose housing benefits, healthcare access, or nutritional support. The administrative apparatus that previously managed eligibility for means-tested programmes has been substantially dismantled, and the civil servants who staffed it have been redeployed to the Land Trust Authority, the healthcare system, and the school system.

Nepal's Gini coefficient — its measure of income inequality — has fallen. Not to zero; inequality will always exist. But the *floor* has risen, and the *ceiling* is lower, and the gap between them reflects genuine differences in economic contribution rather than the accident of land inheritance.

### 10.2 The Global Replication

If the model works in Kathmandu — and there is every reason to believe it will, if implemented with institutional integrity — the demonstration effect matters enormously.

The housing crisis is, simultaneously, one of the most politically paralysing and one of the most economically consequential problems facing cities across the world. It is paralysing because the political economy appears intractable: landowners are concentrated, organised, and politically powerful; renters are diffuse, disorganised, and politically weak. It is consequential because housing costs consume a growing share of household budgets, suppress labour mobility, reduce birth rates, increase inequality, and divert capital from productive investment.

A successful ULCS implementation in one city would provide something enormously valuable: proof that it can be done. Not in theory. Not in a simulation. In practice, in a real city with real political constraints and real institutional challenges. Other cities would learn from it. Their reformers would point to it. Their politicians would use it as a shield against the charge that reform is impossible.

The ratchet would turn, city by city.

## Conclusion: The Floor Must Hold

There is a thought experiment that clarifies what is at stake. Imagine two cities. In the first city, a young woman who grows up in poverty has a reasonable expectation of affordable shelter, decent healthcare, adequate nutrition, and free education — regardless of whether she earns ten rupees or ten thousand. In this city, the floor holds. She can take risks. She can invest in herself. She can build something.

In the second city, that same woman lives one missed rent payment from the street. One illness from bankruptcy. One job loss from hunger. In this city, the floor does not hold. Rational behaviour in this city looks like paralysis: take no risks, hold no debts, trust no institutions, save every rupee as a buffer against the fall that may come at any moment.

Kathmandu is the second city. So is most of the world.

The argument of this article is not that ULCS is the only possible solution. Other configurations — a pure Georgist land value tax, a full Singapore-style HDB transplant, a community land trust network without the ceiling mechanism — might achieve some of the same goals through different institutional routes. What ULCS offers is a synthesis: the permanent removal of land from the speculative market (the ceiling), the public provision of housing as infrastructure (the LTA), the elimination of the welfare cliff (the universal floors), and the efficient financing of all of the above through the capture of economic rent (the LVT and surcharge).

What it requires, ultimately, is a political consensus that shelter is not a commodity. That a city is not a casino. That the value created by everyone — by public investment, by community density, by the accumulated choices of millions of people to live and work in a place — should benefit everyone, not accrue to the diminishing fraction who happened to get in first.

The land beneath our feet was not made by any of us. Its value was made by all of us. It is time to say so, in law.

The ratchet turns. The floor holds.
