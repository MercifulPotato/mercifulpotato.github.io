## The execution gap: the most important fact about Nepali budgets

We have referred to it repeatedly throughout this series, and now we confront it directly, because it is the central fact that determines whether any Nepali budget's promises become reality: **the execution gap** — the chronic, persistent difference between what the budget *plans* to spend and what the government actually *spends*, and between what it plans to collect and what it actually collects.

Recall the pieces we have assembled across the series. We learned in the posts on the budget's size and on recurrent versus capital that Nepal chronically fails to spend its *capital* budget — that a substantial share of the money allocated for building infrastructure goes unspent each year, the authority lapsing unused, so that the country builds far less than it budgets for. We learned that the recurrent spending — salaries, operations, interest — gets spent in full, because it is easy, while the capital spending lags, because building is hard, and that what capital spending does occur is often crammed into a wasteful, low-quality year-end rush. We learned in the revenue post that revenue has often fallen *short* of its targets, because the import-dependent tax base is fragile and the targets are set optimistically. And we learned, in the post on how budgets become law, that there is a long machinery between announcement and implementation in which promises routinely die.

Put these together and the execution gap is the sum of them: a persistent pattern in which the actual outcome of a Nepali budget — the money actually spent, the revenue actually collected, the projects actually completed, the promises actually delivered — falls *short* of the plan, often substantially, and in predictable ways. The capital budget is under-spent; the revenue target is missed; the ambitious promises are partly delivered at best; the budget shrinks at mid-year revision and shrinks again in actual outturn. This is not an occasional failure but a *chronic, structural* feature of Nepali public finance, repeated year after year across governments of every stripe. And it is the single most important fact for judging this budget's credibility, because it means that the realistic expectation for *any* Nepali budget — including this one — is that its actual achievement will fall meaningfully short of its plan: that not all the money will be spent, not all the revenue collected, not all the promises delivered, and that the gap will be concentrated in exactly the places (capital spending, ambitious new programmes) where the country can least afford the shortfall. So when we ask "can this government do what it has promised?", the execution gap supplies the sobering baseline answer: history strongly suggests that, like its predecessors, it will achieve considerably less than it has announced — not necessarily through bad faith, but through the chronic, structural failure of execution that has defeated every recent government's plans.

This is why this series has insisted, at the foot of every post, on the distinction between announced, enacted, and implemented, and on judging the budget by outcomes rather than announcements. The execution gap is the reason that distinction matters: because in Nepal, more than in many countries, the gap between the announced plan and the implemented reality is wide and reliable, so that to judge a budget by its announcements is to be systematically misled about what will actually happen. The credible expectation is set not by the speech but by the execution record, and the execution record says: expect significant shortfall.

## The opacity problem

A second concern about this budget's credibility, and about Nepali budgets generally, is *opacity* — the presence of large allocations that are not transparently specified, where it is unclear exactly what the money is for. This budget contains a notable example: a *miscellaneous* or *undisclosed* heading, associated with the finance ministry, on the order of **NPR 90.42 billion** (around USD 600 million) — a very large sum, larger than the entire allocation to many individual ministries, classified in a way that does not clearly specify what it will be spent on.

Why does opacity matter, and why should a citizen be skeptical of large miscellaneous allocations? Because the whole purpose of a budget, as we established at the beginning, is *transparency and accountability* — the budget is supposed to specify, openly, how public money will be raised and spent, so that the people's representatives and the public can scrutinise and hold the government to account. A large sum classified as "miscellaneous" or otherwise unspecified *defeats* this purpose for that portion of the money: it is public money whose intended use is not clearly disclosed, which cannot therefore be properly scrutinised, and which may be used with greater discretion and less accountability than transparently-specified spending. Large miscellaneous allocations are a concern in any budget because they reduce transparency, increase the scope for discretionary and potentially unaccountable use of public money, and make it harder for the public and the legislature to know and to judge what the money is actually doing. A sum of NPR 90.42 billion — around USD 600 million, a large amount in the context of this budget — classified opaquely is, on its face, a significant gap in the budget's transparency, and economists and analysts have rightly criticised such opacity. This is not to allege any specific misuse — the money may be intended for legitimate purposes that are simply not transparently specified — but to note that opacity *itself* is a problem, because it removes the scrutiny that is the budget's whole point, and because a citizen cannot judge or hold to account spending whose purpose is not disclosed. A budget's credibility and accountability are weakened by large opaque allocations, and a citizen should note them, ask what they are actually for, and be skeptical of public money that is not transparently specified — because transparency is the precondition for all the accountability that a budget is supposed to provide.

## The credibility gap: targets versus reality

A third concern is the gap between the budget's *targets* and recent *reality* — the question of whether the assumptions on which the budget rests are realistic or optimistic, because a budget built on unrealistic assumptions is a budget whose plans will not hold.

The clearest example, which we have noted throughout, is the *growth target*. The budget assumes economic growth of 7 percent for the coming year. But the economy, by the government's own statisticians' estimate, grew only about 3.85 percent in the year just ending — so the budget assumes the economy will grow at nearly *double* the rate it just achieved. Why does this matter so much? Because, as we learned in the revenue post, the budget's revenue projections, and therefore its whole financing plan, rest on assumptions about growth: a faster-growing economy generates more taxable activity and more revenue, so a high growth assumption supports a high revenue projection, which makes the deficit look smaller and the plan look more affordable. If the growth assumption is unrealistic — if the economy grows at something closer to its recent 3.85 percent than the assumed 7 percent — then the revenue will likely fall short, the deficit will widen, and the budget will have to be cut at mid-year or financed by additional borrowing. So the gap between the 7 percent target and the 3.85 percent reality is not a minor optimism; it is a *credibility gap* at the foundation of the budget, because so much of the plan depends on the optimistic growth actually materialising, and recent reality suggests it may well not. A budget that assumes its way to a comfortable-looking plan, on growth far above recent experience, is a budget whose comfort may prove illusory.

This connects to the pattern of *mid-year revision* we met in the post on the calendar. Because budgets are built on optimistic assumptions that often do not hold, the government typically *revises* the budget partway through the year, cutting it down toward a more realistic figure as the optimistic assumptions fail — which is exactly why this year's budget, recall, was advertised as a large increase over last year's *revised* (deflated) estimate rather than its original one. The pattern is self-perpetuating: each budget is set optimistically high, then revised down at mid-year as reality asserts itself, and the next budget is then compared favourably against that deflated revision. So the credibility gap and the mid-year revision are linked: budgets are set on optimistic assumptions, the assumptions fail, the budgets are revised down, and the cycle repeats. A citizen who understands this expects this budget, too, to be set optimistically high (the 7 percent growth, the ambitious revenue, the long list of promises), to encounter the reality of slower growth and the execution gap, and to be revised down at mid-year toward a more modest reality — exactly as its predecessors were. The credibility gap means the announced plan is the optimistic ceiling, and the realistic expectation is meaningfully below it.

## The brand-new government: clean slate or no track record?

A fourth and genuinely uncertain factor in this budget's credibility is the one we examined in the post on who wrote it: this is the *first budget of a brand-new government with no track record of governing*. And this factor, uniquely, cuts genuinely both ways, so an honest reckoning must hold both possibilities open rather than assuming either.

On one hand, the brand-new government and its clean slate offer real grounds for *cautious hope* that this budget might fare better than the chronic pattern. The government was elected on a mandate for change, for cleaning up the corruption and dysfunction that fed the protests; it is unburdened by the failures and compromises of the past; it has a strong single-party majority that could let it actually pass and pursue its programme rather than being held hostage by coalition partners; and it has serious economic expertise in the finance role. It is *possible* that such a government, with the will and the mandate and the expertise, might actually tackle the structural problems — the execution gap, the capital under-spending, the opacity — that defeated its predecessors, and so might narrow the gap between plan and achievement that has been so chronic. The clean slate is a genuine opportunity, and one should not assume, cynically, that a new government must fail just because old ones did. There is a real chance, however uncertain, that this government does better.

On the other hand, the lack of a track record is precisely a *lack of evidence* that it can do better, and there are real grounds for skepticism. Governing is hard, and this government has never done it; the structural problems are deep and have defeated able people before; the binding constraints (the capacity to spend capital well, the fragile revenue base, the entrenched bureaucracy) do not yield easily to good intentions or strong mandates; and the temptation to over-promise, which we noted is especially strong for a new government riding a wave of hope, is itself a risk to credibility — a budget stuffed with ambitious promises by a government eager to match the expectations that elected it is exactly the kind of budget most likely to disappoint. And the execution gap, the opacity, and the credibility gap we have just discussed are all present in this budget, suggesting it shares the features of its predecessors more than it transcends them. So the lack of a track record means we genuinely *do not know* whether this government will do better — there is no evidence either way, only the hope of the clean slate against the weight of the chronic pattern and the deep constraints.

The honest verdict on this factor is therefore *genuine uncertainty*, and a citizen should resist both the naive hope that a new government will surely fix everything and the reflexive cynicism that it surely cannot. The right stance is to *watch the evidence as it comes* — to judge this government, over the coming year and years, by what it actually achieves against what it promised, by whether it actually narrows the execution gap and improves transparency and meets its targets, rather than by either the hope of its mandate or the despair of the past. The clean slate offers a real chance; the lack of a track record offers no assurance; and only the actual record, as it accumulates, will tell. This is itself an application of the series' core discipline: judge by outcomes, not by announcements or hopes or fears — and for a brand-new government, that means suspending judgement and watching the evidence, giving real credit if it delivers and real criticism if it does not, based on what actually happens rather than what is hoped or feared.

## The complete toolkit: how to read a budget like an auditor

We come now to the culmination of this series — the assembly, in one place, of every technique of deception we have exposed, post by post, into a single complete toolkit for reading any budget the way an auditor reads it. An auditor does not take a budget's claims at face value; an auditor examines, questions, reconciles, and demands evidence, asking always not "what does the budget say?" but "what is actually true?" This toolkit is how you do the same. Each entry names a deception, recalls how it works, and states the defence — and together they form a complete framework that you can apply to this budget, to next year's, and to any budget you ever encounter.

**The headline total.** A budget is announced as "the largest in history." The defence: deflate it — ask whether it is larger in *real* terms after inflation (nominal records are nearly automatic), and remember that the total is a *plan*, not an outcome, much of which may go unspent.

**The base-year switch.** A change is described as a percentage ("25 percent larger") measured against a quietly-chosen base. The defence: ask *larger than which base exactly* — original estimate, revised estimate, or actual — and look up the others, because the choice of base does the deceiving.

**The mandate halo.** A budget's numbers are defended by pointing to the government's electoral win. The defence: separate the *authority* to govern (settled by the election) from the *soundness* of the arithmetic (settled by evidence) — a mandate is not a forecast or a proof.

**Announcement as achievement.** A promised programme is presented as though it were delivered. The defence: track every claim through the stages of announced, enacted, and implemented, and credit only what is actually implemented and verified, treating announcements as mere intentions.

**Consumption dressed as investment.** Recurrent running costs are labelled "development" or "investment" to inflate the apparent building. The defence: judge by substance, not label — ask whether spending will actually leave a durable, productive asset — and watch the *executed* capital figure, not the rhetoric.

**Revenue optimism.** The revenue target is set higher than is realistic, to make the deficit look smaller. The defence: interrogate the target — what growth and import assumptions underlie it, and does the government have a record of meeting or missing its targets?

**The hidden deficit.** The gap between spending and revenue is made to look smaller through optimistic revenue, opaque labels, or off-budget items. The defence: reconstruct the deficit yourself — total spending minus revenue minus grants — and ask what the borrowing actually buys.

**Decentralisation theatre.** Money and power are announced as devolved to local governments while control is kept at the centre through conditions. The defence: examine the *strings* — how much of the money flowing down is unconditional versus tied — not just the headline transfer totals.

**Relief for everyone.** A tax cut whose benefits are concentrated is framed as evenly shared. The defence: decompose every tax change by *where it bites and who benefits*, remembering that in a progressive system no significant change affects everyone equally.

**Simplification that shifts the burden, and virtuous labels.** Tax changes are framed as benign "simplification" while shifting the burden (often onto the poor through regressive consumption taxes), and measures are given virtuous names ("green," "development") that overstate their substance. The defence: judge whole packages for their distributional effect, and judge every measure by its substance, not its label.

**Reform-by-announcement.** Bold reforms are announced and credited, while the legislation, implementation, and the deep constraints that would make them real go unaddressed. The defence: track reforms from announcement to actual implementation, credit only results, and watch whether the binding constraints are addressed.

**Green-washing and the megawatt mirage.** Revenue measures are dressed as climate policy, and generation capacity built is celebrated while delivery is ignored. The defence: judge environmental measures by what they actually do, and judge energy progress by power actually *delivered*, not capacity built.

**Ribbon-cutting accounting.** The same unbuilt project is re-announced across budgets, and incomplete projects are prematurely inaugurated. The defence: credit only completed, functioning, durable infrastructure, and ask whether a "new" project has been announced before.

**Input-counting.** Inputs (rupees spent, buildings built, people enrolled) are presented as though they were outcomes (people actually healthier, children actually learning). The defence: insist on outcomes, and treat every input as a promise still to be tested.

**Announcing programmes without delivery.** Generous benefits are announced for vulnerable groups while the machinery to actually reach them is absent. The defence: ask about the *delivery mechanism* and the *reach* — whether the benefit will actually penetrate to the most vulnerable it names.

**Subsidies that exclude the people they name.** Support presented for a broad, sympathetic group is designed, through eligibility conditions, to reach only a privileged few. The defence: examine the *eligibility conditions* and ask who they actually admit and exclude.

**The shiny object.** A fashionable, visionary announcement is sized for headlines rather than impact, unmatched by funding and unaddressed by constraints. The defence: test every visionary announcement against its funding, its constraints, and its substance, and keep attention on the consequential rather than the glamorous.

That is the complete toolkit — eighteen techniques of deception and their defences, assembled from the whole series. And running through all of them is a single, unifying discipline, the one principle that underlies every defence: *judge a budget by what is actually true and what actually happens, not by what it announces or how it frames itself.* Reconstruct the numbers yourself; distinguish announced from enacted from implemented; judge by substance not label, by outcomes not inputs, by delivery not announcement, by what reaches people not what is promised them; examine the conditions and the structure beneath the headlines; and watch the actual record as it accumulates. An auditor does not trust; an auditor verifies. This toolkit is how a citizen verifies — how you read any budget, this one or any other, with the skepticism and the discipline that no framing can penetrate. Master it, and you can never again be fooled by a budget, because you will always be asking the questions that the framing is designed to prevent.

## A worked example: auditing one promise end to end

To show the toolkit in action, let us audit a single promise from this budget end to end, the way you could audit any promise yourself using public information, because the method matters more than the particular example.

Take a concrete promise — say, the commitment to build 336 basic hospitals (we could equally take any flagship road, the health-insurance coverage target, or the AI compute centre; the method is the same). An auditor — or a citizen with this toolkit — would not stop at the announcement, but would work through the stages and apply the relevant tools.

First, *announced versus enacted versus implemented*: is this commitment actually backed by a specific, enacted allocation in the Appropriation Act and the Red Book, or is it only words in the speech? Find the actual budget line. Second, *input versus outcome*: "336 hospitals" is an input (and a construction target subject to the capital-absorption problem); the outcome is functioning hospitals delivering care. Ask whether the recurrent funding to *staff, equip, and operate* the hospitals is also provided, not just the capital to build them — because a hospital building without staff and supplies delivers no care. Third, the *execution gap and capital absorption*: given Nepal's chronic under-spending of capital budgets, ask how likely it is that 336 hospitals will actually be built in the planned timeframe, and check, as the year progresses, the actual *execution rate* — how much of the allocated money is actually being spent — from the Financial Comptroller General Office's data. Fourth, *ribbon-cutting accounting*: has this or a similar hospital-building commitment been announced before in previous budgets, and what happened to it? Fifth, the *outcome over time*: over the coming year and years, track not the announcement but the reality — how many hospitals are *actually built*, how many are *actually functioning* with staff and equipment, and whether they are *actually delivering care* to the people they were meant to serve, using the budget execution data, the health ministry's reports, the Auditor General's findings on the spending, and on-the-ground reporting.

Working through these stages, using the public sources we have identified throughout the series — the budget documents and Red Book from the Ministry of Finance, the actual-expenditure data from the Financial Comptroller General Office, the audit findings from the Auditor General, and independent reporting — a citizen can *audit the promise*: moving from the announcement, through the enacted allocation, through the actual spending, to the actual outcome, applying the relevant tools (input-versus-outcome, the execution gap, ribbon-cutting accounting) at each stage, and arriving at an evidence-based judgement of whether the promise is actually being delivered, rather than accepting or dismissing it on the basis of the announcement alone. This is what it means to read a budget like an auditor: not to trust the announcement, and not to cynically dismiss it, but to *verify* — to follow the promise through its stages using public evidence, applying the toolkit, and judging by what actually happens. And the crucial, empowering point is that *you can actually do this*: the documents are public, the sources are identified, the method is the toolkit, and the judgement is within reach of any citizen willing to verify rather than trust. ## For the technically minded: the auditor's checklist and the execution-rate calculation

For readers who work with numbers, here is the toolkit as an auditor's checklist — a structured set of questions to apply to any budget claim — followed by the single most important calculation for judging execution:

| When you encounter... | Ask (the auditor's question) | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| "Largest budget in history" | Larger in *real* terms? Will it be *spent*? | Headline total |
| Any percentage change | Compared to *which base*? | Base-year switch |
| A defence citing the election | Is this about *authority* or *arithmetic*? | Mandate halo |
| A promised programme | Announced, enacted, or *implemented*? | Announcement as achievement |
| "Investment" / "development" | Will it leave a *durable asset*? | Consumption as investment |
| A revenue target | What *assumptions*? Recent *record*? | Revenue optimism |
| A small-looking deficit | Reconstruct it: spending − revenue − grants | Hidden deficit |
| "Empowering local governments" | How much is *unconditional*? | Decentralisation theatre |
| "Tax relief" | *Who* benefits, and who pays? | Relief for everyone |
| "Simplification" / "green" | Distributional effect? Actual substance? | Labels and burden-shifts |
| Announced reforms | Tracked to *implementation*? Constraints? | Reform-by-announcement |

Now the execution-rate calculation — the single most important number for judging whether a budget's promises become reality, which a citizen can compute from public data:

```text
# The execution gap, quantified -- the most important number after the budget year.
def audit_execution(allocated, actually_spent, monthly_spend):
    execution_rate = actually_spent / allocated          # the headline judgement
    year_end_share = monthly_spend[-1] / actually_spent   # share crammed into final month

    verdict = []
    if execution_rate >= 0.95:
        verdict.append("HIGH execution -- the plan was largely realised")
    elif execution_rate >= 0.80:
        verdict.append("MODERATE execution -- meaningful shortfall")
    else:
        verdict.append("LOW execution -- the plan was largely NOT realised (the chronic pattern)")

    if year_end_share > 0.30:
        verdict.append("YEAR-END RUSH -- spending crammed at the deadline; quality/corruption risk")
    return execution_rate, verdict

# Apply this AFTER the fiscal year, using the Financial Comptroller General Office's actual data,
# SEPARATELY for recurrent and capital -- because recurrent execution is typically HIGH
# (salaries get paid) while CAPITAL execution is typically LOW (building lags).
# The gap between the budget's PROMISE (allocation) and its DELIVERY (execution rate)
# is the truest single measure of whether a budget was real.
#
# Likewise for revenue: revenue_realisation = actual_revenue / revenue_target
# A target met is a credible budget; a target missed is the optimism unravelling.
```

The calculation embodies the entire reckoning of this post. The execution rate — actual spending divided by allocation, computed separately for recurrent and capital — is the single number that reveals whether a budget's promises became reality, and the chronic Nepali pattern is high recurrent execution and low capital execution, with a damaging year-end rush. The revenue realisation — actual revenue divided by target — reveals whether the optimistic revenue assumptions held. A developer or analyst, or a citizen willing to consult the public data after the year, would compute these numbers to judge whether *this* budget was real or whether, like its predecessors, it fell short of its plan — under-spending its capital, missing its revenue, delivering less than it promised. And these numbers, computed from public data, are the evidence-based answer to this post's question — "can they actually do it?" — an answer written not by the budget speech but by the actual record, which a citizen can read for themselves. The toolkit lets you ask the right questions; the execution data, computed this way, lets you answer them with evidence.

## How budgets deceive: the consolidated truth

There is no single new deception to expose in this synthesis post, because we have exposed them all; instead, the deception to name is the *meta-deception* that underlies the entire eighteen-part toolkit: **the substitution of framing for truth** — the pervasive tendency of a budget, and of those who present it, to manage the *impression* it creates rather than to disclose the *reality* it contains.

Every individual deception we have catalogued is a particular instance of this one underlying move. The headline total, the base-year switch, the mandate halo, announcement-as-achievement, consumption-as-investment, revenue optimism, the hidden deficit, decentralisation theatre, relief-for-everyone, the virtuous labels, reform-by-announcement, green-washing, ribbon-cutting accounting, input-counting, programmes-without-delivery, subsidies-that-exclude, the shiny object — every one of them works the same way: by managing the impression the budget creates (of bigness, generosity, progress, vision, care, reform) rather than disclosing the reality (of modest real growth, concentrated benefits, chronic under-execution, unaddressed constraints, excluded vulnerable groups, underfunded buzzwords). The meta-deception is that a budget is presented as a *communication exercise* — an effort to create favourable impressions in the public mind — when it should be examined as a *factual document* whose claims must be verified against reality. And the public is the audience for the communication exercise, crediting the impressions, when it should be the auditor of the factual document, verifying the claims.

The defence is the unifying discipline we have named: *be the auditor, not the audience.* Refuse to be the audience for the budget's communication exercise — refuse to credit the impressions it manages — and instead be the auditor of its factual claims, verifying each against reality using the toolkit and the public evidence. Reconstruct the numbers; distinguish announced from enacted from implemented; judge by substance not label, outcomes not inputs, delivery not announcement, reach not promise; examine the conditions and structures beneath the headlines; test visions against funding and constraints; and watch the actual record as it accumulates, computing the execution rates and revenue realisation that reveal the truth. A citizen who does this — who is the auditor rather than the audience for every budget — cannot be deceived by any of the eighteen techniques or by the meta-deception that unites them, because they are always asking the one question that all the framing is designed to prevent: *not what does the budget say, but what is actually true and what actually happens?* That question, asked relentlessly and answered with public evidence, is the whole of reading a budget like an auditor, and it is the gift this series has tried to give you — the ability to see through the framing to the truth, for this budget and for every budget you will ever encounter.
