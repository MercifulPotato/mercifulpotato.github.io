There is a particular kind of optimism that surrounds the passage of major economic legislation in developing countries, and it deserves to be treated with some skepticism. The Alternative Development Finance Fund Act 2081 is a serious piece of legislation, prepared by people who understood the problem they were trying to address and who drew on substantial international experience and domestic analysis in drafting it. The governance mechanisms it establishes are thoughtfully designed. The financial instruments it authorizes are appropriate for the problems Nepal faces. The oversight framework it creates is more robust than many comparable institutions have operated under. All of this is true, and this series has tried to explain why.

It is equally true that legislation in Nepal, and more broadly in the South Asian context, does not always perform the way it was designed to perform. Institutions whose design looks good on paper can be captured, underfunded, staffed with loyalists, have their oversight mechanisms nominally complied with rather than substantively engaged, and end up serving political interests rather than the public interest that motivated their creation. This is not a counsel of despair — some institutions do work as designed, and the difference between an institution that succeeds and one that fails is in specific choices made by specific people under specific pressures, not in some inevitable logic of developing country governance. But it is a counsel of vigilance. Good design is necessary but not sufficient. Implementation matters more than legislation.

This installment attempts to lay out, without diplomatic softening, the specific risks that deserve attention, the questions that citizens and journalists should be asking as the Fund comes into being, and the structural limitations that neither this law nor any law can fully address.

**Political Capture: The Central Risk**

The single greatest risk to the Alternative Development Finance Fund is political capture — the process by which an institution designed to make decisions on the basis of technical and financial merit becomes one that makes decisions on the basis of political connection and patronage. Political capture does not usually happen suddenly or obviously. It happens incrementally, through a series of individually defensible decisions that cumulatively tilt the institution toward political service.

The process typically begins with the appointment process. The legislation requires that the CEO be selected through a committee composed of the Civil Service Commission Chair, the Nepal Rastra Bank Governor, and the Infrastructure Bank CEO — people with institutional standing and, at least formally, independence from the government of the day. But institutional standing does not guarantee independence from political pressure. If a government determines that it wants a particular person as Fund CEO, it can communicate that preference to the selection committee members in ways that are not visible in the formal record. Selection committee members who prioritize their own institutional interests — which include not antagonizing a government that has authority over many aspects of their institutional environment — may make choices that are technically defensible but politically convenient. The formal independence of the process depends on the individuals who participate in it having both the commitment to resist political pressure and the practical capacity to do so without unacceptable personal cost.

Political capture can also happen through the investment decision-making process. The Fund's board is chaired by the Finance Ministry Secretary — a civil servant, not an elected official, but someone who serves at the government's pleasure and who depends on the government for career advancement. Board members include the secretaries of multiple line ministries, all similarly positioned. When an investment proposal comes before the board that has political support from senior government officials, the incentives for board members to approve it — even if their technical analysis suggests it is marginal — are real. The board members' technical staff can raise concerns, but raising concerns about a project that the Finance Minister has personally endorsed is a career-limiting activity in most bureaucratic environments, and Nepal's bureaucracy is not notably different in this respect.

The question of political capture should not be asked only in the abstract. It should be asked specifically and continuously: which projects has the Fund invested in, what analysis was presented to the board, who proposed the projects, and do the project sponsors have political connections that might explain their selection? This is the kind of question that a well-functioning parliamentary oversight process would ask, that a free press would investigate, and that civil society organizations with expertise in public finance would monitor. Nepal has all three — parliament, press, and civil society — though all three have significant limitations in their analytical capacity and their willingness to ask uncomfortable questions of politically powerful institutions.

**Debt Sustainability: The Slow Risk**

Political capture tends to produce bad investments quickly and visibly. Debt sustainability problems tend to develop slowly and become visible only when it is too late to avoid significant harm, which makes them in some ways more dangerous.

The Fund's authorized capital is 100 billion rupees, with an initial paid-up government contribution of 25 billion rupees. The legislation authorizes the Fund to raise additional capital through bonds, equity issuance, and the mobilization of remittance funds. In theory, the Fund could significantly lever up its initial capital, borrowing against it to invest at several times the initial amount. This leverage is potentially appropriate — development finance institutions routinely operate with leverage — but it creates obligations that the Fund must service regardless of whether its investments perform as expected.

If the Fund invests in projects that do not generate the expected returns — because they are delayed in construction, because power purchase agreements are renegotiated at lower prices, because cost overruns exceed the financial structure's absorption capacity — the Fund's ability to service its bonds and other obligations depends on either recovering its investments through enforcement of security interests, drawing on its government-contributed capital, or receiving additional government support. Each of these options has limitations and costs. Enforcement of security interests in Nepal's legal environment is slow and uncertain. Drawing down government-contributed capital reduces the Fund's asset base and its ability to make further investments. Receiving additional government support means the government is effectively subsidizing the Fund's losses — which is a transfer from taxpayers to whoever benefited from the bad investment decision.

The debt sustainability risk is most acute if the Fund's investment pace is set by political ambition rather than financial prudence, if projects are appraised on optimistic rather than conservative assumptions, and if the diversification of the portfolio is insufficient to protect against a scenario in which multiple projects in the same sector underperform simultaneously. Nepal's hydropower sector, if it is the primary focus of the Fund's investments, exposes the Fund to concentrated sector risk: a scenario in which the cross-border power trade with India experiences a regulatory disruption, or in which prolonged drought reduces generation across multiple hydro projects, could simultaneously stress multiple investments.

**Environmental and Social Risk**

Infrastructure investment in Nepal inevitably involves environmental and social trade-offs. The country is ecologically extraordinary — the river systems that make hydropower possible are the same systems that support fragile mountain ecosystems, downstream agricultural communities, and cultural landscapes that have been inhabited for millennia. Large dam projects flood valleys, displace communities, and alter river hydrology in ways that can affect fish populations, downstream sediment flows, and the agricultural productivity of floodplains. Roads built through mountain terrain create landslide risk, fragment habitat, and bring economic disruption to communities that may not be equipped to manage rapid change.

The Alternative Development Finance Fund legislation incorporates standard references to environmental and social standards — projects must have appropriate approvals and must comply with applicable environmental laws. This is necessary but probably insufficient for a financing institution with the ambitions articulated in the legislation. International best practice for development finance institutions, as established by the Equator Principles and the performance standards of the International Finance Corporation, goes considerably beyond legal compliance to require comprehensive environmental and social impact assessments, meaningful consultation with affected communities including indigenous peoples, grievance mechanisms that give affected people a channel to raise concerns during project implementation, and ongoing monitoring and reporting on environmental and social performance. Whether the Fund will adopt these standards in its investment policies is not specified in the legislation and will depend on decisions made by the Fund's management and board.

The human rights dimension of large infrastructure investment deserves explicit attention. Nepal has significant indigenous peoples' populations — Janajati communities — many of whom live in areas that are likely targets for hydropower development. The International Labour Organization Convention 169, which Nepal has ratified, requires free, prior, and informed consent from indigenous communities for projects affecting their territories. Implementation of this requirement has been inconsistent in Nepal's infrastructure development history, with consultation processes sometimes conducted in ways that are formally compliant but substantively inadequate. A Fund that invests in projects where indigenous peoples' rights have not been genuinely respected faces both reputational risk and, potentially, project risk if community opposition disrupts construction or operation.

**The Expertise Gap**

An honest assessment of the Fund's prospects must acknowledge that the Nepali state has limited experience with the kind of sophisticated investment and financial engineering that development finance institutions at their best perform. Evaluating a complex blended finance structure, modeling the risk profile of a hydro project with both construction and hydrology risk, negotiating guarantee instruments with international partners, managing a portfolio of equity investments across multiple sectors — these require skills that are not widely available in Nepal's civil service or even in its private financial sector.

The risk is not that no one in Nepal has these skills. A generation of Nepali professionals has been educated at international universities, has worked at multilateral development banks, and has developed precisely the expertise the Fund requires. The risk is that recruiting and retaining them in a government-adjacent institution that offers civil service pay scales and a political environment that may not value or protect analytical independence is difficult. International development finance institutions and private investment firms offer more attractive career prospects for people with the skills the Fund needs.

This is a genuine constraint that thoughtful legislation cannot fully address. The Fund's legislation allows for competitive salaries for professional staff — a departure from standard civil service pay scales — but whether this authorization translates into pay packages actually competitive with the private market depends on implementation and on political willingness to sustain compensation levels that will be visible and therefore potentially controversial. If the Fund is staffed primarily by civil service transfers whose primary qualification is availability rather than competence, its investment analysis will not be reliable regardless of the governance mechanisms built into the law.

**What to Watch For**

The following specific indicators, if observed, should be taken as evidence that the Fund's governance is failing and that the risks described above are materializing.

The appointment of the Fund's first CEO through a process that is formally compliant but where the selection is visibly politically motivated — where the selected individual has obvious political connections to the ruling government rather than a track record of relevant professional achievement — is the earliest and most important signal.

The approval of projects in the Fund's first two to three years that have not undergone rigorous independent technical and financial appraisal, that are in areas represented by politically powerful figures, or whose sponsors are known to have political connections, is the investment-decision signal of capture.

The failure of the Auditor General to conduct a meaningful first annual audit — either because the audit is conducted with insufficient resources and depth, or because the audit report raises no significant concerns about an institution making complex financial decisions in a difficult environment, which would suggest that the audit is not actually searching for problems — is the oversight signal.

Debt growth in the Fund's balance sheet that significantly outpaces the development and revenue generation of its investment portfolio — meaning the Fund is borrowing more than its investments can service — is the financial sustainability signal.

The absence of any meaningful reporting to parliament during the first parliamentary review — where the review consists of a presentation of favorable statistics rather than an honest accounting of both successes and problems — is the democratic accountability signal.

None of these outcomes is predetermined. The Fund could succeed. It could develop into an institution that genuinely demonstrates that Nepal can manage public capital with the discipline and competence that infrastructure investment requires. That success would be significant not only for the specific projects it finances but as a demonstration effect — evidence to international development finance institutions, private investors, and Nepal's own citizens that the country's public institutions can be trusted with consequential responsibilities. The possibility of success is real. The risks are also real. Watching carefully, asking hard questions, and maintaining the expectation that performance rather than intention is the relevant standard is the appropriate posture for anyone who cares about the outcome.
