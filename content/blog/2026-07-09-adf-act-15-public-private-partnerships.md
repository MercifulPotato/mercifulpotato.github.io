In the summer of 2017, the government of Nepal signed a development agreement with a Chinese consortium for the Budhi Gandaki hydropower project. The agreement was celebrated as a breakthrough for a project that had been studied and delayed for decades. It lasted several months before being terminated. In 2019, a new agreement was signed with a different Chinese entity. That agreement also did not progress to financial close. Budhi Gandaki, a project that Nepal's planners have been discussing since at least the 1990s, remains unbuilt as of this writing. It is not alone. Nepal's infrastructure development history is generously populated with projects that reached the signing-ceremony stage, generated headlines about billions of dollars of incoming investment, and then quietly died in the gap between a signed agreement and an operating facility. Understanding why requires understanding the mechanics of public-private partnerships — what they actually involve, what makes them work, what makes them fail, and why Nepal finds them so persistently difficult.

**What a Public-Private Partnership Actually Is**

The term "public-private partnership," usually abbreviated PPP, is used so loosely in development discourse that it has almost lost meaning. Journalists use it to describe any arrangement where a private company does something a government used to do. Politicians use it to describe any project that involves both public and private money. Neither usage is precise. For the purposes of understanding infrastructure development, it is useful to use a more specific definition: a PPP is a long-term contractual arrangement between a public sector authority and a private sector entity in which the private entity finances, builds, operates, and sometimes owns infrastructure, and in which the allocation of risks between the public and private parties is explicitly negotiated and specified in the contract.

The core logic of the PPP model is risk allocation. Governments are generally good at certain things — providing sovereign backing for long-term obligations, regulating monopoly services, maintaining continuity through political changes — and generally bad at others — managing construction projects on time and budget, operating commercial services efficiently, adapting to market changes. Private companies have the opposite profile: they can be efficient operators, they respond to market signals, and they face hard budget constraints that governments can avoid by simply spending more. A well-designed PPP tries to assign each risk to the party best placed to manage it. Construction risk — the possibility that the project will cost more and take longer than expected — goes to the private developer, who has incentives and tools to manage it. Demand risk — the possibility that fewer people will use the infrastructure than projected — is shared, or sometimes retained by the government, because demand for public infrastructure depends on government policy choices that the private operator cannot control. Regulatory risk — the possibility that the government will change the rules in ways that harm the project's economics — is typically allocated back to the government in the form of compensation provisions, because a private operator cannot be expected to invest billions of dollars in a country if the government can simply change the rules and destroy the investment.

The specific structures of PPPs vary enormously. In a Build-Operate-Transfer arrangement, which is common in South Asian infrastructure, the private party finances, builds, and operates the facility for a specified period — typically twenty to thirty years for energy projects — and then transfers ownership to the government at the end of the period. In a Build-Own-Operate arrangement, the private party retains ownership indefinitely. In a concession, a government-owned asset is leased to a private operator who takes over responsibility for operation and maintenance and sometimes capital investment, paying a concession fee to the government. In a joint venture, a government entity and a private entity share equity ownership in a special-purpose vehicle that develops and operates the facility. Each structure has different implications for who takes which risks, who provides which financing, and who receives which returns.

**Why PPPs Work When They Work**

When public-private partnerships succeed, the success typically rests on a set of enabling conditions that are often discussed in the development literature but whose interdependence is not always appreciated.

The first enabling condition is a credible legal and regulatory framework. A private investor committing capital for twenty or thirty years needs confidence that the contract will be enforced, that disputes will be resolved fairly, that the regulatory environment will not change in ways that retroactively destroy the project's economics, and that if the government fails to deliver on its obligations there is a meaningful remedy. This requires not just good laws on paper but institutions — courts, regulatory bodies, arbitration mechanisms — that have a track record of enforcing those laws. Without this, no amount of headline investment announcements will translate into actual construction.

The second enabling condition is a bankable project structure. "Bankable" is a term of art in project finance meaning that the project's risk profile, revenue structure, and contractual arrangements are acceptable to commercial lenders and institutional investors. A project is bankable if lenders can model its cash flows with confidence, if the risks are allocated clearly enough that lenders can assess their probability, and if the government entities involved have the sovereign backing and institutional standing to make their contractual commitments credible. Many infrastructure projects in developing countries fail not because the physical project is not viable but because the structure presented to lenders is not bankable — the power purchase agreement has carve-outs that introduce revenue uncertainty, the government counterparty has limited creditworthiness, the land acquisition process is incomplete, the environmental clearances have conditions that could interrupt operation.

The third enabling condition is transaction expertise on both sides. Negotiating and structuring a large infrastructure PPP is a sophisticated legal and financial exercise that requires teams of experienced transaction lawyers, financial advisers, environmental specialists, and technical engineers. Governments in developing countries often lack this expertise on their side of the table, which creates an asymmetric negotiation where the private party — typically a large multinational company advised by international law firms and investment banks — has a deep understanding of the deal being structured while the government counterpart may be working from templates and generalizations. This expertise asymmetry leads to contracts that are tilted toward the private party, to risk allocations that are subsequently resented when they materialize, and to disputes that could have been avoided with better initial drafting.

**Nepal's Investment Board: What It Is and What It Does**

Nepal established the Investment Board of Nepal in 2011 as the primary institution for facilitating large private investment in the country. The Board is chaired by the Prime Minister, which signals both the political priority given to it and one of its structural vulnerabilities — a change in government necessarily means a change in the Board's most senior leadership, with potential disruption to ongoing negotiations and relationships. The Board's mandate is to facilitate investment in projects above a threshold size — currently projects above 6 billion rupees in value — by serving as a single-window mechanism for regulatory approvals, providing a point of contact for potential investors, and negotiating investment and project development agreements on behalf of the government.

The Board's track record is mixed, in a way that illuminates both the genuine difficulties of infrastructure investment facilitation in Nepal and some institutional limitations specific to the Board. On the positive side, the Board has been the vehicle through which several significant investments have been structured, including the Arun III hydropower agreement with SJVN and agreements for certain Special Economic Zones. It has developed project preparation facilities, technical assistance arrangements, and model contract templates that represent genuine improvements over what existed before it was created. The Board's existence as a dedicated investment promotion entity, separate from line ministries, allows a degree of focus and specialization that the general bureaucracy cannot provide.

On the negative side, the Board has been criticized for the rate at which signed agreements have not advanced to construction, for the opacity of some of its negotiations, for conflicts with line ministries whose regulatory jurisdiction over specific sectors creates coordination difficulties, and for the political vulnerabilities introduced by Prime Ministerial chairmanship. When a government changes — and Nepal has had many governments in its post-2006 constitutional period — investors who have been working with one Board leadership find themselves starting relationships with new officials who may have different priorities or who may not feel bound by their predecessors' commitments. This is not unique to Nepal, but it is a significant obstacle in a country where political instability has been endemic.

The Investment Board and the Alternative Development Finance Fund are distinct institutions with different mandates that are, in principle, complementary. The Board's role is to attract and facilitate private investment — it is a deal-making and regulatory-coordination entity. The Fund's role is to invest public and mobilized capital — it is a financing entity. In an ideal scenario, the Board facilitates an investment agreement for a large infrastructure project, and the Fund co-invests alongside the private investor as an equity partner, providing the local-currency capital component that gives the project financial viability and gives the government a stake in the project's success. The coordination between these two institutions will be important to watch in the Fund's early years.

**The Catalogue of Things That Go Wrong**

Nepal's infrastructure development history offers a fairly comprehensive catalogue of the ways in which PPP-structured projects can fail, and examining it honestly is more useful than simply celebrating the Fund's creation without acknowledging the environment into which it will be deployed.

Projects fail at the project identification stage when the underlying economics are not actually viable and the process of preparing a project for investment involves generating optimistic projections rather than honest analysis. Nepal has had projects whose feasibility studies were conducted by consultants whose fees depended on the project proceeding, creating an obvious conflict of interest. Infrastructure feasibility is genuinely uncertain — traffic projections, power demand forecasts, and cost estimates are all imprecise — but there is a difference between honest uncertainty and systematically optimistic bias. When the Fund invests on the basis of feasibility analysis, ensuring that analysis is genuinely independent and conservative is a governance matter of the first importance.

Projects fail at the negotiation stage when the agreement structure does not adequately align the incentives of the private developer with the public interest, or when important provisions are not included because neither party's negotiating team understood the issue at the time. A common problem in developing-country infrastructure agreements is that the contract looks reasonable at signing but includes provisions — stabilization clauses that prevent governments from updating environmental regulations, dispute resolution clauses that route all disputes to overseas arbitration forums where the costs of participation favor large companies over small governments, change-in-law provisions that obligate the government to compensate the investor for the cost of new laws — that create long-term problems not appreciated in the initial euphoria of deal signing. Nepal has had contracts challenged in parliament and civil society after signing for exactly these reasons.

Projects fail at the permitting and land acquisition stage more than at any other point in Nepal's specific context. Land acquisition for infrastructure in Nepal involves a complex overlay of community rights, forest land regulations, indigenous peoples' territories, and a legal framework that has evolved faster than the administrative capacity to implement it. A project that has a signed investment agreement but cannot actually acquire the land needed for its construction is effectively stalled indefinitely, and developers with a choice of investment locations will redirect their capital elsewhere. Environmental impact assessment requirements have also become more stringent over time, which is genuinely appropriate given Nepal's ecological fragility, but the regulatory capacity to process assessments efficiently has not kept pace with the requirements, creating bottlenecks.

Projects fail at the financing stage when the project has made it through negotiation and permitting but cannot achieve financial close — the moment when all the financing agreements are signed and construction can actually begin. Financial close requires all lenders to agree simultaneously on terms, all equity investors to confirm their contributions, all counterparties to have necessary government authorizations, and all conditions precedent to be satisfied. Any single outstanding issue can prevent financial close indefinitely. The period between a signed project development agreement and financial close is often measured in years, sometimes more than a decade, during which the project generates costs for both the developer and the government but no investment or construction.

Projects fail during construction when cost overruns exceed the financial structure's ability to absorb them, when the contractor encounters conditions not anticipated in the original design, or when disputes between the contractor, developer, and lender prevent coordinated decision-making. Nepal's remote mountain terrain creates particularly acute construction risk — access roads, worker accommodation, materials transport, and the management of the monsoon season all introduce complications that would not arise in less challenging geography.

And projects can fail during operation when the market or regulatory environment has changed significantly enough from what was assumed in the contract that either the private operator cannot make the project commercially viable, or the government finds the contract terms so unfavorable that it seeks to renegotiate or terminate, as happened with Budhi Gandaki. Renegotiation of major infrastructure contracts is always costly — it creates uncertainty for future investors who observe what happened to their predecessor's contract — but governments sometimes conclude that living with a bad contract is more costly than bearing the reputational damage of renegotiation.

**What the Fund Can Realistically Contribute**

The Alternative Development Finance Fund is not a solution to all of these challenges. Many of the problems described above — political instability, land acquisition difficulties, permitting bottlenecks, expertise asymmetries in negotiation — require reforms in government processes and institutions that the Fund cannot provide simply by having capital available. But the Fund can make a genuine contribution in specific areas where its existence changes the economics or incentives of project development.

By being a credible equity co-investor, the Fund changes the incentive structure for both the government and the private developer. A government that holds equity in a project through the Fund has a financial interest in the project's success, not just a regulatory or political interest. This can help align the government's post-agreement behavior — in providing permits, enforcing the contract on its side, supporting the project through political transitions — with the project's needs. A private developer that has a government-backed co-investor with aligned financial interests in a project's success is dealing with a different counterpart than a developer who faces a government whose only formal relationship with the project is regulatory. The Fund's equity participation changes the relationship from adversarial regulation to shared ownership.

The Fund's guarantee instruments can address one of the most persistent problems in Nepali infrastructure financing: the creditworthiness of the domestic buyer of infrastructure services. The Nepal Electricity Authority, the primary power purchaser for domestically-oriented power projects, has historically had financial health that made commercial lenders nervous about extending long-term project debt where debt service depended on the NEA's payment reliability. If the Fund can provide a guarantee backstopping the NEA's payment obligations under power purchase agreements, it transforms the lenders' risk exposure — instead of evaluating the NEA's creditworthiness, they are evaluating the Fund's, and the Fund, as a government-backed institution with its own capital, is a more creditworthy guarantor. This one instrument, properly deployed, could unlock significant commercial lending to hydropower projects that are currently stuck because the financing structure doesn't work without a creditworthy payment guarantee.

The Fund's blended finance capability is similarly significant. Development finance institutions — the International Finance Corporation, the Asian Development Bank's private sector arm, the British International Investment, the US Development Finance Corporation — are continuously looking for opportunities to deploy their capital in developing country infrastructure, but they require risk mitigation. They are not willing to take the first-loss position in a project that has no local partner willing to absorb junior risk. The Fund, by taking a subordinated equity or first-loss position in a project structure, allows development finance institutions to take a senior position that they consider acceptable, unlocking far more capital than the Fund contributes directly. This leverage effect — one dollar of Fund capital unlocking three or four dollars of development bank capital — is how development finance institutions are supposed to work, and why having a credible domestic institution with its own capital to deploy changes the landscape.
