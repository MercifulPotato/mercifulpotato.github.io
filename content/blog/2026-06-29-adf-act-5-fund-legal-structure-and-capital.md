## Part 1: Why the Legal Structure of an Institution Matters

Before we can understand what the Alternative Development Finance Fund will do, we need to understand what it is — in the legal and organizational sense. The legal structure of an institution determines:

- Who owns it, and in what proportions
- Who controls it, and under what rules
- What it can do — what activities are legally permitted
- What it cannot do — what restrictions apply
- Who is liable if things go wrong
- What happens to it if it fails
- How it is taxed and regulated
- How it reports to the public and to government

These are not bureaucratic technicalities. They are the architecture within which every subsequent decision — about investments, about governance, about accountability, about risk — takes place. A poorly designed legal structure can make a good institution fail. A well-designed legal structure cannot guarantee success, but it creates the conditions within which success is possible.

Section 6 through Section 9 of the Alternative Development Finance Mobilization Act 2081 establish the legal structure of the fund. Let us work through them in detail.

## Part 2: The Fund as an Autonomous Institution with Legal Personality

The Act establishes the fund as an "svashashit sangathit sanstha" — an autonomous organized institution. The key characteristics of this legal form are:

**Perpetual succession:** The Act says the fund will have "avichhinna uttaradhikar" — unbroken succession. This means the institution continues to exist even if its leadership changes, if the government changes, or if individual shareholders buy or sell their shares. The institution has a legal life of its own, independent of the specific people who lead it or own shares in it at any given moment. This is important for investor confidence: a bond issued by the fund is backed by the fund's assets and obligations, not by the personal commitment of whoever happens to be Finance Minister when the bond matures.

**Legal personality:** The Act says the fund "kanooni vyaktitwu prapta garnecha" — will acquire legal personality. Legal personality means the institution can do things that individuals and informal groups cannot: it can sign contracts in its own name, sue and be sued in courts, own property, hire employees, and hold bank accounts. Without legal personality, every transaction would require the personal involvement and liability of the individuals running the institution, which would be unworkable for a major financial institution.

**Autonomy:** The fund is designed to be autonomous from day-to-day government instruction. It has its own board of directors (the Board of Trustees/Sanchalak Samiti), its own chief executive officer, its own staff, and its own budget and financial management. The government does not — in principle — direct individual investment decisions. The government's role is to set broad policy through legislation and to hold the institution accountable through oversight mechanisms.

This autonomy is the most important — and the most fragile — feature of the fund's design. We will return to it repeatedly, because the gap between designed autonomy and actual autonomy in Nepali public institutions is historically significant.

## Part 3: The Capital Structure — Authorized Capital and Paid-Up Capital

Sections 7 and 8 establish the capital structure of the fund. This is where things get specific and worth examining carefully, because the capital structure determines the financial capacity of the institution.

### Understanding Share Capital

When a company or institution is created, it is given a capital base — money that belongs to the institution, that cushions it against losses, and that underpins its ability to borrow and invest. This capital can come from shareholders who contribute money in exchange for shares — proportional claims on the institution's assets and profits.

The capital structure typically distinguishes between:

**Authorized capital (adikrit punji):** The maximum amount of shares that the institution is legally permitted to issue. This is the theoretical ceiling. It can be changed by amending the law or the institution's founding charter.

**Paid-up capital (chakta punji):** The amount that shareholders have actually contributed. This is the real money that exists in the institution. Paid-up capital is always less than or equal to authorized capital.

The difference between authorized capital and paid-up capital represents potential for future capital raising — if the institution needs more capital in the future, it can issue additional shares (up to the authorized limit) to raise more funds from shareholders.

### The Fund's Capital Under the Act

The Act establishes the following capital structure:

**Authorized capital:** Rs. 1 kharba (1 kharba = 100 billion). So the maximum possible capital base of the fund is Rs. 100 billion, or approximately $750 million at recent exchange rates. This is divided into shares with a face value of Rs. 100 each, giving a total of 1 billion shares.

**Paid-up capital:** Rs. 25 arba (1 arba = 1 billion). So the initial funded capital is Rs. 25 billion, or approximately $188 million. This is divided into 250 million shares at Rs. 100 each.

The ratio of paid-up capital to authorized capital is 25% — meaning there is room to raise three more times the initial capital through future share issuances as the institution proves itself and grows.

To put Rs. 25 billion in context: this is a meaningful but not enormous amount of capital for a development finance institution. The Korea Development Bank, for comparison, has equity of many billions of dollars. Germany's KfW has a balance sheet of hundreds of billions of euros. Even within South Asia, the Infrastructure Development Finance Corporation in India operates at a scale many times larger. But Rs. 25 billion is a substantial starting point for Nepal — it provides real capacity to lend, guarantee, and invest, while remaining within the range that the identified shareholders can realistically contribute.

The Finance Ministry's economic commentary appended to the bill notes explicitly that Nepal's government will need to contribute 51% of the paid-up capital, which amounts to Rs. 12.75 billion (51% of Rs. 25 billion). This is a significant fiscal commitment — real government money that must be appropriated through the budget process and transferred to the fund.

## Part 4: The Ownership Structure — Who Holds the Shares

Section 8 (Sub-section 3) specifies who will own shares in the fund. The ownership structure is:

**Nepal Government (Nepal Sarkar): Category "A" shares**
The government will own at least 51% of the fund. This gives the government a controlling majority stake — meaning no significant decision can be made without the government's approval (or at least without the approval of enough shareholders to form a majority, which the government alone can constitute). The Act specifically states (Section 8, Sub-section 9) that the government's stake cannot be diluted below 26% through any share sale — meaning even as other investors enter and the ownership structure evolves, the government retains at minimum a blocking minority stake (which under typical company law would allow it to block major decisions).

The 51% government ownership is the mechanism for maintaining public control over a publicly-purposed institution. Development finance critics have noted that majority government ownership can undermine the commercial discipline and independence needed for good investment decisions. Proponents note that public infrastructure investment is not purely commercial and that some degree of political direction is legitimate and appropriate. We will return to this tension in the governance sections.

**Retirement funds and fund management institutions: Category "B" shares**
The Act specifically names three institutions in this category:
- **Karmachari Sanchaya Kosh (Civil Servants' Provident Fund):** This is the retirement fund for Nepal's civil servants. It holds large amounts of long-term savings — the accumulated pension contributions of hundreds of thousands of current and former civil servants — and is one of the largest institutional investors in Nepal.
- **Nagarik Lagani Kosh (Citizens Investment Trust):** A government-owned mutual fund that pools savings from individual investors.
- **Samajik Suraksha Kosh (Social Security Fund):** Nepal's newer social security institution, which manages contributions from formal sector workers and their employers.

These three institutions collectively represent a large pool of long-term domestic institutional capital — exactly the kind of patient, long-term investors that infrastructure projects need. They are expected to hold a significant minority stake in the fund.

**Life insurance, non-life insurance, and reinsurance companies: Category "C" shares**
Insurance companies are natural long-term investors — their business involves collecting premiums now and paying claims in the future, creating investment horizons that match infrastructure timelines well. The Act carves out a specific ownership category for Nepali insurance companies.

### How Share Allocation Within Categories Works

For Categories B and C, the exact share percentage given to each institution within the category is to be determined by agreement among the institutions in that group. If they cannot agree, shares within the group are allocated equally. If shares allocated to Categories B and C are not fully subscribed (i.e., if the institutions decline to buy their shares), the Nepal government can sell those shares to other institutions — including government financial institutions or other international financial bodies — by decision of the Cabinet, with the sale notified in the Nepal Gazette.

### Why This Ownership Structure Was Chosen

This ownership structure makes several pragmatic choices.

By requiring government majority ownership (51%), it maintains public control and public accountability. The fund is a public institution serving public purposes — infrastructure development for national benefit — and public ownership is one mechanism for ensuring that purpose remains central.

By including retirement funds, the Act tries to align the interests of domestic savings with domestic infrastructure investment. Nepali civil servants' retirement money invested in Nepali infrastructure creates a direct connection between the institution's performance and the welfare of millions of Nepali beneficiaries. If the fund performs well, their pensions are more secure. This alignment of interest is a design feature — it creates a constituency within Nepal with a direct financial stake in the fund's success.

By including insurance companies, the Act recognizes that insurance companies have natural incentives (long investment horizons, need for steady returns) that align with infrastructure investment.

What is notably absent from this ownership structure is foreign investors. The initial ownership is entirely domestic. This is a pragmatic choice — starting with known domestic institutional investors is less complex than trying to bring in foreign capital at the founding stage — but the Act does provide mechanisms for the government to sell shares to international financial institutions if needed (Section 8, Sub-section 8).

## Part 5: Government Share Dilution Policy

Section 8, Sub-section 9 contains an important policy signal: it states that government shareholding should not be reduced below 26%. But it also adds a policy direction: the commentary attached to the bill says that as the fund becomes stronger and private investment grows, it is appropriate for the government's share to gradually decrease.

This is a vision of a "sunset" model for government ownership — the government takes a controlling stake at the start when the institution needs public backing to establish credibility, but as the institution matures and proves itself, private ownership grows and government ownership decreases. The precedent is South Korea's Korea Development Bank, which was gradually partially privatized as the Korean economy matured, or Germany's KfW, which maintains government ownership but whose role has evolved from reconstruction lending to climate and international development finance.

The policy logic is sound: heavy government ownership at founding establishes credibility and accountability; declining government ownership over time introduces more commercial discipline and reduces the fiscal risk to the government. But the mechanism for managing this transition — and the risk that political interests will intervene to maintain government control even when commercial logic would suggest dilution — is a real governance challenge.

## Part 6: What the Capital Structure Means for the Fund's Capacity

The paid-up capital of Rs. 25 billion is the foundation, but it is not the limit of the fund's investment capacity. A development finance institution can typically lend or invest several times its equity base by borrowing — issuing bonds, taking loans from banks and international institutions. The ratio of total assets to equity (the leverage ratio) determines how much investment capacity the equity capital can support.

If the fund maintains a moderate leverage ratio of, say, 3:1 (total investments of three times equity), then Rs. 25 billion in equity supports Rs. 75 billion in total investment capacity. At a leverage ratio of 5:1, it supports Rs. 125 billion. The exact leverage capacity depends on prudential regulations, the fund's credit rating, the quality of its assets, and the terms on which it can borrow.

For context: the Act notes that Nepal needs infrastructure investment of Rs. 100 billion per year or more. The fund's initial investment capacity — even with reasonable leverage — will not cover the entire infrastructure gap. The fund is intended to be one instrument in a much broader toolkit, not the sole solution to Nepal's infrastructure financing challenge. But it can be a catalytic instrument — demonstrating the viability of certain types of investments, establishing new financing structures, and attracting private co-investment that multiplies its own direct investment capacity.

## Part 7: The Fiscal Risk — What Government Ownership Means for Taxpayers

One of the most important — and least discussed — aspects of the fund's capital structure is the fiscal risk it creates for the government.

When the government owns 51% of the fund and has injected Rs. 12.75 billion of taxpayer money into it, that money is at risk. If the fund makes bad investments, loses money, and eventually becomes insolvent, the government's equity contribution could be lost. Worse: the government has guaranteed certain bonds issued by the fund (more on this in later articles), which means it could face calls on those guarantees even beyond its equity stake.

This fiscal risk is real and should be acknowledged honestly. Development finance institutions that have failed — and some have — have left taxpayers holding large bills. The governance structures, investment discipline, and accountability mechanisms in the Act are the mechanisms designed to minimize this risk. Whether those mechanisms are adequate is one of the central analytical questions of this series.

The Act includes one important safeguard: the fund cannot dissolve itself (go through the wind-up process) unless the Cabinet first confirms that the fund is capable of fully paying all its debts and obligations. This prevents a scenario where political actors decide to wind up the fund prematurely, leaving bondholders and other creditors unpaid and the government on the hook through its guarantees.
