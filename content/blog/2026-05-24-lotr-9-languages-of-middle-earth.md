# The Languages of Middle-earth: A Complete Guide to Quenya, Sindarin, Khuzdul, and the Tongues of Arda

*This is Part Nine of the comprehensive history of Middle-earth — a dedicated examination of the languages that Tolkien built, which are not merely decoration but the skeleton upon which the entire mythology was constructed. Tolkien was a professional philologist, a scholar of language at Oxford for most of his adult life. For him, language was not a tool for storytelling; it was the thing itself. The stories grew from the languages, not the other way around.*

---

## Part 1: The Philologist's Mythology — Language First

John Ronald Reuel Tolkien held the Rawlinson and Bosworth Professorship of Anglo-Saxon at Oxford from 1925 to 1945, and the Merton Professorship of English Language and Literature from 1945 to 1959. He was not primarily a creative writer in any ordinary academic sense; he was a scholar of medieval languages and literature who happened to also be constructing an entire mythology in his spare time — spare time that was, by most accounts, not particularly spare.

His scholarly work included the definitive edition of Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, a landmark translation of Pearl and Sir Orfeo, the famous 1936 lecture "Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics" which changed the academic reception of Beowulf permanently, and extensive work on the history of Old and Middle English. He knew Old English, Middle English, Old Norse, Old High German, Gothic, Latin, Greek, Finnish, Welsh, and several other languages at varying levels of scholarly command.

This background is not merely biographical context. It is the explanation for everything distinctive about Tolkien's languages. When he constructed Quenya and Sindarin, he was not guessing at what a language should look like; he was applying his professional knowledge of how real languages develop, how they split from common ancestors, how they borrow from each other, how their sound systems evolve over time according to predictable rules. The Elvish languages are not random invented vocabularies. They are systematic constructions with consistent phonology, morphology, and etymology — the same features that distinguish real historical languages from each other.

Tolkien described himself as having a "native language" instinct — a sense of what combinations of sounds felt right together, what patterns of word formation felt natural. His enthusiasm for Finnish (encountered as a student when he read the Kalevala in the original) and for Welsh (encountered partly through his West Midlands background, where Welsh place names were common) gave him two distinct aesthetic models for language construction that he eventually built into the two main branches of his Elvish language family.

## Part 2: The Common Elvish Ancestor — Primitive Quendian

In Tolkien's mythology, all the Elvish languages of Middle-earth descend from a single common ancestor: the language the Elves invented spontaneously when they awoke at Cuiviénen. This original language is called Primitive Quendian (or in some texts, the Primeval Speech, or Quenderin). It was not a language anyone taught them; it arose from their nature as Children of Ilúvatar, for whom language was as natural as sight or hearing.

Primitive Quendian was characterized by certain phonological features that Tolkien worked out in considerable technical detail in his linguistic essays (many published in The Peoples of Middle-earth and in The History of Middle-earth volumes). It had a system of root-words — typically of the form CV̄CV or similar consonant-vowel patterns — from which derived forms were built by systematic modification: vowel gradation, consonant mutation, affixes of various kinds. This root-based morphology, in which a small set of root consonants carries the core meaning and vowels modify it, is reminiscent of Semitic language families (Arabic, Hebrew) and reflects Tolkien's interest in how meaning is encoded differently in different language types.

From Primitive Quendian, the Elvish languages diverged during the Great Journey. The Elves who made the journey to Valinor developed their languages in one direction; the Elves who stayed in Middle-earth developed them in another. The split between Quenya and Sindarin is the most important fork in this divergence, but it was not the only one.

The Elvish language family tree, simplified:

```
Primitive Quendian
├── Common Eldarin (spoken by those who set out on the Great Journey)
│   ├── Quenya (Vanyarin and Noldorin dialects)
│   │   ├── Vanyarin Quenya (used by the Vanyar, the Firstborn)
│   │   └── Noldorin Quenya (the form used in Middle-earth by the exiles)
│   ├── Telerin (the language of the Teleri of Valinor and Tol Eressëa)
│   └── Common Telerin (ancestor of the Grey-elven speech)
│       ├── Sindarin (the dominant language of Beleriand)
│       │   ├── Old Sindarin (also called Doriathrin in some contexts)
│       │   └── Later Sindarin (as spoken in the Third Age)
│       ├── Nandorin (the language of the Nandor / Green-elves of Ossiriand)
│       └── Silvan Elvish (the languages of the Wood-elves east of the Misty Mountains)
└── Avarin languages (the many languages of the Avari who refused the Great Journey)
```

This tree represents a real historical-linguistic reconstruction — the kind of work that scholars do for real language families like Indo-European. Tolkien was applying the same comparative method to his invented languages that professional linguists apply to natural ones.

## Part 3: Quenya — The High-Elven Tongue

Quenya is the most fully developed of Tolkien's languages and the one he worked on longest. Its aesthetic model was Finnish — specifically the sound quality of Finnish, its open syllables, its long vowels, its musical feel. Tolkien encountered Finnish as a student and was immediately seized by it: he described the experience in letters as being struck by a language of extraordinary beauty that felt like a discovery rather than a learning.

Quenya's phonology reflects this Finnish influence:

- Long vowels and long consonants are phonemically distinct (a short vowel and a long vowel can change a word's meaning)
- Syllables are predominantly open (ending in vowels) — words do not pile consonants at the end
- The sound inventory excludes certain consonant clusters common in English: no initial consonant clusters beyond relatively simple ones, no final clusters of the type found in English words like "strength" or "prompts"
- The vowels are pure, as in Spanish or Italian or Finnish, without the complex diphthong system of English

Quenya's grammar is highly inflected — it shows grammatical relations (subject, object, possessive, and many more) through changes to word endings rather than through word order. This is characteristic of Finnish and of Latin and other older Indo-European languages. A Quenya noun in the nominative (subject) form looks different from the same noun in the accusative (object), dative (indirect object), genitive (possessive), and a range of other cases. Tolkien worked out a substantial portion of the Quenya case system in his linguistic notes.

Quenya word formation relies heavily on derivation from elemental roots. The root KWEND-, meaning "person/being who speaks," gives us Quendi (the Elvish name for themselves), Quenya (the tongue of the Quendi), and related words. The root EL- or ELEN-, relating to stars and Elvishness, gives us Eldar (the Elves who made the Great Journey), elen (star), Elentári (Varda's title, Queen of Stars), and dozens of other words. Learning a handful of roots gives access to large portions of the vocabulary.

**Key Quenya vocabulary that appears in the published works:**

*Elen síla lúmenn' omentielvo* — "A star shines on the hour of our meeting" — the greeting Frodo uses when he meets Gildor's company in the Shire. This single sentence shows Quenya's morphology beautifully: *elen* (star), *síla* (shines, present tense), *lúmenn'* (on the hour, lúmë + locative ending contracted before vowel), *omentielvo* (of our meeting, *omentie* + *lva* first person plural inclusive possessive).

*Namárië* — the Lament of Galadriel, the longest Quenya poem in The Lord of the Rings, sung as the Fellowship leaves Lothlórien. *Namárië* means "farewell" (literally "be well in going," imperative form). The full poem is one of the most beautiful pieces of constructed language verse ever written:

> Ai! laurie lantar lassi súrinen,  
> yéni únótimë ve rámar aldaron!  
> Yéni ve lintë yuldar avánier  
> mi oromardi lisse-miruvóreva...

"Ah! like gold fall the leaves in the wind, long years numberless as the wings of trees! The years have passed like swift draughts of the sweet mead in lofty halls beyond the West..."

The poem uses the full range of Quenya's phonological beauty — the long vowels, the liquid consonants (l, r), the open syllables — in combination with imagery of enormous weight. It is simultaneously a technically accomplished piece of linguistic construction and a poem of genuine emotional force.

*Aiya Eärendil elenion ancalima!* — "Hail Eärendil, brightest of stars!" — Frodo's cry when he uses the Phial of Galadriel against Shelob. *Aiya* is a greeting/hail, *Eärendil* is the name of the mariner whose star is the Phial's light, *elenion* is the genitive plural of *elen* (of stars), *ancalima* is a superlative (brightest).

**Quenya's use in the mythology:**

In the years of the Trees, Quenya was the lingua franca of Valinor — the language of the Vanyar and Noldor, learned by all the Elves who came to the Blessed Realm. When the Noldor were exiled to Middle-earth, they brought Quenya with them. But in Beleriand, Sindarin was the dominant Elvish language, and Thingol — offended by the Kinslaying at Alqualondë, which had killed Telerin Elves — forbade the use of Quenya in his realm. Over time, the Noldorin exiles adopted Sindarin for daily use, using Quenya only for ceremony, high poetry, and recorded history.

By the Third Age, Quenya was to the Elvish world what Latin was to medieval Europe: a learned language of record and ceremony, understood by the educated, used for formal occasions, not a living vernacular. Aragorn's coronation name — Elessar — is Quenya. The Ring inscription uses the Black Speech (Sauron's deliberate corruption of Quenya principles). The names of the Elvish months and days in the Shire calendar are calques from Quenya. Tolkien treated Quenya as the "Latin" of his world explicitly in letters, which is why so many names of significant things (the Silmarils, the Ainur, Valinor itself) are in Quenya rather than Sindarin.

## Part 4: Sindarin — The Grey-Elven Tongue

Sindarin is the language most readers actually encounter in The Lord of the Rings. When Legolas speaks Elvish, he is speaking Sindarin. When the Fellowship passes through Moria and sees the inscription on the gate, it is in Sindarin and Quenya. When the Elves of Rivendell sing, they usually sing in Sindarin. It is the living Elvish language of the Third Age, used in daily speech by those Elves who remained in Middle-earth.

Its aesthetic model was Welsh. Tolkien had grown up near the Welsh border, had encountered Welsh place-names throughout his youth, and had a deep and particular love for the sound of Welsh — specifically for what he called its "Elvish" quality, the way its sound system produced combinations that felt simultaneously foreign and somehow right. Welsh characteristically has:

- Consonant mutation at word beginnings, where initial consonants change according to grammatical context (in Welsh, *pen* [head] becomes *ben* after certain words, *phen* after others, *mhen* after still others)
- Rich diphthongs: *ai*, *ei*, *oe*, *ae*, *aw*, *ew*
- A distinctive rhythm produced by stress typically falling on the penultimate syllable
- The voiceless *ll* [ɬ], a lateral fricative found in Welsh (as in *Llanfair*) and also present in Sindarin

Tolkien took all of these features into Sindarin. The consonant mutations of Sindarin — one of the most complex features of the language — are directly modelled on Welsh mutation, though the specific mutation rules differ. A Sindarin noun at the beginning of a sentence sounds different from the same noun after a preposition, different again after a particular kind of adjective, and so on. This makes Sindarin sound more fluid and less mechanically predictable than languages without mutation.

**Key Sindarin phonology:**

Sindarin vowels include the long versions marked with the circumflex: â, ê, î, ô, û (pronounced as pure long vowels, as in most European languages). The diphthongs include *ae, ai, au, aw, ei, oe, ui*. The consonants include *lh* (voiceless l), *rh* (voiceless r), *ch* (like Scottish *loch* or German *Bach*), and *dh* (voiced th, as in English "the").

**Consonant mutation in Sindarin:**

The lenition (soft mutation) of Sindarin changes initial consonants according to context. Under lenition:
- *b* → *v*
- *c* → *g*  
- *d* → *dh*
- *g* disappears
- *m* → *v*
- *p* → *b*
- *t* → *d*

So the word *calad* (light) becomes *galad* under lenition, which is why the name Galadriel (radiant garland of light) begins with G rather than C — the *c* of *calad* has been mutated. And the word *penneth* (young one, used as an address) is an endearment *pen* (person/one) with the diminutive suffix *-eth*, but *pen* has been mutated in certain combinations.

This level of grammatical detail was not invented casually. Tolkien worked out mutation paradigms and applied them consistently, both in the texts he published and in the constructed Elvish writing he produced privately.

**Key Sindarin in the published works:**

*A Elbereth Gilthoniel* — the hymn to Varda, sung by Elvish communities and used as a ward against evil. *A* is a vocative particle (O!), *Elbereth* is Varda's Sindarin name (Star-queen, from *el* [star] and *bereth* [queen]), *Gilthoniel* is another epithet (Kindler-of-Stars, from *gîl* [star/stars] and *thoniel* [kindler]).

When Frodo cries *O Elbereth! Gilthoniel!* in the chapter "Fog on the Barrow-Downs" and when he addresses her in Weathertop's aftermath, the cry is not merely dramatic. Within the mythology's logic, invoking Varda's Sindarin name is genuinely protective — she is the Vala whose light is anathema to darkness, whose nature repels the Nazgûl.

*Elen síla lúmenn' omentielvo* (quoted above) — actually Quenya, though many readers assume all Elvish in the book is the same language.

*Galadriel* — from *galadh* (tree) and *riel* (garlanded maiden/crowned) — "maiden crowned with a radiant garland." This is the Sindarin name; her Quenya name is Altáriel.

*Aragorn, Isildur's heir, Elessar* — Aragorn is a Sindarin name meaning "noble king" (*ara-* noble/high, *gorn* [difficult to parse, possibly related to "doom" or simply a name-element]). His coronation name Elessar is Quenya for "Elfstone."

*Imladris* — the Sindarin name for Rivendell: *imlad* (a narrow valley with steep sides) + *ris* (to cut, cleft) — "the cleft valley."

*Lórien / Lothlórien* — *loth* (flower, specifically a flower-cluster) + *lórien* (the name of the Vala Irmo's gardens in Valinor, associated with dreams and visions). The full name means "Dreamflower" or "Dream of Flowers."

*Minas Tirith* — *minas* (tower/citadel) + *tirith* (watching, from *tiri-* to watch/guard). Tower of Guard.

*Minas Morgul* — *minas* + *morgul* (dark sorcery, from *môr* [darkness] + *gûl* [sorcery/wraith-craft]). Tower of Dark Sorcery.

*Barad-dûr* — *barad* (great tower) + *dûr* (dark, gloomy, shadowed). The Dark Tower.

*Mordor* — *môr* (black/dark) + *dor* (land, in Sindarin typically applied to a region). Black Land or Dark Land.

*Amon* — hill or mount (used in many place-names: Amon Hen [Hill of Sight], Amon Sûl [Hill of Wind/Weathertop], Amon Amarth [Mount of Doom, the Sindarin name for Orodruin]).

*Ithilien* — from *Ithil* (moon) — the land of the moon, the region of Gondor east of the Anduin where moonlight was particularly beautiful. *Minas Ithil* (Tower of the Moon) was its great fortress before it became Minas Morgul.

## Part 5: The Tengwar — Fëanor's Writing System

The Tengwar is Tolkien's most fully realised writing system, invented by Fëanor in Valinor and used across multiple languages in the mythology. It appears in The Lord of the Rings in several places — the Ring inscription, the title page of the Red Book, Balin's tomb inscription — and is one of the aspects of Tolkien's world that fans most often learn to write.

The Tengwar's design is systematic in a way that reflects its inventor's genius: the shapes of the letters are built from a small number of components that indicate phonological features. All letters consist of:

- A *lúva* (bow): a curved stroke, either under or over the *telco*
- A *telco* (stem): a vertical stroke, either short or long

The placement and shape of these elements encodes information about how the sound is made:

**Voicing:** The same letter with a single bow is voiceless; with a double bow, voiced. So the *tinco* series shows *t* (single bow below, single stem) and *ando* shows *nd/d* (double bow below, single stem).

**Place of articulation:** The four *témar* (series) represent different places where the mouth closes to make sounds:
- *Tinco* series: dental/alveolar sounds (t, d, th, dh, n)
- *Parma* series: labial sounds (p, b, f, v, m)
- *Calma* series: palatal sounds (ch, j variants)
- *Quessë* series: velar sounds (k/c, g, kh, gh, ng)

**Manner of articulation** is modified by the stem: short single-line stems for stops, double bows for nasals preceding stops, raised bows for fricatives.

This is called a featural writing system — one where the shape of the letter encodes features of the sound it represents. Very few real-world writing systems work this way; Korean Hangul is the most notable example. Tolkien invented this principle independently (or derived it from his knowledge of phonological theory) and applied it consistently.

The Tengwar was not used to write a single language. Different *modes* of Tengwar writing were developed for different languages, because the letter-to-sound correspondences were adapted for each language's phonology. The Mode of Beleriand (used for Sindarin, where each tengwa represents a consonant and vowels are written with full letters) differs from the Classical Mode (used for Quenya, where vowels are written with small marks above or below the consonant that precedes them) and from the mode used to write the Common Speech (English in Tolkien's notation, where the Tengwar values are assigned to English sounds rather than Elvish ones).

The Ring inscription uses the Mode of Beleriand in the Black Speech, which is one reason it can be read with knowledge of Sindarin Tengwar conventions even though the language is completely different.

**Tehtar:** The vowel signs in the most common Quenya mode are called tehtar (singular tehta). They are placed above the following consonant (or, if no consonant follows, above a special carrier mark). The tehtar for the six core vowel sounds are:

- *a*: three dots or a wavy mark
- *e*: two dots
- *i*: a single acute-angle mark
- *o*: a hook or curl
- *u*: a different hook shape
- *y* (used as vowel): a specific mark

**The Certhas Daeron:** A separate runic-type writing system, invented in Tolkien's mythology by Daeron the minstrel of Doriath, used primarily by the Dwarves (who adapted it as the Cirth of Erebor). These are the runes seen on Thrór's map, on the gate of Moria (*Ennyn Durin aran Moria: pedo mellon a minno* — written partly in Cirth), and in other contexts where a simpler, carvable script was appropriate. Tolkien modelled the Cirth on historical runic systems, particularly the Elder Futhark, and built a similar logic of shapes into the letter designs.

## Part 6: Khuzdul — The Secret Tongue of the Dwarves

Khuzdul, the language of the Dwarves, is Tolkien's most deliberately concealed language — deliberately so, because the Dwarves themselves kept it secret. They did not teach Khuzdul to outsiders; they did not use it in public; they maintained it as an internal language of their own people, given to them by Aulë and kept sacred. What outsiders knew of the Dwarves they knew through the languages of Men and Elves.

The real-world model for Khuzdul is unmistakable: it is explicitly Semitic in structure, with the same triconsonantal root system that underlies Arabic and Hebrew. Each Khuzdul root consists of three consonants that carry a core meaning, and words are formed by inserting vowels around those consonants according to patterns. This is exactly how Arabic and Hebrew word formation works.

For example: the root *k-l-b* in Arabic relates to heart/core/essence (though this specific root is Tolkien's own construction, not real Arabic). From a Khuzdul root, multiple related words can be derived by applying different vowel patterns and affixes, all sharing the core semantic field of the root consonants.

Tolkien gives us very few Khuzdul words in the published texts — the Dwarves' secrecy is respected even in the narrative, which almost never records what they say when speaking among themselves. What we have:

*Baruk Khazâd! Khazâd ai-mênu!* — the battle-cry of the Dwarves at the Battle of Helm's Deep, shouted by Gimli: "Axes of the Dwarves! The Dwarves are upon you!" *Khazâd* is the Dwarves' name for themselves (singular *Khazad*); *Baruk* is the plural of *bark* (axe); *ai-mênu* is a prepositional phrase indicating "upon you."

*Khazad-dûm* — the Dwarves' name for Moria: *Khazad* (the Dwarves) + *dûm* (from a root related to halls/great spaces/depth). The Halls of the Dwarves, or the Dwarves' Mansions.

*Mithril* — the grey-silver metal, called by the Elves *mithril* (grey brilliance, from Sindarin *mith* [grey] and *ril* [brilliance/glitter]) but perhaps known by a Khuzdul name among the Dwarves themselves.

The Dwarves also used a Sindarin name for themselves in dealings with Elves and Men: the Naugrim (Stunted People) was what Elves called them, but the Dwarves adopted other names too. Their own name for themselves — *Khazâd* — is the one Tolkien treats as the actual name.

**The Dwarvish script:** The Dwarves adapted the Cirth (originally devised by the Elf Daeron of Doriath) for their own use, eventually developing it into the Angerthas — the Long Rune-rows. The version used at Erebor and in the Third Age Dwarvish tradition (Angerthas Moria) differs somewhat from the version used in Beleriand (Angerthas Daeron).

The Dwarves' use of the Cirth for writing Khuzdul is interesting because it means outsiders who learned the Cirth's letter values could potentially read Khuzdul inscriptions phonetically without understanding the language. This is exactly the situation at the tomb of Balin in Moria — the Fellowship can read what is written (once Gandalf identifies the script mode) but the meaning requires knowledge of Khuzdul vocabulary.

## Part 7: The Black Speech — Sauron's Constructed Language

If Quenya was Tolkien's "language of heaven" — the tongue of beauty and purity and Valinor — the Black Speech was its mirror and its negation: a language deliberately constructed to embody domination, to be spoken only by the servants of Sauron, to carry in its very sounds the nature of the power it served.

Tolkien constructed the Black Speech specifically for Sauron's One Ring and for the notation of dark power. Unlike the Elvish languages, which had developed organically over millennia, the Black Speech was invented at a single moment: Sauron made it as one makes a tool, for a purpose.

The Ring inscription is the most extended piece of Black Speech in the published works:

*Ash nazg durbatulûk, ash nazg gimbatul,*
*ash nazg thrakatulûk agh burzum-ishi krimpatul.*

"One ring to rule them all, one ring to find them, one ring to bring them all and in the darkness bind them."

When Gandalf reads this aloud at the Council of Elrond, in the original Black Speech rather than in translation, every Elf in the room reacts with horror — they cover their ears, the sun dims briefly, the words carry a physical weight that their Sindarin translation does not. This is narratively significant: the Black Speech carries in it something of Sauron's own nature, the dominating will that went into the Ring's making. Speaking it is not merely producing sounds; it is, however faintly, invoking what made it.

Tolkien gave the Black Speech a vocabulary and a grammar, though he published very little of it and seems to have been reluctant to develop it extensively. What can be derived from the Ring inscription:

*Ash* — one (the numeral)
*Nazg* — ring (Nazgûl = Ring-wraiths, from *nazg* [ring] + *gûl* [wraith/spirit of the dead])
*Durb-* — to rule (with *-atulûk* being an infinitive or gerundive suffix meaning "them all")
*Gimb-* — to find (with *-atul* suffix)
*Thrakat-* — to bring (with *-ulûk* suffix again for "them all")
*Agh* — and
*Burzum* — darkness (Mordor's name in the Black Speech is *Lugbúrz*, the Dark Tower, where *búrz* is a related root)
*-ishi* — in (locative postposition)
*Krimp-* — to bind

The *-atulûk* / *-atul* endings pattern suggests an agglutinative morphology — suffixes are added to verb stems in a regular pattern. This is unlike the highly inflected Elvish languages; it resembles the morphological type of Turkish or Finnish more than Latin or Greek.

Tolkien also used the Black Speech for Orkish curses in The Two Towers — specifically Grishnákh's outburst when Pippin disturbs him, which the text notes is a "Black Speech of Mordor, which it is not fit here to set down." This suggests that beyond the Ring inscription, Tolkien had a fuller Black Speech vocabulary available but chose not to publish it.

## Part 8: Westron — The Common Speech

The "Common Speech" of Middle-earth in the Third Age — the language spoken by Men of the northwest, by Hobbits, by the multilingual communities of Bree and the trading routes — is called Westron or the Common Tongue. In Tolkien's conceit, everything that appears as "English" in The Lord of the Rings is actually a translation of Westron, with one systematic exception: Hobbit names and Shire-specific terms are given in a form that shows what they would look like if translated the way Westron is translated.

This is a sophisticated piece of narrative craft. Tolkien wrote in Appendix F that if he had been more "adventurous" he would have presented the story in the actual languages of Middle-earth and left readers to learn them. Since that was impractical, he made the "translation" into English as consistent as he could. Westron terms that are specifically Hobbit cultural references are translated into English equivalents: "Samwise" is a translation of the Westron *Banazîr*, meaning "half-wise" or "simple" (from *bana* [half] and *zîr* [wise]); "Peregrin" translates a Westron name meaning "one who travels abroad"; "Meriadoc" translates a Westron name meaning "worthy/famous horseman."

The Elvish names and words are not translated — Tolkien kept them in their actual Elvish forms because the aesthetic effect of Elvish words, their particular sound, was part of the point. But "Bag End" is the English translation of *Laban-neg* (or the Sindarin adaptation of it), meaning "dead end" — the cul-de-sac where Bilbo's home is located.

This translation conceit is more than decorative. It is Tolkien's way of asserting the reality of his secondary world: the story is not set in a place where people speak English. It is set in a place where people speak Westron, and we are reading it in translation, and the translator (implicitly Tolkien himself, translating from the Red Book of Westmarch) has made systematic choices about what to translate and what to leave.

Westron itself descends from Adûnaic — the language of Númenor — which the Númenórean colonists of the Second Age brought to Middle-earth. As Númenórean influence spread through trade and settlement, their language spread too, eventually displacing or supplementing the many local languages of northwestern Middle-earth. By the Third Age, Westron was spoken across an enormous area, from the Shire in the northwest to the edges of Rhûn in the east. It was the lingua franca of the regions covered by the narrative.

## Part 9: Adûnaic — The Tongue of Númenor

Adûnaic, the native language of the Númenóreans, is one of the more fully developed of Tolkien's non-Elvish languages — he wrote an extensive grammatical description of it published in Sauron Defeated (History of Middle-earth Vol. 9). It was the everyday language of Númenor, spoken by both the Faithful and the King's Men alike, and it was this language — rather than Quenya — that the King's Men promoted as the language of Númenórean identity when they turned against the Elves.

Adûnaic has a Semitic flavor similar to Khuzdul, with a triconsonantal root system. This is probably not coincidental: both Dwarves and the Men of Númenor were associated with the same part of the world in Tolkien's cosmology (the east and the Mountains of the World), and the Edain and the Dwarves had long contact in Beleriand during the First Age. Tolkien may have intended that the Semitic-type root system was characteristic of languages originating in or associated with the eastern parts of Middle-earth, as opposed to the inflected-but-not-Semitic structure of the Elvish languages.

Key Adûnaic words that appear in published texts:

*Adûn* — west (giving Adûnaic its name: the tongue of the west, the Númenórean tongue)
*Akallabêth* — "the downfallen" or "she who was cast down" — the name the Elves gave to Númenor after its destruction. From *akall-* (to fall/cast down) + *-abeth* (she/the thing that was).
*Ar-* — royal/king- (the prefix used by Númenórean kings: Ar-Pharazôn, Ar-Adûnakhôr)
*Pharazôn* — "golden" (from a root related to gold)
*Míriel* — this is actually Quenya (*míriel* = one who glitters like a jewel), but it is also the name of the last legitimate queen of Númenor, suggesting the Faithful's culture retained Quenya naming traditions while the King's Men used Adûnaic names.

The decline of Quenya in Númenor — its replacement by Adûnaic in official contexts, the persecution of those who still used Elvish tongues — is itself a story told partly through language. The linguistic shift was not merely symbolic; it was how the cultural shift was enacted. Language politics in Tolkien's world is not a metaphor for cultural conflict. It is cultural conflict, conducted in the medium of speech.

## Part 10: Old English and the Language of Rohan

Tolkien's most directly philological narrative decision in The Lord of the Rings was the assignment of Old English (Anglo-Saxon) to Rohan. In the mythology's conceit, Tolkien explains in Appendix F that the Rohirric language has been represented by Old English because Rohirric and the Common Speech (Westron) bore a similar relationship to each other as Old English bore to modern English — related languages, with significant differences, mutually intelligible with effort.

This choice allowed Tolkien to use his professional expertise in the most direct way possible. He knew Old English thoroughly — it was his scholarly speciality — and using it for Rohan allowed him to populate that culture with names and terms that were etymologically real, that had histories and resonances within the actual Anglo-Saxon tradition.

The names of the Rohirrim are Old English names: Théoden (from *þéoden*, Anglo-Saxon word for "lord, ruler of a people"), Éomer (from *éomær*, related to fame/glory and horses — *éo* is the Old English element meaning horse, found also in Éowyn, Éored [Rohirric for a cavalry troop]), Éowyn (horse-joy or horse-blessed, *éo* + *wyn*), Merry's Rohirric name Holdwine (faithful friend, *hold* + *wine*).

The culture of Rohan — its mead-halls, its horse-culture, its poetry, its sense of heroic fatalism in the face of doom — is modelled directly on the culture of Anglo-Saxon England as reconstructed from Beowulf and other Old English texts. Tolkien had spent decades thinking about this culture professionally, and Rohan is his creative expression of what that culture felt like from the inside.

The great set-pieces of Rohan in The Return of the King have an Old English cadence to them that goes beyond vocabulary. The Ride of the Rohirrim — the horn-call at dawn before Théoden leads his host onto the Pelennor Fields — reads with the rhythmic pulse of an Old English alliterative poem:

*"Ride now, ride now! Ride to Gondor!"*

The alliteration (Ride, ride, ride, Gondor), the monosyllabic directness, the imperative mode — this is prose written to sound like Old English poetry without actually being in Old English. Tolkien's professional training shaped every line.

## Part 11: Old Norse and the Dwarves

If Rohan draws on Old English, the Dwarves draw on Old Norse. The names of Thorin's companions in The Hobbit — Fíli, Kíli, Balin, Dwalin, Óin, Glóin, Dori, Nori, Ori, Bifur, Bofur, Bombur, and Thorin himself — are all taken from the *Völuspá*, the Old Norse cosmological poem that is one of the most important texts in Norse mythology. Specifically, they come from a section called the *Dvergatal* — the Catalogue of Dwarves — a list of Dwarvish names preserved in the poem.

Tolkien lifted the names directly, including the name Gandalf (which in the Dvergatal is a Dwarf's name, meaning "wand-elf" — Tolkien reassigned it to the Wizard) and Durin (also from the Dvergatal). This was not laziness; it was deliberate and characteristic. He was locating his mythology within the Northern European tradition, signalling that his Dwarves were his contribution to a mythological tradition that included the Norse dwarves who make Odin's spear and Thor's hammer.

The Norse resonances in the Dwarves' culture go beyond names. The Dwarves' love of gold, their skill in craftsmanship, their dwelling under mountains, their connection to the earth's deep places — all of these reflect the Norse mythological dwarves, who are primarily craftsmen and hoarders of treasure. Tolkien kept these features while adding his own: the Khuzdul language, the history of Khazad-dûm, the Seven Fathers, the Ents' role as counterpart to the Dwarves in Yavanna's petition.

The Eddic dwarves are also mortal — they are not immortal divine beings, and they turn to stone in sunlight. Tolkien kept the sunlight-vulnerability for Trolls (specifically noting that Trolls were made by Morgoth in mockery of Ents, just as Orcs were made in mockery of Elves) but removed it from his Dwarves, who were far more dignified and less comic than the typical Norse dwarf figure.

## Part 12: Entish — The Language That Cannot Be Hurried

Entish is Tolkien's most self-aware linguistic joke. The Ents, who are the oldest living things in Middle-earth, have a language that is as old as they are and as slow as they are: a language in which nothing can be said hastily, in which every statement carries its full history within it, in which naming something is simultaneously describing its complete nature.

Treebeard explains to Merry and Pippin that Elvish languages were the first languages learned by the Ents — the Elves, who loved trees and loved naming things, came and taught the Ents to speak. But Entish — the language the Ents developed for talking to each other — is not really learnable by non-Ents, because the sounds involved are not sounds that the short-lived races can make, and the time it takes to say anything at all is longer than most conversations need to be.

What we hear of Entish in the text is described rather than transcribed. Treebeard says that his real name for himself would take a very long time to say even in summary, because his name is his whole history, and his history is very long. This is Tolkien's comment on the nature of language: all languages encode history, all words carry their etymologies within them, but Entish makes this relationship explicit and complete. In Entish, the word for an oak tree would contain the history of that particular oak, of oak-trees in general, of the forests where oaks live, of the geological and meteorological conditions that produce oak forests.

Tolkien was fascinated by the relationship between language and time throughout his life. A recurring theme in his scholarly work was how much history is preserved in words — how the etymology of a word carries its whole past within it, available to those who know how to read it. Entish is an extreme version of this principle: a language so complete in its historical embedding that nothing can be said without saying everything relevant to it.

## Part 13: Orkhaimë — The Orkish Tongues

The Orcs had languages, but they were not languages in the sense that Quenya or Sindarin were languages. They were corruptions — debased versions of the Elvish and Mannish tongues that the Orcs had been in contact with, thrown together without systematic structure, different from tribe to tribe, unable to serve as a means of communication between Orc-groups from different parts of Morgoth's or Sauron's domain.

The Black Speech was Sauron's attempt to solve this problem — to provide his servants with a common language that would replace the tribal variety. During the Second Age, when Sauron's control was most complete, the Black Speech served this function. After Sauron's defeat at the end of the Second Age, the Black Speech fell out of use among the Orcs, who reverted to their regional varieties.

By the Third Age, the Orkish speech was a pidgin or creole of the Common Speech — a simplified version, grammatically reduced, heavily influenced by the Black Speech's vocabulary for weapons and commands and terms of domination, borrowing from whatever languages the Orcs had contact with. The Uruk-hai created by Saruman spoke a form of Orkish that was more stable than the average because Saruman's discipline imposed more order on his forces, but it was still not a language of the richness of the Elvish tongues.

The practical difference between Orc-speech and Elvish speech in Tolkien's mythology is a deliberate symbolic contrast: languages are not arbitrary; they reflect the minds of those who speak them. Beautiful minds make beautiful languages; corrupted minds make corrupted languages. This is not a statement about any real-world language or people — it is a statement about the in-universe connection between creative and spiritual nature and the linguistic forms those natures produce.

## Part 14: The Tengwar in Practice — Writing Systems Across Languages

The Tengwar was adapted for use across many languages, and the variety of modes in which it was used demonstrates its flexibility as a writing system. Understanding the main modes helps readers decode the inscriptions that appear in Tolkien's texts and in the many publications that reproduce authentic Tengwar writing.

**Mode of Beleriand (Full Writing Mode):**
Used for Sindarin, and used for the Ring inscription (in the Black Speech). In this mode, every phoneme — consonant and vowel alike — is written as a full tengwa. Vowels are not tehtar (diacritical marks) but full letters with their own shapes. This makes the Beleriand mode more visually complex and more consistent — each sound has an unambiguous written form.

**Classical Mode (Quenya Mode):**
Used for Quenya. Consonants are written as full tengwar; vowels are written as tehtar (small marks) placed above the consonant that precedes them. If no consonant precedes a vowel, the tehta is placed on a silent carrier (a vertical stroke). Long vowels are marked with a vertical line through the tehta or doubled in various conventions.

**English Mode / Westron Mode:**
The Tengwar can be adapted to write English (or, in the mythology's terms, Westron). The title page of The Lord of the Rings first edition reproduced in Tengwar, and the Shire-map included a Tengwar text — these used an English mode where the letter-values were assigned to English phonemes rather than Elvish ones. The word *shire* in Tengwar English mode looks quite different from any Elvish Tengwar text.

**The Cirth (Angerthas):**
The runic system used by the Dwarves and in some northern contexts. The Cirth are meant for carving — they consist of straight strokes and angles, suitable for being cut into stone or wood, unlike the more curved Tengwar which were adapted to pen-and-ink writing. The door inscription at Moria (*Ennyn Durin aran Moria*) is in Cirth. Tolkien showed it in Roman letters in The Fellowship of the Ring with the note that it was in Elvish letters, specifically the mode used by the Noldor and Dwarves of Moria.

## Part 15: The Elvish Calendars — Linguistic World-Building in the Appendices

Appendix D of The Return of the King describes the calendar systems of the Shire, of Rivendell, and of Gondor — and these calendar systems are constructed using the Elvish language family, demonstrating how deeply language permeated Tolkien's world-building.

The Elvish week was originally six days (a star week, associated with the stars the Elves loved), later expanded to seven by the Edain who added a day for the Sun. The names of the days in Quenya:

*Elenya* — Star Day (*elen* + *ya*, adjectival suffix)
*Anarya* — Sun Day (*anár* [sun] + *ya*)
*Isilya* — Moon Day (*isil* [moon] + *ya*)
*Aldúya* — Two Trees Day (*aldu* [two trees] + *ya*)
*Menelya* — Heavens Day (*menel* [the heavens/firmament] + *ya*)
*Valanya* or *Tarion* — Valar Day (*valan-* [Vala] + *ya*, or *tar-* [king] + *ion* [son of, in a generalised sense])
*Eärenya* — Sea Day (*eär* [sea] + *ya*)

The Sindarin equivalents were the names used by the Sindar; the Mannish adaptations of these were used in Gondor and its dependencies. The Shire calendar was derived from Gondorian reckoning but had its own Hobbit names for months and days.

The months of the Quenya calendar:

*Narvinyë* (January-ish), *Nénimë* (February-ish), *Súlimë* (March-ish), *Víressë* (April-ish), *Lótessë* (May-ish), *Nárië* (June-ish), *Cermië* (July-ish), *Urimë* (August-ish), *Yavannië* (September-ish), *Narquelië* (October-ish), *Hísimë* (November-ish), *Ringarë* (December-ish).

Each month name is derived from Quenya roots: *Narvinyë* from *nár* (fire) + *vinya* (new) — "new fire," the first month of the year; *Súlimë* from *sûl* (wind) — the windy month; *Lótessë* from *lótë* (flower) — the flowering month; *Yavannië* from Yavanna's name — harvest month; and so on.

The calendar systems are not narrative-essential in any plot sense. Tolkien included them because they were part of what a fully realised secondary world would have — a people's calendar is one of the most intimate expressions of their relationship to time and nature. The Elvish names for months are their relationship to time, encoded in language.

## Part 16: The Name-Giving Tradition — Elvish Naming Conventions

Elvish names in Tolkien's mythology are never arbitrary. They are either:

*Father-names* (ataressë): given at birth by the father, often reflecting some quality or quality of the child or some hope for the child's future. Fëanor's father-name was Curufinwë ("cunning" + a name-element), reflecting his father Finwë's hope for his son's craft-gifts.

*Mother-names* (amilessë): given somewhat later by the mother, often prophetic — Elves, and especially Elvish mothers, could sometimes perceive something of their child's future nature or fate and encode it in the name. Fëanor's mother-name was Fëanáro, "spirit of fire" — Míriel saw in her son the burning intensity that would consume both him and her.

*Later names*: Names acquired through deeds or characteristics, either self-given or given by others. Thingol's name — *Singollo*, Grey-cloak — was acquired because of his grey appearance; his real name was Elwë. Morgoth's name was given by Fëanor as a curse-name, not the name Melkor was born to.

Most published Elvish names can be parsed etymologically:

*Elrond* — from *el* (star/Elf-related) + *rond* (vaulted hall, cave, dome). "Star-dome" or "Elf of the cave" — reflecting his role as the lord of Rivendell, the valley refuge under the mountains.

*Celebrimbor* — *celeb* (silver) + *rimbor* (of holly/of the holly-fingers). "Hand of silver" or "silver hand" — a name reflecting his craft-gifts as a silversmith, and perhaps foreshadowing the silver hands given to Maedhros (who had lost his right hand) and the imagery of craft and loss in the House of Fëanor.

*Legolas* — *laeg* (keen-eyed, fresh, green) + *las* (leaf). "Green-leaf" or "keen-eyed leaf." His father Thranduil's name means "Vigorous Spring" — both names have a vegetal/natural quality appropriate to Wood-elves.

*Celeborn* — *celeb* (silver) + *orn* (tree). "Silver tree." He was a Telerin Elf, lord of Doriath's region before Galadriel came, and his name may refer to the silver trees of the Sindar's forest realm.

*Galadriel* — *galadh* (tree, or *galad*, radiance) + *riel* (garlanded maiden). "Maiden crowned with a radiant garland" — or in the alternate parsing, "tree-woman crowned." Both parsings have some validity, as Tolkien used the name across the period when he was not fully fixed on the *galadh/galad* distinction.

*Gil-galad* — *gîl* (star, or stars collectively) + *galad* (radiance, light). "Radiant star" or "brilliant light of stars." He bore this name as his Sindarin name; his father-name in Quenya was Ereinion, "scion of kings."

## Part 17: Sound Symbolism — Why the Languages Feel Right

Tolkien wrote extensively about his intuition that certain sounds had inherent qualities — that some phonological features felt "right" for certain kinds of meaning, independently of any particular language's conventions. He called this "sound symbolism" or "phonaesthesia," and it runs through all his language construction.

In an essay called "A Secret Vice" (delivered as a lecture in 1931, published posthumously), Tolkien described his approach to language invention and noted that the aesthetic pleasure of language came partly from the relationship between sounds and meanings:

> "The invention of languages is the foundation. The 'stories' were made rather to provide a world for the languages than the reverse. To me a name comes first and the story follows."

This is a remarkable statement: the languages preceded the mythology. Tolkien heard *Eärendel* in a line of Old English poetry (*Hail Earendel, brightest of angels*) and was struck by the sound, by what it felt like the name must belong to. Then he invented Eärendil. Then he built the story around Eärendil. Then he built the mythology that the story required.

His feeling that *l, r, n, m* are inherently liquid and gentle (used in Elvish words for beautiful and peaceful things), that *k, g, r* in combination feel hard and abrupt (used in Orkish and harsh contexts), that *f, v* feel airy and ethereal — these intuitions are not purely subjective. Psycholinguistic research on sound symbolism (the *kiki/bouba* effect and related phenomena) suggests that associations between sound qualities and concepts are cross-culturally consistent to a significant degree. Tolkien was working with an intuition that has real linguistic basis.

The result is that Elvish words *sound* right for the things they name. *Lórien* sounds like a dream-garden. *Mordor* sounds dark and heavy. *Mithril* sounds like something light and precious. *Palantír* sounds like something you would look into and see far distances. None of this is accidental.

## Part 18: Neo-Elvish — The Living Languages After Tolkien

Tolkien published enough Quenya and Sindarin that enthusiasts have been working since the 1970s to reconstruct and extend the languages. The field is called Tolkienian linguistics, and it produces serious scholarship published in journals such as *Vinyar Tengwar* (founded 1988) and *Parma Eldalamberon* (founded 1971), both devoted to the publication of Tolkien's unpublished linguistic manuscripts and to scholarly analysis of the languages.

Since Tolkien's death, Christopher Tolkien and the Tolkien Estate have authorised the publication of many of Tolkien's linguistic manuscripts through these journals, giving scholars access to grammatical paradigms, vocabulary lists, etymological dictionaries, and extensive notes on phonological change that Tolkien never intended to publish in his lifetime. This material has allowed a significantly more complete picture of Quenya and Sindarin to emerge than was available from the published texts alone.

The "Neo-Elvish" community — people who compose poetry, translate texts, and construct conversation in Quenya and Sindarin — numbers in the thousands. The most common areas of Neo-Elvish activity:

*Translation projects:* Translating existing texts (the Lord's Prayer has been translated into both Quenya and Sindarin; portions of the Bible exist in various Elvish language versions; poems by Tolkien's contemporaries have been translated).

*Original composition:* Poetry and prose written originally in Quenya or Sindarin, following Tolkien's published and unpublished grammatical frameworks.

*The Peter Jackson films' Elvish:* The films required much more spoken Elvish than Tolkien had published. The linguist David Salo developed the language for the films, extending and adapting Tolkien's published material. His work is considered Neo-Elvish rather than canonical Tolkienian Elvish — he made interpretive choices that Tolkienian linguistics scholars sometimes dispute.

The field has genuine scholarly standards and genuine scholarly disagreements. How to treat grammatical features that Tolkien changed between versions (which version's rules do you follow?), whether to trust the film Elvish as reference material, how to handle words not attested in any Tolkien manuscript but needed for translation — these are real debates with reasoned positions on multiple sides.

## Part 19: The Language of the Music — The Ainulindalë's Linguistic Dimension

The creation myth of Middle-earth — the Ainulindalë — is told in language that is simultaneously a creation narrative and a meditation on the relationship between music and language. The Ainur sing; they do not speak. The world is made by music, not by words. But after the vision, when the Ainur enter Arda, they discover that the world itself responds to their speech — that there is a linguistic dimension to reality, that naming things is part of knowing them.

The Elvish invention of language — described as spontaneous, arising from their nature — is in this context a participation in the original act of creation. When the Elves name things, they are completing the act that the Music began: they are giving the world's features their proper identities. This is why Elvish place-names are so often descriptive (Gondolin: hidden rock; Rivendell/Imladris: cleft valley; Lothlórien: dream-flower) rather than arbitrary — naming is description, and description is recognition of what something truly is.

The Ents take this to its logical extreme: a complete description of a thing is its name, and any incomplete name is therefore not truly a name at all. Hobbit names in the Red Book, Tolkien notes, were often what he calls "Significance names" — names that pointed to something about the person. "Baggins" points to a bag-oriented family; "Took" points to a family that took unexpected journeys.

Tolkien believed that the meaning of names mattered — that the name carried the thing's nature, and that beautiful names pointed to beautiful things, and that degrading names expressed something about the debasement of what was named. This is why Sauron's naming himself Annatar ("Lord of Gifts") was a lie not merely in content but in form: he took a beautiful name built from beautiful Quenya elements and used it to cover an intent that was antithetical to gifts freely given.

## Conclusion: Language as Mythology

Tolkien said in a letter: "The invention of languages is the foundation." He was not speaking modestly or imprecisely. The Elvish languages are not ornaments on the mythology; they are the mythology's foundation. The stories grew from the names. The names grew from the languages. The languages grew from the aesthetic intuitions and philological knowledge of one of the twentieth century's greatest language scholars.

When Frodo cries *A Elbereth Gilthoniel* in the dark of the Barrow-downs, he is not merely saying words. He is using a language that carries within it the history of the First Age, the love of the Elves for the star-maker, the specific phonological beauty that Tolkien found most moving, the weight of a tradition of worship that predates the Sun. The language is the vehicle for all of that — and the vehicle and the cargo are inseparable.

To read the languages of Middle-earth carefully is to discover that they are not invented. Or rather: they were invented, in the sense that a specific person made them; but they do not feel invented, in the sense that great art does not feel made. They feel found — as if they were always there, waiting for someone with the knowledge and the love to hear them.
