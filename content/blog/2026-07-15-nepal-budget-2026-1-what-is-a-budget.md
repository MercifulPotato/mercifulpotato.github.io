Every year, on a fixed day in late spring, one person stands up in a large hall in Kathmandu and reads aloud a very long document full of numbers. Most of those numbers are larger than any sum of money you will ever hold. The person reading is the Finance Minister. The hall is Nepal's Parliament. The document is the national budget. And although it is read in a room most people will never enter, in a language of accounting that most people were never taught, that document will reach into the life of every single person in Nepal — the price of the cooking gas in their kitchen, the salary of the teacher in their child's school, the condition of the road to the nearest hospital, the tax taken out of a paycheck, the size of the loan the country owes to foreigners, and the value of the rupee in their pocket.

This is the first post in a twenty-part series about Nepal's budget for the fiscal year that runs from mid-July 2026 to mid-July 2027 — a year Nepalis call 2083/84 in their own calendar, and which the rest of the world would call 2026/27. Over the next twenty days we are going to take that long, intimidating, number-filled document apart, piece by piece, until there is nothing left in it that you cannot understand and judge for yourself.

We are going to do this the hard way and the honest way, which means we are going to start from the very bottom.

## What this series promises, and what it refuses to do

A great deal of what is written about budgets falls into one of two traps. The first trap is to simply repeat the government's own words — to print the speech, list the announcements, and call it journalism. The second trap is the opposite: to dismiss the whole thing as theatre, to sneer at every number without explaining a single one. Both traps leave the reader exactly as confused as they started, only now they are confused *and* they have an opinion.

This series refuses both. Here is what we promise instead.

We will assume you know nothing. Not because we think little of you, but because a budget touches accountants and farmers and software developers and drivers and students alike, and almost none of them were ever taught what a "fiscal deficit" is or why a government would borrow money it has to pay back with interest. So we will define every term the first time it appears. We will explain what Nepal is. We will explain what money is. We will explain what a government is and why it needs money at all. If at any point you find yourself nodding along to a word you could not actually define if a child asked you, stop and re-read, because we will have failed you, and you should expect better.

We will be skeptical of everyone. We will not assume this budget is good. We will not assume the government that wrote it is competent, or that the governments before it were better, or that the opposition would have done a finer job. We will not assume a number is true simply because an important person said it into a microphone. The single most useful habit you can develop about public money is to ask, every time, *says who, measured how, compared to what?* We will ask that on every page.

We will show you both sides of every choice. A budget is a series of decisions, and almost every decision has a real upside and a real downside. Cutting a tax puts money in people's pockets — and takes money out of the treasury. Building a highway connects towns — and runs up a debt that your children will repay. Promising free cancer treatment for children is a genuinely beautiful thing — and a genuinely expensive one, which means something else got less. We will not pretend these trade-offs away. A choice presented as having no cost is almost always a choice whose cost is being hidden from you.

We will give every amount in two currencies. Nepal's currency is the Nepalese rupee, written NPR. Because a great many readers think in US dollars, and because the numbers in this budget are so large that they stop feeling real, we will translate every figure into US dollars (USD) as well. We will use a single, fixed, honest assumption for the whole series: **one US dollar buys about 150 Nepalese rupees.** We will explain at the end of this post why we chose that number, why it drifts, and why every dollar figure we give you is an approximation for the sake of intuition rather than an accounting fact. We will repeat that reminder at the bottom of every post, because honesty about your assumptions is not a footnote — it is the whole game.

And we will arm you against being fooled. At the end of every post there is a section called "How budgets deceive." It is not there because budgets are uniquely dishonest. It is there because numbers are the easiest thing in the world to use as a fog machine, and the people who do it best are not amateurs. Each post will show you exactly one trick — how it works, why it works, and how a careful citizen catches it. By the end of the series you will have a complete toolkit.

That is the deal. Now let us start at the bottom, with a question that sounds too simple to ask.

## What is Nepal?

Begin with the ground itself, because geography is destiny in a way that is unusually literal for this particular country.

Nepal is a landlocked nation in South Asia, wedged between the two most populous countries on Earth: India, which wraps around it to the south, east, and west, and China, which sits along its northern border high in the Himalayas. "Landlocked" means it has no coastline and no port of its own. That single fact, which sounds like a piece of trivia, is one of the most important economic facts about the country. Almost everything Nepal imports from the wider world — fuel, machinery, medicines, electronics — has to travel overland, most of it through India, before it ever reaches a Nepali shelf. A country that cannot put a ship in the water on its own soil is a country whose trade depends permanently on its neighbours' goodwill and its neighbours' roads.

Nepal is also, famously, vertical. In the space of about 200 kilometres, the land rises from humid plains barely above sea level in the south — a strip called the Terai, where much of the country's farmland and a large share of its population sit — up through the middle hills where the capital Kathmandu lies in a bowl-shaped valley, and finally to the high Himalayas in the north, including Mount Everest, the highest point on the planet. This vertical geography is breathtaking and brutal in equal measure. It is why building a road in Nepal costs many times what it would cost across a flat country, why a landslide in the monsoon can cut a district off from the rest of the nation for weeks, and why "connecting the country" is a phrase that appears in every Nepali budget and is fulfilled in none of them completely.

Roughly thirty million people live in this space. That makes Nepal a medium-sized country by population — larger than most of the world's nations, smaller than its giant neighbours. The people are extraordinarily diverse: many languages, many ethnic and caste groups, many religions, layered across that vertical landscape. This diversity matters for a budget because a national plan written in Kathmandu has to somehow be fair to a herder in the high mountains, a farmer in the southern plains, and an office worker in the city, who may not share a language, a livelihood, or a sense that the distant capital understands them at all.

Now the part that is hardest to say plainly: Nepal is poor. Not poor in culture, history, or human capacity — poor in the narrow, measurable, money sense that a budget cares about. Economists measure the size of a country's economy using a figure called Gross Domestic Product, or GDP, which is simply the total value of everything a country produces in a year — every haircut, every sack of rice, every software contract, every hotel night, added up. Nepal's GDP is on the order of the equivalent of forty-some billion US dollars a year. Spread across thirty million people, that works out to an average income that places Nepal firmly among the lower-income countries of the world. We will return to GDP many times, because nearly every honest judgement about whether a budget number is "big" or "small" depends on comparing it to the size of the economy, the same way the meaning of a household's spending depends entirely on the household's income.

But the most revealing economic fact about Nepal is not its GDP. It is where a great deal of the money in Nepali life actually comes from. For decades, one of the country's largest "exports" has not been a product at all. It has been people. Millions of Nepalis — overwhelmingly young men, increasingly women and students too — leave to work abroad, historically in India, then in large numbers in the Gulf states and Malaysia, and more recently in places like the United States, Australia, and the United Kingdom. The money they send home to their families is called *remittance*. In recent years remittances have been worth something on the order of the equivalent of nine to eleven billion US dollars annually, a sum that amounts to roughly a quarter or more of the entire economy. To put that in perspective, in some recent years the money Nepalis abroad sent home exceeded the entire revenue collected by their own government. We will devote a whole post to this later, because you cannot understand Nepal's budget — where its tax money comes from, why its currency holds its value, why a slowdown abroad terrifies its finance ministry — without understanding that the country runs, to a remarkable degree, on the wages of its absent children.

So: a small, vertical, landlocked, diverse, low-income country, dependent on its neighbours for trade and on its migrants for cash. Hold that picture. Every choice in the budget is a response, wise or foolish, to that situation.

## What is money, really?

This question feels almost insulting. Everyone knows what money is. But almost no one can say what it *is*, as opposed to what it *does*, and the difference matters for understanding a national budget.

Strip it down. Money is a tool for solving a problem, and the problem is this: in any economy more complicated than a single family, people need to trade things, but they rarely want exactly what the other person is offering at exactly the moment of trade. A farmer with rice who needs his roof fixed cannot easily find a roofer who happens to want rice that week, in the right quantity, of the right kind. Barter — trading goods directly for goods — collapses under its own awkwardness the moment an economy grows. Money is the human solution. It is a thing everyone agrees to accept in trade, not because the thing itself is useful, but because everyone is confident everyone else will accept it in turn. The rice farmer sells rice to anyone for money, then hires the roofer with money, and the roofer takes it because she knows the baker will take it from her. Money is, at bottom, a shared story that a society agrees to believe, and it works exactly as long as the belief holds.

Money does three jobs. It is a *medium of exchange* — the thing that passes from hand to hand in trade. It is a *unit of account* — the common ruler we use to measure and compare the value of utterly different things, so that we can say a sack of rice and a bus fare and a year of a teacher's labour can all be priced in the same units and added together. And it is a *store of value* — a way to hold purchasing power across time, so you can earn today and spend next month. A budget leans on all three jobs at once: it counts the nation's plans in a single unit (the rupee), it moves money through the economy in exchange for work and goods, and it relies on the rupee holding its value long enough for those plans to mean anything.

Nepal's money is the Nepalese rupee. And here we meet a fact that is central to this entire series, so we will state it carefully and explain it slowly.

The Nepalese rupee is *pegged* to the Indian rupee. "Pegged" means the two currencies are locked together at a fixed, officially maintained rate: by long-standing arrangement, one Indian rupee is worth exactly 1.60 Nepalese rupees, and Nepal's central bank works to keep it there. This is a deliberate policy choice, and like every choice it has two faces. On the one face, the peg buys stability and trust: because India is by far Nepal's largest trading partner, locking the rupees together keeps cross-border prices predictable and reassures everyone that the Nepali rupee will not suddenly collapse in value. On the other face, the peg means Nepal hands a great deal of control over its own money to decisions made in India. Nepal cannot freely set its own currency's value against the dollar, the euro, or anything else; the Nepali rupee floats against the rest of the world only as a passenger, rising and falling with the Indian rupee to which it is chained.

That is why, when we translate Nepali rupees into US dollars in this series, the number we use is really a number about India. The Nepali rupee's value against the dollar moves only when the Indian rupee's value against the dollar moves. As this is written, it takes somewhere around 150 Nepali rupees to buy one US dollar. That rate is not fixed and not eternal; it drifts a little every day. We will say much more about this at the end of the post. For now, simply absorb the shape of it: Nepal's money is a story the country shares, and that story is bound, by choice, to the larger story of the Indian rupee next door.

## What is a government, and why does it need money?

Now the second actor in our story. We have money. Who is this "government" that wants to collect and spend so much of it?

A government is the organisation a society sets up to do the things that the society has decided cannot be left to each person acting alone. That is the whole idea, and it is worth pausing on, because people argue endlessly about *which* things belong on that list without ever agreeing on the principle behind the list itself.

Consider a road between two towns. If it is left to individuals, almost no one will build it. It is enormously expensive, it benefits thousands of people who did not pay for it, and any single person who paid to build it could not stop their neighbours from using it for free. Economists call something like this a *public good* — a thing that is valuable to everyone, hard to provide for profit, and easy to enjoy without paying. Roads, national defence, courts of law, public health measures against an epidemic, clean air, the very system of money and contracts that lets a market exist at all — these are the classic examples. Left to individuals, they get built too little or not at all. A government exists, in large part, to provide the things that markets, left alone, will not.

But governments do more than provide public goods. They also *redistribute* — they take from some and give to others, through taxes and benefits, because a society has decided that letting the market's outcomes stand untouched would be unjust or unstable: that a child should not starve because her parents are poor, that an old person should not be destitute because they can no longer work, that a region should not be abandoned because it happens to be remote. And governments *stabilise* — they try to smooth out the booms and busts of the economy, spending in bad times and restraining in good ones. How much of each of these three jobs a government should do is the central argument of all politics. But that there is *some* such job, requiring *some* pooled money, is something almost everyone accepts the moment they want a road, a court, or a defended border.

To do any of this, a government needs money, and a government gets money in essentially three ways, all of which will recur throughout this series. It can *tax* — compel its citizens and businesses to hand over a share of their income, their purchases, or their imports. It can *borrow* — promise to repay money today with interest tomorrow, from its own citizens or from foreigners. And it can receive *grants* — gifts, typically from foreign governments and international institutions, which unlike loans need not be paid back. Nepal uses all three, and the balance among them is one of the most important things a budget reveals. A government that funds itself mostly by taxing is living within the means its own economy generates. A government that leans heavily on borrowing is borrowing against its future. A government that depends on foreign grants is, to that extent, dependent on the priorities of foreigners. We will weigh Nepal's particular mix carefully in the posts on revenue and debt.

## What is a budget?

We finally have all the pieces: a country, a kind of money, a government, and the reasons a government collects and spends. A budget is what happens when you put them together and write it down in advance.

A national budget is three things at once, and most confusion about budgets comes from forgetting that it is all three simultaneously.

First, a budget is a *plan*. It is the government's statement, before the year begins, of how much money it expects to take in and how much it intends to spend, broken down by where the money will come from and where it will go. In this sense a budget is exactly like a household sitting down before the month begins and saying: we expect this much income; we will spend this much on rent, this much on food, this much on the children's school, and we will try to save or borrow the difference. The national version is unimaginably larger and more complicated, but the logic is the same. It is a forecast and an intention, made in advance, about money.

Second, a budget is a *law*. This is the part most people miss, and it changes everything. In Nepal, as in most democracies, the government cannot simply spend public money because it feels like it. The legal authority to collect each tax and to spend on each purpose has to be granted by the people's elected representatives in Parliament, through formal legislation. The budget speech you hear in May is, strictly speaking, a *proposal*. It becomes binding only when Parliament passes the laws that authorise the taxing and the spending, and the President formally certifies them. We will devote an entire post to this distinction, because it is the source of one of the most common deceptions in all of public finance: the gap between what is *announced* in a speech, what is *enacted* into law, and what is actually *implemented* on the ground. These three are not the same, and a great deal of political theatre depends on you believing that an announcement is an accomplishment.

Third, a budget is a *political document*. A government has far more things it could spend money on than money to spend. Every rupee given to one purpose is a rupee denied to another. So the budget is the single clearest statement a government makes about its real priorities — not the priorities in its speeches and slogans, but the priorities revealed by where it actually points the money. If a government claims to care about something and allocates almost nothing to it, the allocation tells you the truth and the claim is decoration. Reading a budget well means reading past the adjectives to the arithmetic. Throughout this series we will keep asking the budget the only question that cannot be spun: *where did the money actually go?*

## Why does Nepal make a budget every single year?

It might seem more sensible to plan for five years or ten. Governments do make longer plans, and Nepal has a National Planning Commission that thinks in multi-year horizons. But the budget itself is annual, and the reasons are worth understanding, because the annual rhythm shapes a great deal of the dysfunction we will encounter later.

The deepest reason is accountability. An annual budget forces the government to come before the people's representatives every single year and account for itself: here is what we collected, here is what we spent, here is what we plan to do next. Each year is a fresh permission slip that the elected Parliament must sign. A government that could spend indefinitely without returning for renewed authority would be a government with no leash. The annual budget is the leash, at least in principle.

The annual rhythm also reflects reality. Economies change. A good monsoon or a bad one, a surge or a slump in the wages migrants send home, a spike in the global price of oil, a disaster, a pandemic — any of these can blow a hole in last year's assumptions. Replanning every year lets the government adjust. The cost of this flexibility, as we will see, is a country perpetually planning and re-planning, often spending in a frantic rush at the end of each fiscal year to use up money before the permission expires, with predictable consequences for quality.

So once a year, on a date fixed by the constitution, the cycle turns: the government estimates, Parliament debates and authorises, the money is spent across twelve months, the spending is audited, and the whole thing begins again. The budget we are studying is one turn of that wheel — but a turn that arrives at an unusual moment in Nepal's history, presented by a government unlike any the country has had before. We will come to who they are. First, the numbers in one breath.

## The 2026-27 budget at a glance

Here, stated plainly and in both currencies, is the shape of the thing we will spend twenty posts dissecting. Do not try to absorb every figure now. Just let the scale and the proportions register; we will earn each number properly in later posts.

The total budget for fiscal year 2083/84 is **NPR 2,124.34 billion**, which at our assumed rate is **about USD 14.16 billion**. The government and much of the press describe this as the largest budget in Nepal's history. In a narrow, literal, nominal sense, that is true. Whether "largest in history" actually means very much is a question we will take apart in a later post, and the short answer is: far less than it sounds.

That total divides into three broad streams. The first is *recurrent* spending — the cost of running the state day to day, dominated by the salaries of public employees, the operation of offices, and the interest on past debts. It is **NPR 1,270.58 billion (about USD 8.47 billion)**, which is 59.8 percent of the whole. The second is *capital* spending — money meant to build durable things: roads, bridges, schools, power lines, the physical assets that outlast the year. It is **NPR 431.10 billion (about USD 2.87 billion)**, just 20.3 percent of the total. The third stream is *financing*, or financial management — chiefly the repayment of the principal on past loans and the lending of money onward to public enterprises and lower levels of government. It is **NPR 422.64 billion (about USD 2.82 billion)**, 19.9 percent. The relationship between that large first number and that small second number — a country spending three times as much running itself as building itself — is one of the most important and most worrying facts in the entire budget, and it gets its own post.

Where does the money to cover all this come from? The government expects to raise **NPR 1,405.31 billion (about USD 9.37 billion)** in revenue, mostly taxes. It expects **NPR 61.74 billion (about USD 0.41 billion)** in foreign grants. Everything beyond that has to be borrowed: **NPR 247.28 billion (about USD 1.65 billion)** from foreign lenders and **NPR 410 billion (about USD 2.73 billion)** from within Nepal. The gap between what the government plans to spend and what it expects to collect in revenue and grants — the *deficit* — is on the order of **NPR 657 billion, roughly USD 4.4 billion**. A deficit is not automatically a sin, but it is always a question, and we will ask it hard.

The budget also sets out ambitions: an economic growth target of 7 percent for the year, against an economy that the government's own statisticians estimate grew only 3.85 percent in the year just ending; and a promise to hold inflation — the rate at which prices rise and money loses value — to no more than 6 percent. Whether those targets are plausible or merely aspirational is exactly the sort of thing this series exists to examine.

Per person, the whole budget works out to roughly **NPR 70,000 per Nepali (about USD 470)** for the year — a useful figure to keep in your pocket, because it turns an incomprehensible national total into something human-sized: this is, very roughly, what the government plans to collect-and-spend on behalf of each man, woman, and child in the country over twelve months.

That is the budget in a single breath. Now we spend twenty days learning to breathe.

## How budgets deceive: the trick of the headline total

We promised that every post would expose one technique of deception. The first is the most basic and the most universal, because it is built into the very first sentence of nearly every budget story: **the headline total**.

"The largest budget in the country's history." You will read that phrase, or hear it, every single year, in almost every country. Here is the trick, and it has three layers.

The first layer is *inflation*. Money loses value over time as prices rise. A budget of NPR 2.12 trillion this year and a budget of NPR 2.12 trillion ten years ago are not the same amount of real resources, because the rupee buys less now than it did then. Almost any budget will be "the largest in history" measured in plain rupees, for the same boring reason that almost every year sets a record for the highest prices in history: the number on the money keeps climbing even when the real quantity of stuff barely moves. To know whether a budget is genuinely larger in any meaningful sense, you have to strip out inflation and compare *real* resources, not *nominal* rupees. The headline almost never does this.

The second layer is the *comparison base*, and it is sharper. This budget is advertised as 25.2 percent larger than last year. But larger than last year's *what*? It is 25.2 percent larger than last year's *revised* estimate — the shrunken figure the previous government settled on partway through the year after admitting it could not spend or collect what it had originally planned. Last year's *original* budget was about NPR 1.964 trillion. Measured against that original figure, this year's budget is only about 8 percent bigger. The same budget can be sold as a dramatic 25 percent expansion or a modest 8 percent one, and both can be technically defensible, depending entirely on which base you quietly choose. The honest question is always: *bigger than what, exactly, and why did you pick that comparison?*

The third layer is the deepest: *a budget is a plan, not a fact*. The headline total is the amount the government *intends* to spend. It is not the amount it *will* spend. Nepal, as we will document in painful detail later, has a long and consistent record of failing to spend its capital budget — of allocating money for roads and bridges that the year ends without spending. A "record budget" that is never actually executed is a record only on paper. Celebrating the size of an intention is like congratulating someone on the size of their New Year's resolution.

So here is your defence, the move you can make every single year for the rest of your life: when you hear "the largest budget in history," ask three questions. Largest in *real* terms, after inflation, or just in plain rupees? Larger than last year's *original* plan, or than its quietly reduced revision? And is this the amount that will actually be *spent*, or merely the amount *announced*? Ask those three questions and the headline deflates from a triumph into what it actually is: a starting point for analysis, not a conclusion.

## A worked example: the journey of one hundred rupees

Abstractions are slippery, so let us make this concrete with a single small sum, and trace it through the machinery we have described.

Imagine a woman named Sita who runs a small shop in Pokhara. One afternoon she sells a bag of imported biscuits to a customer for 113 rupees. Of that price, 13 rupees is not really Sita's at all — it is *value-added tax*, a tax on the sale that Sita is legally required to collect from the customer and pass on to the government. (We will explain VAT properly in a later post; for now, notice only that the customer paid a tax without thinking about it, simply by buying biscuits — which is precisely why such taxes are so easy for a government to collect and so easy for citizens to overlook.)

Those 13 rupees travel from Sita's till into the government's revenue — part of that NPR 1,405 billion total we just met. Now the government, following the plan laid out in the budget, spends it. Suppose those particular rupees become part of the salary of a schoolteacher named Bishnu in a hill district — recurrent spending, the largest stream in the budget. Bishnu receives his pay, and with it he buys vegetables, pays his own children's fees, and fills a cylinder of cooking gas. The vegetable seller, the school, and the gas dealer each receive a slice, and each of them, in turn, will spend it again. The 13 rupees does not vanish into the government; it cycles back out into the economy and keeps moving, and at several points along the way it is taxed again.

But notice what the budget did *not* do with Sita's 13 rupees in our story. It did not build a road. Roads come out of the capital budget — the small stream — and if the capital budget goes unspent, as it so often does, then the road Sita drives to the wholesale market each week stays cracked and slow no matter how much was "allocated" for it. The same rupee that flowed smoothly into a salary may sit frozen when earmarked for a bridge, because spending on salaries is easy and spending on construction is hard. Hold on to that asymmetry. It is the seed of much of what is wrong with how Nepal — and many countries like it — actually spends.

This tiny story contains the whole budget in miniature: money raised quietly through taxes most people never notice, flowing easily into the costs of running the state, flowing reluctantly into the costs of building it, and recycling endlessly through the lives of ordinary people who are, every one of them, both the source of the money and its destination.

A useful discipline, and one we will practise throughout the series, is to *make the numbers reconcile*. When you force yourself to add a budget up rather than read it down, you discover quickly which parts the government wants you to look at and which parts it would rather you skimmed. That habit — totalling things the speech presents one line at a time — is the beginning of reading a budget like an auditor rather than an audience.

## A Note on Numbers and Assumptions

A few honest caveats, which will appear at the foot of every post in this series.

All US dollar figures in this post are approximations, given for intuition rather than accounting. They use a single assumed rate of **1 US dollar = 150 Nepalese rupees**, a round figure close to the Nepal Rastra Bank reference rate in mid-2026. The Nepalese rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee at a fixed 1.60 rupees per Indian rupee, so the rupee's value against the dollar moves only when the Indian rupee's does, and it drifts a little every day. Do not treat any dollar figure here as exact.

All rupee figures are *nominal* — that is, in the money of the day, not adjusted for inflation — unless we explicitly say otherwise. The budget figures are drawn from Nepal's FY 2083/84 budget statement and supporting documents. Some figures reported in secondary sources are rounded slightly differently from the official Red Book; where precision matters, the official Ministry of Finance documents are the authority.

Finally, the most important assumption of all, which we will repeat until it is second nature: a figure that has been *announced* in a budget is not the same as a figure that has been *enacted* into law, which is not the same as money that has actually been *spent* and turned into something real. Keep those three apart, and you are already reading this budget more clearly than most.
