The Dominion War lasted two years and eight months. By the standards of wars, this is not long. By the standards of what wars can cost in a short time, it was catastrophic.

When the war ended in late 2375, the Cardassian Union had suffered the deaths of over 800 million civilians — not combatants, not soldiers, but ordinary people — in a genocide ordered by a dying shapeshifter who had decided that if the Dominion was going to lose, it would drag its allies down with it. The federation fleet had lost hundreds of ships. The Klingon Empire had lost more than any accounting was prepared to calculate. The Romulan Star Empire had entered the war through a fabricated intelligence operation that killed a senator and would, if the full story ever emerged, represent one of the most significant violations of Federation ethics in recorded history.

And the man who had commanded Deep Space Nine for the duration of the war — who had forged the Romulan alliance through that deception, who had commanded the Defiant through more engagements than the record fully details, who had been the Emissary of the Prophets while simultaneously being a Starfleet officer — was gone. Not dead. But gone.

He had gone into a cave on Bajor and come out somewhere else entirely.

This is the account of how the war was won, what it cost, and what came after.

# Part One: The Shape of the War (2373–2374)

## How Bad It Got

The record of the war's early months is, by the standards of Starfleet reporting, unusually stark. The opening communiqués do not soften the numbers or bury the casualties in bureaucratic language. The Federation was losing. Not slowly, not in ways that could be described as strategic retreats toward a position of eventual strength. Losing.

The Dominion's advantages were structural. The **Jem'Hadar** did not fear death — this was not courage but biology, the product of genetic engineering that produced organisms without the survival instinct that makes ordinary soldiers hesitate at the wrong moment. They were dependent on **ketracel-white**, the compound the Vorta controlled, but they were reliably supplied with it. They were physically superior to humans in combat. And they had Cardassian ships, Cardassian territory, Cardassian industrial infrastructure.

The Federation had the Klingon alliance (restored when the Dominion threat became undeniable in 2373), eventual Romulan participation (which we will reach shortly), and — most crucially — the **wormhole**.

The wormhole was the strategic pivot of the entire war. The Dominion's forces in the Alpha Quadrant were substantial but not unlimited. Their ability to resupply and reinforce depended on transit through the Bajoran wormhole. Sisko had mined that passage at the outbreak of hostilities with self-replicating mines that replaced themselves as fast as the Dominion could destroy them. The mines held the Dominion to the forces already present. Without them, the war was unwinnable.

Deep Space Nine itself fell. When Dominion forces came in sufficient numbers, the station's crew evacuated and Sisko ordered the Defiant into open space, and the Dominion took Terok Nor back. **Dukat** held his old station again. The flag was changed. The plaques in the corridors were replaced. Bajoran artifacts were removed from public display.

The crew of Deep Space Nine became the crew of the Defiant, a warship on perpetual operations. This is not a metaphor. They lived aboard a military vessel, in sustained combat conditions, for the duration of the occupation. The record of this period — the six-episode arc of Season 6 — is among the most grounded documentation of what prolonged wartime service actually looks like from the inside: the routine exhaustion, the sustained moral weight, the ordinary competence in extraordinary circumstances.

## The Wormhole's Reopening and the Battle That Reclaimed the Station

In 2374, the Dominion found the mechanism to bring down the minefield. The mines could be disabled from inside, and the Dominion had the time and the resources to engineer the solution. Once the minefield fell, tens of thousands of Dominion ships from the Gamma Quadrant would pour through and the war would end in Dominion victory within weeks.

Sisko took the Defiant into the wormhole.

What happened inside the wormhole is documented in the mission records and disputed in its theological implications. Sisko was confronted by the Prophets — the non-linear beings who inhabited it — and made the argument that had become his central function as Emissary: he explained, in terms they could understand, the nature of linear existence, the nature of what the Dominion would do if it passed through, the nature of what it meant to the people the Prophets had been guiding for millennia to have their world subjugated. He asked them to act.

The Prophets stopped the Dominion fleet. An armada of thousands of ships simply vanished into the wormhole's depths and did not emerge. The Dominion forces holding Deep Space Nine found themselves without reinforcement, without supply, and without strategic position. They evacuated. The Federation retook the station.

The theological question — whether the Prophets intervened as divine protectors of Bajor, as non-linear beings acting on calculations incomprehensible to linear minds, or as something else entirely — was not resolved then and has not been resolved since. The military record simply notes: the fleet did not come through.

# Part Two: "In the Pale Moonlight" — The Price of Romulan Intervention (2374)

## The Problem

The Federation was winning back what had been lost but was not winning the war. The war was becoming a grind, measured in casualty lists that Sisko read every week and posted on a board in his office. The lists grew every week. The people on them had names and assignments and people who had known them.

The Federation needed a third front. The **Romulan Star Empire**, which had signed a non-aggression pact with the Dominion, possessed the military strength to tip the balance — three directions of pressure rather than two was a difference the Dominion could not absorb. Sisko knew this. He also knew that the Romulans had a non-aggression pact that they had carefully negotiated and that they would not abandon without compelling evidence that the Dominion intended to eventually betray them.

No such evidence existed. The Dominion was honoring the pact. The intelligence Sisko sent operatives to gather from what remained of Cardassia's networks came back either empty or dead — Garak's Cardassian contacts, one after another, died within days of being contacted, presumably killed before they could transmit anything useful.

The evidence did not exist. The war was still being lost.

## What Sisko Did

Sisko commissioned **Garak** to fabricate it.

A recording of a meeting between Dominion leadership discussing plans to eventually betray the Romulans. A holographic simulation detailed enough to pass inspection. A Romulan senator, **Vreenak**, whose intelligence access and whose political position made him the right audience — suspicious, powerful, and someone whose assessment of the evidence would be taken seriously by the Romulan government.

Grathon Tolar, a criminal forger whose work was necessary for the project, was acquired from a Klingon prison in exchange for his charges being dropped. He produced the recording. Vreenak came to Deep Space Nine under the pretense of other business. Sisko showed him the recording.

Vreenak examined it. He went quiet. He looked at Sisko with the specific Romulan expression that means the other person in the conversation has made a serious mistake. And he said: "It's a fake."

He left. The operation had failed. In a few days, he would tell the Romulan government that Sisko had attempted to manipulate them with forged evidence, and whatever slim diplomatic relationships remained between the Federation and the Romulan Empire would be destroyed.

Garak acted without Sisko's knowledge or authorization.

He planted a bomb on Vreenak's shuttle. The shuttle was destroyed. The explosion and the subsequent investigation produced a finding that suited Garak's purposes exactly: the recording that Vreenak had carried aboard was found in the wreckage, damaged by the explosion in precisely the ways that obscured the signs of forgery, and the damage pattern was consistent with Dominion sabotage. The Romulans concluded that the Dominion had assassinated their senator to prevent him from revealing the contents of the recording. The recording was now authenticated by implication.

The Romulans declared war on the Dominion.

Sisko confronted Garak in his quarters. Garak laid out the accounting with the precision of a man who had been an intelligence officer for decades and who had made peace with the mathematics of catastrophe: one forger, whose life had not been particularly valuable, had died in the explosion. One senator, who had served the empire that had been the enemy of Bajor through most of his career, had died in the explosion. The self-respect of one Starfleet captain had been compromised. In exchange, the Romulans had entered the war, and the war would now be won, and the billions of people in the Federation and the Klingon Empire and on Bajor and everywhere else the Dominion would eventually have reached would continue to live.

"I can live with it," Garak said. "Can you?"

Sisko recorded the entire sequence in his personal log and then deleted it. The record that survives is his account of deciding to delete it — his acknowledgment that what he had done was real, and that erasing the log did not erase the act, and that he was going to have to find a way to carry it.

"I lied," he told the empty room. "I cheated. I bribed men to cover my crimes. And in the end, I think it was worth it."

The pause after that sentence is the longest in the mission record.

"And I think that's the most terrifying thing about it."

The Romulans entered the war. A third front opened. The tide began to turn.

# Part Three: The Cardassian Uprising — Damar's Choice (2375)

## How the Cardsassians Became Colonized

The arc that produced **Legate Damar** is one of the more remarkable character transformations in the war record, precisely because it was so gradual and so reluctant and so completely without the dramatics of sudden conversion.

Damar had been Dukat's subordinate, then his ally, then his successor as leader of Cardassia. He had brought Cardassia into the Dominion alliance because Dukat had arranged it and because, at the time, it looked like the winning side. He had genuinely believed it would make Cardassia great again — that the Dominion's military strength combined with Cardassian territory and industrial capacity would produce an arrangement where Cardassia was an equal partner in empire rather than a conquered province.

What it produced instead was a conquered province.

The Dominion needed Cardassia's infrastructure. It did not need Cardassian input on strategic decisions. The Jem'Hadar were quartered in Cardassian cities and Cardassians were expected to accommodate them. When Cardassian military commanders objected to orders, the Vorta simply rerouted around them. When Cardassian casualties mounted and Damar asked for Dominion forces to reinforce Cardassian positions, he was informed that those forces were needed elsewhere and that Cardassian losses were an acceptable sacrifice.

The Klingons attacked the Eleventh Order on Septimus III. Damar requested Dominion assistance. The Dominion declined. The entire Eleventh Order was massacred.

Then the Dominion announced — without consulting Damar — that they had made a secret alliance with the **Breen Confederacy**, granting the Breen unspecified Cardassian territorial concessions in exchange for military participation. Territory that was not the Dominion's to give.

Damar requested an emergency meeting with Weyoun, the Vorta administrator whose cheerful, precise manner had become the daily face of the occupation. Weyoun explained the strategic necessity. Damar listened. Damar said he understood. Damar went to his office and began planning a rebellion.

## The Cardassian Liberation Front

The rebellion began publicly in 2375 when Damar broadcast a statement to Cardassia: the alliance with the Dominion was over. The Cardassian people had been used and were now being discarded. He was calling for resistance.

It was, initially, a military operation with no civilian base, a handful of loyal officers, and no resources that could be described as adequate to the task of fighting an occupying army on its own territory. Damar knew this. He did it anyway.

The Federation, recognizing that Damar's rebellion was tying down Dominion resources and creating supply disruptions that were helping the war effort on every front, provided what support it could: replicators, weapons, food. And one person who knew more about conducting a resistance against a military occupation than anyone in Starfleet: **Commander Kira Nerys**.

The partnership between Kira and Damar is among the most precisely observed character interactions in the record. Kira had spent her adolescence fighting the Cardassian occupation of Bajor — had killed Cardassians, had planted bombs in Cardassian installations, had watched her people die under Cardassian administration. Damar had been one of the Cardassians who administered that occupation. He had been stationed on Bajor. He had done what occupying forces do.

They were now, by the requirements of war, on the same side.

Kira did not let him forget what his people had done. When the Dominion found and executed Damar's wife and son — killed them to punish him and deter others — Damar raged at what kind of state murders innocent civilians. Kira looked at him and said: "Yeah, Damar. What kind of people give those orders?"

Damar went quiet.

Garak, who was present, observed afterward that if Damar was truly the leader of a new Cardassia, this was perhaps the moment when that became real — when the specific moral horror of what had been done to his family connected, in his own mind, to the moral horror of what had been done to Bajor, and the connection changed what he was capable of becoming.

## Damar's Death and What It Meant

The rebellion's organized military component was eventually crushed by the Dominion. Gul Revok, who had offered military support and half a million soldiers, was a Dominion collaborator who betrayed the location of every rebel base. In a single coordinated operation, the Dominion destroyed eighteen installations. The organized resistance was gone.

Damar, Kira, and Garak found themselves hiding in the basement of the house where Enabran Tain — Garak's father, the former head of the Obsidian Order — had lived. The city was hostile. The Dominion was looking for them. They appeared, by any reasonable accounting, to be finished.

What they found instead was that Damar had become a symbol. The Cardassian people — the ordinary civilians who had been told that the Dominion alliance would make their lives better, and who had watched it make their lives worse, year by year, until Jem'Hadar soldiers were executing their neighbors in the streets — had heard Damar's broadcast and made of it something the rebellion's military organizers had not planned for.

They made Damar into a hero. And heroes are harder to kill than rebel cells.

Damar, Kira, and Garak emerged from hiding and bombed a Jem'Hadar barracks in the open streets of Cardassia City. Damar made another broadcast. He called on every Cardassian on the planet to rise up. Cardassian military units that had been nominally cooperating with Dominion orders began defecting, began firing on the Jem'Hadar who had been quartered beside them, began choosing.

The uprising spread across Cardassia Prime simultaneously with the Federation Alliance fleet crossing into Dominion space for the final assault.

Damar was killed in the charge on Dominion Headquarters. He died having made the specific choice that defined his arc: when his remaining ally **Rusot** pulled a weapon on Kira and argued that they should take the Breen weapon for Cardassia alone and restore the old empire, Damar shot Rusot.

"He was my friend," Damar said. "But his Cardassia is dead, and it won't be coming back."

He was dead within hours of saying it. He had chosen the future over the past, and the future killed him before he could see what it became.

# Part Four: Section 31's Genocide — and the Price of Winning

## The Morphogenic Virus

Sometime in 2372, **Section 31** — the Federation's unauthorized intelligence service, which operated without oversight, without authorization, and without acknowledgment — had infected **Odo** with a disease.

Odo was a changeling. He was one of the Founders' people. When he returned to the Great Link, as he had been compelled to do in 2372 to face judgment for killing a Changeling, he carried the disease with him without knowing it. And when he linked with the Great Link — when he merged, as changelings do, into the shared consciousness — the disease spread.

The morphogenic virus attacked the changelings' ability to change form. In its terminal stages, it was fatal. The Founders, by 2375, were dying.

Section 31 had engineered a disease to commit genocide against the Founders. Not the Jem'Hadar, not the Vorta — the Founders themselves. The beings who were the ultimate leadership of the Dominion, the beings who Odo was one of, the beings who had created the Jem'Hadar and the Vorta specifically because they had been hunted by solids in the ancient past.

The Federation Council, when the virus was discovered, debated whether to share the cure. The strategic argument for withholding it was real: a dying Founder leadership was a weakened Dominion, which was an advantage in a war the Federation was still fighting. The ethical argument against withholding it was also real: withholding a cure from a dying species, regardless of what that species had done, was genocide by omission, and the Federation's founding principles did not authorize genocide as a war strategy.

The Federation Council decided to withhold the cure.

**Dr. Julian Bashir** did not accept this decision.

Bashir had discovered that Section 31 had engineered the virus and that Section 31 therefore had the cure. He lured a Section 31 agent named **Luther Sloan** to Deep Space Nine, extracted the cure from Sloan's mind through a process that killed Sloan, and produced the treatment. He intended to give it to the Founders.

The question of whether he was right to do so — whether preserving the Founders, whose recovery enabled the Dominion's continued resistance and thus prolonged the war and increased casualties on the Allied side — was one the record does not resolve. Bashir believed that there were acts that could not be authorized by the necessity of winning. The Federation Council believed that wartime strategy occasionally required acts that peace would not tolerate. Both positions are coherent. The record presents them in conflict and declines to adjudicate.

# Part Five: The Battle of Cardassia and the End of the War (2375)

## The Final Assault

The Allied fleet — more than a thousand ships from the Federation, the Klingon Empire, and the Romulan Star Empire — departed Deep Space Nine for Cardassia Prime in late 2375. The Klingon contribution was almost not made.

**Chancellor Gowron**, whose political position had become threatened by **General Martok**'s growing popularity among the Klingon people — popularity earned honestly, through sustained and effective military command — had responded by taking direct command of the fleet and ordering Martok on a series of increasingly suicidal missions. Gowron was attempting to have Martok killed or disgraced without having to directly challenge him.

Worf, understanding what was happening and understanding that the war effort could not survive Gowron's political maneuvering at the moment of the final engagement, challenged Gowron to personal combat and killed him. He then named Martok as Chancellor of the Klingon High Council.

This was, by Klingon law, entirely legitimate. It was also an extraordinary act in the context of Worf's biography — a man who had spent his adult life navigating the specific tension between his Klingon identity and his Starfleet service, who had been discommendated by the High Council and had had his honor restored, who had fought in a civil war on behalf of a Chancellor he believed to be legitimate, making the ultimate decision that the Chancellor he was serving was wrong and acting to remove him. He did this in the middle of a war, at the moment when Klingon participation was most critical. He did it because it was right.

Martok, taking command, led the Klingon fleet into the assault.

## The Genocide

The Female Changeling — the Founder who had been coordinating Dominion strategy in the Alpha Quadrant, and who was now in the final stages of the morphogenic virus — ordered the Jem'Hadar to begin systematically killing Cardassian civilians as the Allied fleet approached. Not as military strategy. As punishment. For every act of sabotage by the Cardassian uprising, one Cardassian city would be destroyed. For every Cardassian soldier who defected to the rebellion, the Jem'Hadar would execute a corresponding number of civilians.

The Jem'Hadar went from installation to installation. They executed people in the streets. They destroyed buildings. The city of **Lakarian City** was destroyed with all its inhabitants — a city of civilians, a city that had nothing to do with the rebellion — as a demonstration.

When the Cardassian soldiers in the Dominion-aligned fleet received this news, they turned their weapons on the Dominion ships beside them.

The Cardassian fleet changed sides in the middle of the battle. This was the act that broke the Dominion's defensive formation. The Allied fleet pressed through the gap. The Dominion and Breen forces fell back to Cardassia Prime.

## The Capture of the Founder and the Surrender

On the surface, Damar's remnants — Kira, Garak, and a handful of Cardassian volunteers — fought their way to Dominion Headquarters through the chaos of the planetary uprising. Most of them died in the approach. Damar died in the approach.

Kira and Garak reached the command center. They captured the Female Changeling. They captured **Weyoun** — or rather, the latest clone of Weyoun, the Vorta administrator whose series of cloned bodies had made him a recurring presence throughout the war. Garak shot him. He said nothing particular while doing it. He had wanted to for years.

The Female Changeling refused to surrender. She intended to order the Dominion and Breen forces to fight to the last soldier, ensuring that the Allied victory would cost the maximum possible price. She was dying and had decided that if the Founders were going to die, the process of defeating them would be as expensive as she could make it.

**Odo** beamed down to the command center.

He sat with her. He linked with her — the changeling form of direct contact, the sharing of consciousness that is the most intimate connection the species has. He shared with her what he had learned: that the fears of solids were real but not universal, that there were ways of existing in the quadrant that did not require domination, that the future he had built in the Alpha Quadrant was a demonstration. And he told her that he would cure her. He would cure her of the morphogenic virus that Section 31 had deployed. He would go to the Great Link and cure all of them. The disease that the Federation had made and refused to deploy the cure for — he would fix it.

In exchange, he asked for the surrender.

She agreed. The Dominion forces in the Alpha Quadrant surrendered unconditionally. The first time, in Dominion history, that they had ever done so in battle.

The **Declaration of Peace** was signed aboard Deep Space Nine. The Female Changeling was taken into Federation custody to stand trial for war crimes. Odo prepared to return to the Great Link.

The war was over. More than 800 million Cardassians were dead. The number of Federation, Klingon, and Romulan casualties across two and a half years of fighting is the subject of several dedicated historical analyses, none of which produce the same number, but all of which exceed one billion combined dead and missing.

Martok toasted the victory in bloodwine. Sisko and Admiral Ross declined to drink.

# Part Six: The Fire Caves — Sisko's Last Mission (2375)

## Dukat's Long Descent

**Gul Dukat** had not simply disappeared from the record after the retaking of Deep Space Nine. His arc following the loss of the station — following the death of his daughter Ziyal, killed by **Damar** in the chaos of the evacuation — had taken him from political leader to madman to something more dangerous: a true believer.

Dukat was brilliant. He was also, as the record had demonstrated across seven years, completely unable to distinguish between his own desire for significance and the truth of what he was. He had convinced himself that the Cardassian occupation of Bajor had been beneficial for the Bajorans. He had convinced himself that his alliance with the Dominion would make Cardassia great. He had convinced himself, after these positions became untenable, that he had a special destiny involving the Bajoran religion.

The **Pah-wraiths** — the beings who had been expelled from the wormhole at some point in the deep past, the adversaries of the Prophets in Bajoran religious cosmology — had found in Dukat's narcissism and spiritual emptiness a person who could serve their purposes. He had become their instrument.

His mission: to open the **Bajoran Fire Caves**, to release the Pah-wraiths from their imprisonment, and to use the **Book of the Kosst Amojan** — an ancient text containing the knowledge of how to accomplish this — to bring about the destruction of the Prophets and, as Dukat explained with complete composure, the eventual destruction of the Alpha Quadrant and the galaxy.

He accomplished the first step by manipulating **Kai Winn Adami**, the political head of Bajoran religion, whose faith in the Prophets had not survived her sense that the Prophets had never given her the recognition she deserved. Winn, who had spent years as the Prophets' official representative while never experiencing the direct contact with them that the Emissary had, was susceptible to Dukat's argument that the Pah-wraiths had been mischaracterized — that they were another form of the Prophets, unjustly exiled.

She helped him. She obtained the Book. She began the ritual.

The Prophets warned Sisko. He went to the Fire Caves.

## The Final Confrontation

What happened in the Bajoran Fire Caves is documented in what survive as mission logs filed by the crew of the Defiant, and in Kira's subsequent account, and in the indirect records of what the Prophets transmitted through the wormhole. The primary record is Sisko's personal log, which ends mid-sentence.

Dukat had acquired enough Pah-wraith power to be physically formidable in ways that Sisko, a human in a cave, could not match through conventional means. The Pah-wraiths rejected Kai Winn at the last moment — she was not the Emissary they wanted — and chose Dukat. His eyes glowed red. He forced Sisko to the ground. He had won.

Sisko had one option. He grabbed the Book of the Kosst Amojan — the text that contained the knowledge of how to release the Pah-wraiths permanently — and threw himself and Dukat into the Fire Caves' abyss.

The book burned. The Pah-wraiths were sealed back into their prison, along with Dukat, and the sealing was permanent in a way the previous imprisonment had not been — the Book that contained the knowledge of opening it was destroyed. They cannot be released. Dukat is with them. Whether this constitutes death or something else is a theological question the record declines to answer definitively.

Sisko did not fall. As he and Dukat dropped, a light took him.

He appeared to his wife **Kasidy Yates** — his pregnant wife — in a vision. He told her he was in the Celestial Temple. He told her he had things to learn from the Prophets and work still to do. He told her he would come back, though he could not say when. He said goodbye.

Kasidy stood in an empty room on Bajor holding the air where he had been.

Kira and O'Brien searched the cave system. They found no trace of Sisko. His runabout was in orbit. There was nothing else.

To the Bajoran people, the Emissary had completed his work and ascended to the Celestial Temple to be with the Prophets. To Starfleet, he was missing in action. Both statements are factually accurate. Neither captures what actually happened, which was something for which the available categories were insufficient.

Jake Sisko sat by a window of Deep Space Nine and looked at the wormhole for a long time.

# Part Seven: What They Left Behind — Character Fates After DS9

## The Crew Disperses

The end of the Dominion War and the departure of Sisko from the station produced the most significant personnel changes in Deep Space Nine's history.

**Odo** fulfilled his commitment to the Female Changeling. He returned to the Great Link and cured his people of the morphogenic virus. He remains among the Founders. What he has done to the shape of Dominion philosophy in the period since — whether his presence has moderated the Founders' approach to the solids who inhabit the quadrant with them — is not fully documented in the available record, but the inference is that it has.

**Miles O'Brien** left Deep Space Nine to teach at Starfleet Academy on Earth. He took his family — **Keiko** and their children — with him. He had been a working engineer in some of the most demanding operational environments in Starfleet history, and he brought that experience into the classroom. He was, by all accounts, exactly the kind of teacher that the experience of the war made necessary: someone who could explain what it was actually like to fix a warp core under fire.

**Worf** was appointed Federation Ambassador to Qo'noS. This position — the Federation's permanent diplomatic representative to the Klingon homeworld — suited him in ways that desk assignments in Starfleet had not. He understood the Klingons from the inside. He had served in their military, had killed their chancellor, had restored his own honor through their own legal processes. He could speak to them as someone who had lived what they celebrated.

**Garak** returned to Cardassia. He went back to a homeworld that had lost 800 million people, to a civilization that would need to be rebuilt from rubble, to a place where the old structures — the Cardassian Union, the Central Command, the Obsidian Order — were gone and whatever came next was genuinely undetermined. He had no allies there, no protections, no status. He had always described himself as a simple tailor. Now he would have to be something else. What exactly that was, he did not specify to Bashir in their last conversation.

"I hope we'll see each other again," Bashir said.

"I hope so too," Garak replied. "But I wouldn't count on it."

**Kira Nerys** was promoted to Colonel and remained on Deep Space Nine as its commanding officer. The woman who had begun her life as a Bajoran resistance fighter, who had spent years as first officer of a Starfleet installation she had never fully trusted, who had watched her religion's Emissary ascend into the wormhole, was now the station's commander. She was exactly the right person for it.

**Ezri Dax** — the new host of the Dax symbiont after Jadzia's death in 2374 — continued her Starfleet career. The Dax symbiont had seen seven lifetimes by this point and carried the memories of all of them, including Curzon's mentorship of Sisko, Jadzia's friendships and scientific achievements, and now Ezri's own contributions. The symbiont's continuity through hosts is itself a form of immortality that the record treats with appropriate complexity — the Dax that would board the next ship was continuous with the Dax that Sisko had called "old man," but the woman carrying that continuity was someone new.

**Jake Sisko** stayed on the station. His father had vanished into the wormhole. His father had promised to return. Jake stayed somewhere he could watch the wormhole and continue his work as a writer. The record notes this without editorial comment. The record does not need to editorialize.

# Part Eight: The Three TNG Films — First Contact, Insurrection, Nemesis

*While the Dominion War was unfolding in the Alpha Quadrant, the Enterprise-E and her crew were conducting their own confrontations with threats that, in the first case, were literally existential for the entire history of the Federation. These three films are presented here in chronological order alongside their war-era context.*

## First Contact (2373) — The Second Invasion

The **Battle of Sector 001** began while the *Enterprise*-E was on assignment near the Romulan Neutral Zone, kept there by Starfleet Command because Picard's history with the Borg was considered a liability — his judgment was believed to be compromised in ways that made him unsuitable for the engagement. He had been Locutus. The admirals were afraid he was still, in some part, the Borg.

They were wrong to keep him away, and they knew they were wrong within hours of making the decision. The fleet at Sector 001 was being destroyed. The Borg cube was advancing on Earth. Picard disobeyed orders and brought the *Enterprise* to the engagement.

His knowledge of the Borg — the specific, intimate, agonizing knowledge of the Collective that came from having been Locutus — told him exactly where to concentrate firepower. He identified a single point on the cube's hull that was not a structural weakness but a nexus — a location so central to the cube's function that its destruction would cascade through the entire vessel. He directed the fleet's firepower to this point. The cube was destroyed.

Before its destruction, a smaller Borg vessel — a sphere — ejected from the cube and created a temporal vortex. The *Enterprise* followed it in.

The Borg's plan was not to win in 2373. Their plan was to go back to 2063 — to the day before **Zefram Cochrane**'s first warp flight — and prevent first contact with the Vulcans, preventing the formation of the Federation entirely. On an Earth still recovering from World War III, humanity without first contact would have been conquered by the Borg before Starfleet existed.

The *Enterprise* destroyed the Borg sphere over Montana. An away team secured the warp ship, the **Phoenix**, and Cochrane himself — a man who, as it emerged, had built the most historically significant vessel in human history primarily for financial gain, and who was considerably less heroic in person than his later mythology suggested. He was drunk. He was scared. He did not want to be the figure the Enterprise crew described.

**Data** was captured by the Borg, who had established a beachhead in the *Enterprise*'s lower decks and were assimilating the ship deck by deck. The **Borg Queen** — a being who expressed the Collective through a specific individual form, who was in some sense the consciousness that the Collective used to interact with individuals — grafted human skin onto Data and offered him what he had always wanted: the physical sensations that his android construction precluded. He could feel the new skin. He could feel touch. He experienced it as the thing he had always been told he was missing.

The Queen wanted his encryption codes. She believed she had Data. She had miscalculated.

Picard, on the ship, confronted his own breakdown. He had been given command of the escape pods and ordered to evacuate while Worf and a small team held the remaining unassimilated decks. He could not make himself give the order. He was destroying Borg drones with a Tommy gun from a holodeck program and he did not want to stop. **Lily Sloane** — the 21st-century woman who had been beamed aboard for medical treatment and who had no knowledge of the future she had accidentally arrived in — told him, without diplomatic softening, that he was doing the same thing Ahab had done, that he was prepared to destroy everything rather than acknowledge that the obsession was the problem.

He ordered the evacuation. He stayed behind to get Data.

Data, given the signal he had been waiting for, ruptured the coolant tanks. The plasma dissolved the Borg Queen's organic components. The new skin on Data's arm burned away. Data fired the one torpedo that sealed the timeline: when the Phoenix made its warp flight, the Borg sphere's attack on Cochrane's launch site was already history. The timeline was preserved.

Cochrane, on the morning of April 5, 2063, walked out to meet the Vulcan ship that had detected his warp signature. He held out his hand in a clumsy attempt at a Vulcan greeting. The Vulcan gave him the salute. "Live long and prosper."

"Thanks," said Cochrane.

The *Enterprise* crew watched from orbit and then slipped back to the 24th century without being noticed. The crew they had sent off in escape pods returned. The ship headed back to Earth for repairs.

Picard stood in his ready room and told no one in particular: "I'll be alright."

## Insurrection (1998) — Six Hundred People

The **Ba'ku** were 600 people living on a planet in a region of space called the **Briar Patch** — an unstable zone of hazardous phenomena that made navigation difficult and discouraged visitors. They had been there for three hundred years, having fled their original civilization when it nearly destroyed itself through weapons development. On the Ba'ku planet, a specific metaphasic radiation from the planet's rings slowed cellular aging to the point of effective immortality. The Ba'ku did not age. They had been 600 people, living the same unhurried life, for three centuries.

The **Son'a** were a dying species — physically deteriorating, dependent on medical technology to delay death, looking for any solution that could extend their lives. Their relationship to the Ba'ku was the central revelation of the film: the Son'a were the Ba'ku's own children. Three centuries earlier, a faction of young Ba'ku had tried to seize control of their colony and been expelled. Outside the planet's rings, they aged. They became the Son'a. Their grievance against their parents was three hundred years old and entirely personal.

The Federation's involvement was brokered by **Admiral Dougherty** who had made an arrangement: the Son'a would provide the technology to harvest the planet's radiation, the Federation would study the medical applications, and the Ba'ku would be relocated — without their knowledge or consent, using a holoship that would recreate their village in enough detail that they might not notice the transition until it was too late.

Picard discovered the holoship in the lake. He found Data's malfunctioning body near where the covert Federation observation post had been — Data had caught Dougherty's team in the act and been shot for it, his positronic systems destabilized by a Son'a weapon. Picard confronted the Admiral.

He refused to carry out the relocation.

Dougherty told him it was only 600 people. Picard asked: "How many people does it take, Admiral, before it becomes wrong? A thousand? Fifty thousand? A million? How many people does it take?"

Picard, with a handful of his senior officers, led the Ba'ku into the mountains while Riker took the *Enterprise* out of the Briar Patch to transmit what was happening to Starfleet Command. The Son'a pursued. Dougherty, finally understanding that Ru'afo had gone beyond any agreement the Federation had authorized, tried to stop the relocation — and was killed by Ru'afo, who had waited 300 years to retrieve his mother planet and was not going to let one Federation admiral's last-minute conscience interfere.

The collector was destroyed. Ru'afo died in the explosion. The Ba'ku stayed on their planet. The Federation Council ordered the relocation plans suspended pending review.

The philosophical question the film raises — whether it is acceptable to forcibly relocate 600 people to provide medical benefits to billions — was not answered by the film, which framed the operation's wrongness through the Federation's failure of process and consent rather than through a direct engagement with the utilitarian calculation. Picard's position is that some things are wrong regardless of the math. The film suggests this is the correct position. The record does not adjudicate this definitively.

The Riker-Troi relationship, which had been unresolved since the first episode of TNG, was rekindled in the Briar Patch's rejuvenating radiation. They were married two years later.

## Nemesis (2002) — What Picard Might Have Become

In 2379, the Romulan Senate was assassinated. A device left in the chamber emitted thalaron radiation — the most lethal substance known to applied physics, capable of consuming all organic matter in a space within seconds — and turned the senators to dust while they were still in session. A new Praetor took power: **Shinzon** of Remus.

Shinzon was human. He was young. He had led twelve successful military engagements in the Dominion War as the commander of Reman troops — the Remans being the slave species of the Romulan Empire, humanoid, pale, nocturnal, used as miners and expendable assault forces. Shinzon had made himself their leader. He had then, with the support of several Romulan military commanders and a compliant senator who arranged Senator Tal'aura's absence from the chamber during the assassination, seized the Empire.

He requested Federation diplomatic contact. The *Enterprise* was sent.

Shinzon was a clone of Picard. The Romulans, years earlier, had created him to plant a deep-cover operative in the heart of Starfleet — a Picard who was secretly Romulan, who would rise to command through apparent merit. The project had been abandoned when the political leadership changed and the plan became inconvenient. Shinzon, left on Remus as a small child, had survived the mines by forming an alliance with a Reman who protected him, had learned to survive and then to lead, and had grown into a man whose intelligence and tactical capability matched the original's but whose experience was built entirely from slavery and war.

The cloning process, it emerged, was killing him. Temporal RNA sequencing that had not been properly activated meant his body was deteriorating. The only possible treatment was a complete transfusion of blood from the only compatible donor: Picard. Shinzon's request for diplomatic contact was a pretext for getting Picard close enough to take.

He also had, aboard his warship the **Scimitar**, a thalaron weapon of planetary scale. Not a room-clearing device but a world-killing one. His plan was to use it on Earth, destroying the Federation's seat of government and the planet's eight billion inhabitants in a single discharge.

**Data** was taken by Shinzon. The Borg Queen had offered Data sensation; Shinzon offered him nothing, but held him nonetheless. Data had anticipated the capture and had pre-arranged his own extraction.

The confrontation in the Bassen Rift — a region that blocked subspace communications, isolating the *Enterprise* from any support — was the most destructive engagement the ship had survived. Shinzon deployed the Scimitar's cloak, fired while invisible, and the *Enterprise* sustained damage that should have been fatal. Picard rammed the Scimitar. The ships locked together, both crippled.

Picard boarded the Scimitar alone and killed Shinzon with a metal strut through the abdomen, in a chamber that was activating the thalaron weapon. He could not disable the weapon. He was going to die in the discharge.

Data crossed the gap between the ships — crossed the vacuum, in a jump that would have killed a human — using an emergency transport unit that could carry one person. He gave it to Picard and used it to beam Picard off the Scimitar. Then he fired a phaser at the thalaron weapon's generator.

The Scimitar exploded.

Data was killed.

## The Measure of Data's Death

The question that Data's life had raised — whether he was a person, whether he had rights, whether the thing he was doing constituted genuine experience — had never been formally resolved. The legal ruling had protected his practical situation without answering the philosophical question. His death arrived before the philosophical question was answered.

What his death produced in the people who had known him was, by any available definition, grief. Riker stood in the debris field and could not speak. Picard returned to the ship and wrote the ship's log and was unable to complete a sentence about what Data had done.

Before the engagement, Data had downloaded his memories — the full record of his experience — into **B-4**, an earlier android prototype who had been found on Kolarus III at the beginning of this mission, having been planted there by Shinzon as bait. B-4 had Data's memories now but was not, in the ways that mattered, Data. He could not process what he had received. He sang, at one point, a fragment of the song Data had performed at Riker and Troi's wedding — a signal that something remained, that the memories were there — but whether they would ever integrate into something like the person they came from was unknown.

Riker left to command the **USS Titan**. The Titan was the first ship dispatched to begin the peace negotiations with what remained of the Romulan government after Shinzon's coup.

Picard remained on the *Enterprise*, commanding it, continuing to live in the space where Data had stood for fifteen years.

# Part Nine: The Destruction of Romulus (2387)

## The Hobus Supernova

In 2387, the star **Hobus** went supernova in a way that defied the conventional physics of stellar events. The supernova expanded at a rate that should have been impossible — propagating outward far beyond the normal radial limit, carrying the destructive energy of the stellar explosion with it as it moved through subspace.

The Romulan Star Empire's homeworld, Romulus — home to the species whose ancestors had been Vulcans, who had maintained their own civilization for two thousand years, whose politics had defined the Federation's relationship to the outside for over two centuries — was in the path of the expanding shock wave.

**Ambassador Spock** was on Romulus. He had been there, with varying degrees of continuity, since 2368 — working with the reunification underground, offering what he could to the people who were trying to build a different kind of Romulan society. He was 162 years old.

He obtained from the Vulcan Science Academy a quantity of **red matter** — a substance capable of creating an artificial singularity — and equipped a small vessel, the **Jellyfish**, with it. The plan: fly ahead of the supernova wave, deploy the red matter, create a singularity that would absorb the explosion before it reached Romulus.

He was too late.

Romulus was destroyed. The Romulan homeworld, with its entire population, was consumed by the Hobus supernova before Spock could reach his intercept point.

He deployed the red matter anyway — stopping the supernova from propagating further, preventing its destruction from reaching additional worlds. The resulting singularity was unstable. The Jellyfish and the singularity were swept into a temporal anomaly. Spock, along with a Romulan mining vessel called the **Narada** whose crew had been present for the destruction of their world, were pulled through the singularity and deposited in a different point in time.

This is the point at which the Prime Timeline and what Federation scholars call the **Kelvin Timeline** diverge. What happened to Spock and the Narada in the past of the Kelvin Timeline is the subject of a separate body of scholarship, which this account will not address. The Prime Timeline continues without Spock, who is presumed missing and whose fate in the Prime Timeline cannot be confirmed from available records.

## What the Destruction of Romulus Meant

Romulus's destruction was the largest single loss of life in the quadrant's recorded history. The Romulan people were not extinct — Romulan colonists and ships and people throughout the Empire and beyond survived — but the homeworld, the seat of Romulan civilization, the planet that had been the center of Romulan political, cultural, and military identity for two millennia, was gone.

The Federation's response to the disaster had, in the years prior to its occurrence, included a commitment from Admiral Picard to oversee a massive evacuation effort — moving Romulan populations from the system before the supernova arrived. This effort was cut short in 2385 when an attack on the **Utopia Planitia** shipyards on Mars by rogue synthetic workers destroyed fourteen starships and killed 92,000 people, and the Federation Council, responding to public pressure and to the apparent threat posed by synthetic life, suspended the evacuation and imposed a ban on synthetic lifeforms.

Picard resigned from Starfleet in protest. The evacuation was not completed. Romulus was destroyed.

The relationship between these events — the attack on Mars, the synthetic ban, the failed evacuation, and who actually ordered the attack and why — is the subject of the next chapter, which covers the period from 2387 through 2401 and the events of *Star Trek: Picard*.

# Conclusion: Between Eras

The years from 2373 through 2387 produced four defining losses for the Prime Timeline: the Dominion War's cost in lives; Sisko's departure from linear existence; Data's death; and the destruction of Romulus.

Each of them changed the shape of what came next. The Dominion War produced a Federation that had paid a price it had not paid before, that had done things it was not supposed to do, and that had survived by making compromises its founding documents did not authorize. The war was won. The winning cost something.

Sisko's departure left Bajor with an Emissary who had become a Prophet — present in the wormhole, present in the Prophets' non-linear consciousness, gone from the table where his crew ate their meals. The station continued. Kira commanded it. Jake waited.

Data's death left an empty place in the record of what was possible — a reminder that the question of his personhood, which had been argued and protected and legally maneuvered around for fifteen years, had ended without an answer, and that the ending of an unresolved argument is not the same thing as resolving it.

And the destruction of Romulus left the Alpha Quadrant's most persistent adversary, and most complicated potential partner, suddenly without a home — scattered, grieving, and (as the next era would demonstrate) capable of responses to that grief that would take decades to fully understand.

The modern era begins in the wreckage of all of this. It is where we go next.
