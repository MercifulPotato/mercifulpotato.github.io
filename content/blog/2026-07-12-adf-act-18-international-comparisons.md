The creation of a government-backed development finance institution is not a new idea. Countries have been establishing institutions of this type since at least the 1940s, and the evidence base for what makes them succeed or fail is extensive, even if it is not always well-organized or easy to access. Nepal is not inventing something for the first time with the Alternative Development Finance Fund. It is attempting something that dozens of other countries have attempted, with results ranging from transformative to catastrophic. Examining four countries at comparable development stages — Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Ethiopia, and Mongolia — provides a grounded basis for both optimism and caution.

The comparison is imperfect because each country's context differs from Nepal's in important ways. Bangladesh has a different economic structure and a stronger export manufacturing sector. Sri Lanka has a different geography, different geopolitical position, and, as of the early 2020s, a cautionary tale of macroeconomic crisis directly related to infrastructure financing choices. Ethiopia has a centralized political system with a developmental state model quite different from Nepal's federal democratic structure. Mongolia has a small population, large natural resource wealth, and a governance environment shaped by nomadic cultural traditions and a post-Soviet political transition. None of these countries is Nepal. But all of them faced versions of the same core challenge: how to mobilize capital for infrastructure investment in a low-income country where private markets are thin, public resources are constrained, and the stakes of getting it wrong are high.

**Bangladesh Development Bank: The Infrastructure Finance Company**

Bangladesh, with a population of approximately 170 million and a per capita income in the same general range as Nepal's over the past two decades, has pursued infrastructure financing through several institutional mechanisms. The most directly relevant for comparison purposes is the Bangladesh Infrastructure Finance Fund, established in 2011 with capitalization from the government and technical support from the Asian Development Bank and other development partners. The fund was designed to provide long-term debt financing to infrastructure projects — initially primarily in energy — by borrowing from the capital market and on-lending to project developers at rates and tenors that commercial banks in Bangladesh would not offer.

Bangladesh's infrastructure financing experience offers several lessons. The positive lesson is that a dedicated infrastructure finance institution, even one operating in a difficult environment, can fill a genuine gap in project financing. Bangladesh's energy sector investment accelerated significantly in the years following the establishment of the infrastructure financing vehicle, as projects that could not previously access appropriately-structured long-term debt gained access to it. The country moved from chronic power shortages to a position of adequate and eventually surplus generation capacity, roughly paralleling Nepal's own trajectory but on a faster timeline given Bangladesh's different starting point and scale.

The cautionary lesson from Bangladesh relates to the quality of investments made during the rush to expand generation capacity. In the mid-2010s, Bangladesh entered into a large number of rental power agreements — short-term contracts with independent power producers under which the government paid a capacity charge regardless of whether the plant actually produced power — in order to address immediate shortfalls rapidly. These agreements, while successful in their immediate purpose, created long-term financial obligations for the Bangladesh Power Development Board that have subsequently become a fiscal burden, as the power is no longer needed at the scale contracted and the capacity charges continue regardless. This is a version of a problem that any development finance institution faces: the pressure to deploy capital quickly can lead to commitments whose full financial implications are not appreciated at the time they are made.

**Sri Lanka: A Warning at Scale**

Sri Lanka's infrastructure financing story is the most important cautionary tale in the neighborhood and it must be engaged with directly and honestly rather than glossed over. Sri Lanka, which for most of the 2000s and 2010s was treated as a development success story in the South Asian context — a middle-income country with good social indicators, growing per capita income, and an ambitious infrastructure program — experienced a complete sovereign debt crisis in 2022, requiring a bailout from the International Monetary Fund and involving, for the first time in Sri Lanka's modern history, the suspension of debt payments to external creditors.

The causes of Sri Lanka's crisis were multiple and disputed in their relative weights, but the role of infrastructure-related borrowing is central to any honest account. In the years following the end of Sri Lanka's civil war in 2009, the government of Mahinda Rajapaksa pursued an ambitious infrastructure program financed heavily through external commercial borrowing and concessional loans, primarily from China. The program included roads, ports, airports, and other facilities, some of which were genuinely valuable additions to Sri Lanka's infrastructure stock and some of which were white elephants — facilities constructed in locations and at scales that the underlying demand did not justify. The Hambantota Port, developed with Chinese financing in the home district of then-President Rajapaksa, became one of the most internationally discussed examples of infrastructure development driven more by political considerations than economic analysis. When the port did not generate the revenue needed to service its construction debt, Sri Lanka eventually agreed to a 99-year lease arrangement with China Merchants Port Holdings in exchange for debt relief — an arrangement that attracted enormous international attention as a potential model of how infrastructure debt can translate into geopolitical leverage.

The Sri Lankan crisis is not simply the story of Hambantota, and its causes include tax cuts, pandemic-related revenue losses, currency management decisions, and a political system that prioritized short-term popularity over fiscal sustainability. But the infrastructure borrowing decisions of the Rajapaksa era made a significant contribution to the debt burden that eventually became unserviceable. The specific mechanisms — external commercial borrowing in foreign currency to finance domestic infrastructure, inadequate project selection processes, political capture of investment decisions, governance structures that did not provide effective oversight of borrowing decisions — are exactly the failure modes that Nepal's Alternative Development Finance Fund legislation is designed to prevent.

Reading Sri Lanka as a warning rather than simply as a distant disaster is the appropriate response for Nepali policymakers. The Fund's design incorporates several features explicitly targeted at the failure modes evident in Sri Lanka: independent CEO selection, Auditor General oversight, triennial international evaluation, parliamentary review requirements. Whether these features prove adequate depends on whether they are actually implemented with the independence and rigor they require, or whether they are nominally complied with while political influence runs through informal channels. The history of development finance institutions in the developing world provides examples of both outcomes.

**Ethiopia: The Development State Model and Its Limits**

Ethiopia under the governments of Meles Zenawi and his successors pursued a model of infrastructure-led development that was explicitly and ambitiously statist. The Ethiopian government identified infrastructure investment as the primary driver of development, channeled public investment at remarkable scale through state-owned institutions, and financed this investment through a combination of domestic resource mobilization and external borrowing — including significant Chinese lending — at a pace that made Ethiopia one of Africa's fastest-growing economies for most of the period from 2004 to 2020.

The Ethiopian Development Bank and the Commercial Bank of Ethiopia, working alongside sectoral development institutions in energy, telecommunications, and sugar, were the primary domestic instruments of this strategy. The Commercial Bank of Ethiopia was directed to hold large portfolios of low-interest bonds issued by state-owned enterprises — effectively using ordinary depositors' savings to finance state infrastructure programs at below-market rates. This created internal financial transfers within the economy and avoided some of the external debt risks that ultimately affected Sri Lanka, but it came at the cost of suppressing financial returns to ordinary savers and potentially misallocating capital that might have been more productively deployed in private sector lending.

The Ethiopian model's results were genuinely mixed. The infrastructure investment was real: Ethiopia built the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, one of Africa's largest hydropower facilities; the Addis Ababa light rail, the first urban light rail in sub-Saharan Africa; significant road network expansion; and numerous other facilities. These contributed to growth and to improvements in services that benefited ordinary Ethiopians. The limits of the model appeared in debt sustainability — by the early 2020s, Ethiopia's external debt had reached levels that required renegotiation and a formal IMF program — and in governance — the state-led model concentrated economic power in ways that were ultimately difficult to separate from political power, contributing to the internal conflicts that became acute around 2020 and after.

Nepal cannot and should not replicate the Ethiopian model, which required a degree of centralized political authority that is inconsistent with Nepal's federal democratic structure and political culture. But the Ethiopian experience is instructive about the scale at which infrastructure investment can be pursued if institutional mechanisms are aligned, and about the risks of debt sustainability that arise when that pursuit is not adequately constrained by fiscal discipline.

**Mongolia: Natural Resources, Dutch Disease, and the Pro-Cyclicality Trap**

Mongolia's experience with development finance is the most directly cautionary about a specific risk that Nepal may not immediately recognize as relevant: the relationship between natural resource revenues, development finance institutions, and pro-cyclical investment behavior.

Mongolia established its development finance institution — Mongolian Development Bank — in 2011, at a moment of extraordinary optimism about Mongolia's future. The country sat atop one of the world's largest deposits of coal and copper, commodity prices were at historic highs, the Oyu Tolgoi copper and gold mine was under development with Rio Tinto investment, and projections of Mongolian per capita income growth were remarkably bullish. The Development Bank was capitalized with government funds, was mandated to finance infrastructure and development projects, and was expected to leverage Mongolia's resource wealth for transformative investment.

The subsequent decade was a chastening experience. Commodity prices fell sharply, Mongolian government revenues disappointed, the Development Bank's capital proved insufficient for its mandate, and the institution became entangled in political disputes about which projects deserved financing — disputes that were particularly intense because the bank's lending decisions could effectively determine which politically connected businesspeople received financing for infrastructure concessions. The bank accumulated losses, required government recapitalization, and became a source of fiscal risk rather than a source of investment leverage. Mongolia's experience of boom-time optimism giving way to fiscal strain and institutional disappointment is a pattern that has repeated in natural-resource-dependent developing countries across multiple continents.

Nepal does not have Mongolia's mineral wealth, but it has a version of the same structural risk in its hydropower potential. A government that is confident about the transformative potential of the revenue that large-scale hydropower export will generate may be tempted to front-load development spending based on those revenue expectations before the projects are actually built and generating. A development finance institution that is created during a period of optimism about the infrastructure pipeline may take on financing commitments whose serviceability depends on project revenue streams that do not materialize on the assumed timeline. The Fund's management of its own portfolio risk — ensuring that its investment commitments are sized relative to its actual capital rather than projected future capital, and that projects are evaluated on conservative revenue assumptions — will be essential to avoiding a version of the Mongolian experience.

**What the Evidence Collectively Suggests**

The international comparison, read honestly, supports both the creation of the Fund and a set of specific concerns about how it will operate.

The case for the Fund is supported by evidence from Bangladesh: a dedicated infrastructure finance institution, properly governed and working with multilateral development bank partners, can mobilize capital for projects that the market would not otherwise finance, and the resulting infrastructure investment can accelerate economic development in ways that benefit ordinary people. This is not a theoretical claim. It has happened in multiple countries in ways that are documented and measurable.

The concerns are specific and actionable. The Sri Lankan and Mongolian experiences suggest that the greatest risks are governance failure — political capture of investment decisions — and debt sustainability — taking on financial commitments whose scale is not matched to the institution's actual capacity and whose assumptions about project revenues are optimistic rather than conservative. The Ethiopian experience suggests that state-led development finance can deliver infrastructure but carries risks of concentrated power and external debt sustainability that require active management.

For Nepal specifically, the combination of the Fund's governance design and the oversight mechanisms in the legislation represents a genuine attempt to learn from these precedents. The test is not whether these mechanisms exist in the legislation but whether they are implemented with actual independence in practice — whether the CEO selection process will be free of political pressure, whether the Auditor General will have the access and resources to conduct meaningful oversight, whether the parliamentary review will examine the Fund's performance with serious scrutiny rather than rubber-stamping it, and whether the triennial international evaluation will be conducted by evaluators with genuine independence and expertise rather than by consultants selected for their willingness to produce favorable reports.

Nepal has the advantage of having this precedent base available. Countries that created development finance institutions in the 1970s and 1980s did so without this evidence. Nepal is creating its Fund with decades of international experience to draw on. Whether that advantage is translated into better design and better implementation — or whether the same mistakes are repeated because the political economy of infrastructure finance creates the same incentives regardless of what has been learned elsewhere — is the question that Nepal's coming years will answer.
