For the next several posts we turn from the budget's overall shape to the part of it that reaches into citizens' lives most directly: taxes. We begin with the one most people think of first when they hear the word — income tax — because this budget makes the most dramatic change to it that Nepal has seen in over a decade. The government has doubled the amount of income that is exempt from tax, and cut the top rate sharply. These changes will put real money back into the pockets of many Nepalis, and they have been welcomed loudly. But they also cost the treasury revenue, in a budget that is already borrowing nearly a third of what it spends, and they raise hard questions about who actually benefits and whether "tax relief" is as even-handed as the phrase suggests.

This post explains income tax from the ground up, without a single formula, because the concepts are not difficult once stripped of their mathematical disguise. We will learn what income tax is, how the system of brackets or slabs works, and — most importantly — the difference between your marginal rate and your effective rate, a distinction that almost everyone gets wrong and that the deception at the end of this post exploits. Then we will examine exactly what this budget changed, who gains the most from it, what it costs, and whether it is fair. By the end you will be able to read any income-tax change — this one or any future one — and see past the headline to who really wins and who really pays.

## What is income tax?

Start with the plain idea. **Income tax** is a tax on the money people earn. When you have income — wages from a job, profits from a business, and certain other earnings — the government takes a share of it as tax. This is one of the oldest and most important sources of government revenue, and it has a particular character that distinguishes it from the consumption taxes (VAT, customs, excise) we will examine in the next post: income tax is levied on what you *earn*, whereas consumption taxes are levied on what you *spend*. That difference matters enormously for fairness, as we will see, because earning and spending are distributed very differently across a population.

Income tax can apply to individuals — tax on the wages and earnings of people — and to businesses, where it is usually called corporate income tax, levied on companies' profits. This post focuses on the *personal* income tax that individuals pay on their earnings, because that is what this budget has changed most dramatically; we will examine corporate tax separately in the post on business and investment. For an individual, then, the basic idea is simple: you earn money over the year, and the government takes a share of it. The whole question — and the whole subject of this post — is *how much* of a share, and how that share varies depending on how much you earn. Because income tax is almost never a flat, single percentage taken from everyone equally. It is, in Nepal as in most countries, *progressive* — and understanding what that means is the heart of the matter.

## Slabs, brackets, and the meaning of "progressive"

Here is the single most important concept for understanding income tax, and it is one that confuses an astonishing number of people, including people who pay it every year: income is not all taxed at the same rate. It is taxed in *slabs* — also called brackets or bands — and each successive slab of income is taxed at a higher rate than the one below it. This is what makes the tax *progressive*: the more you earn, the higher the rate applied to your *additional* income, so that higher earners pay a larger *share* of their income in tax than lower earners. The intention behind a progressive structure is a notion of fairness — that those with greater ability to pay should contribute a larger proportion — and it is a near-universal feature of income-tax systems around the world.

Let us understand how slabs work with a clear, schematic illustration — and a crucial warning, which we will repeat: the *actual* slab rates and thresholds in Nepal's system are set by the Finance Act and detailed in the Inland Revenue Department's circulars, and they include particular features (such as a low nominal rate on the first slab that functions partly as a social-security contribution, and different thresholds for single individuals and married couples). We will *not* reproduce the precise band table here from memory, because doing so risks stating wrong numbers, and being wrong about something this specific would betray the care this series promises. So the illustration that follows uses *invented, round numbers* purely to explain the *mechanism*; the real figures must come from the Finance Act 2083 and the IRD circulars.

Imagine, purely for illustration, a system with these invented slabs: the first portion of income, up to some threshold, is taxed at a very low rate or not at all; the next portion is taxed at, say, 10 percent; the next at 20 percent; the next at 30 percent; and the highest portion at some top rate. Now suppose a person earns an income that reaches into the middle slabs. Here is the point almost everyone gets wrong: that person does *not* pay the rate of their highest slab on their *entire* income. They pay the low rate on the first portion, the 10 percent rate only on the portion that falls in the 10 percent slab, the 20 percent rate only on the portion in the 20 percent slab, and so on. The higher rate of an upper slab applies *only to the income that falls within that slab*, not to the income below it. Income is taxed slab by slab, each portion at its own slab's rate, and the rates climb only as you move up through the slabs. This is the mechanism, and grasping it is the key to everything else, because it leads directly to the distinction between two very different "rates" that people constantly confuse.

## Marginal rate versus effective rate

From the slab system come two different meanings of "your tax rate," and confusing them is the source of endless misunderstanding and of a specific deception we will expose shortly.

Your **marginal rate** is the rate applied to your *next* rupee of income — the rate of the *highest* slab your income reaches. If your income climbs into the slab taxed at 30 percent, then your marginal rate is 30 percent: any additional rupee you earn, up at that level, is taxed at 30 percent. The marginal rate is the rate "at the margin," on the last and next slice of income. It is the number people usually have in mind when they say "I'm in the 30 percent bracket" or "the top rate is 39 percent."

Your **effective rate** is something quite different: it is the share of your *entire* income that you actually pay in tax, taking the whole slab structure into account. Because the lower slabs of your income were taxed at lower rates (or not at all), the share of your *total* income that goes to tax is *lower* — usually much lower — than your marginal rate. A person whose marginal rate is 30 percent does not pay 30 percent of their whole income; they pay the low rate on the bottom slab, 10 percent on the next, 20 percent on the next, and 30 percent only on the top slice — so the share of their *total* income that goes to tax, their effective rate, might be far below 30 percent. The effective rate is what you actually pay as a fraction of everything you earned; the marginal rate is only the rate on your topmost slice.

This distinction is not a technicality; it is the difference between understanding a tax system and being deceived by it. When someone says "the top tax rate is 39 percent" and you imagine high earners handing over 39 percent of everything they make, you have confused the marginal rate with the effective rate — and you have wildly overestimated what high earners actually pay, because their effective rate, the share of their whole income that goes to tax, is considerably lower, since most of their income was taxed in the lower slabs at lower rates. Conversely, when a government cuts the *top marginal rate*, the effect on what high earners actually pay — their effective rate — is real but smaller than the headline change suggests, because it affects only the rate on their topmost slice of income, not on the rest. Keep marginal and effective firmly distinct: the marginal rate is the dramatic headline number; the effective rate is the quieter truth of what is actually paid. The whole art of reading an income-tax change is translating between the two.

## What this budget changed

With the mechanism understood, we can examine exactly what the Finance Bill 2083 does to personal income tax — and it makes two large changes, both of which reduce what individuals pay.

The first change is to the **exemption threshold** — the amount of income on which a person pays no income tax, or only a nominal charge. This budget *doubles* that threshold, raising it from NPR 500,000 to NPR 1,000,000 (from about USD 3,333 to about USD 6,667). In plain terms, the amount of annual income that an individual can earn before meaningful income tax begins to apply has been doubled. (The precise treatment of this first slab — whether it is fully tax-free or carries the low nominal social-security rate that has historically applied to the first band, and how the thresholds differ between single individuals and married couples — must be taken from the Finance Act 2083 and the IRD circulars; the headline is the doubling of the threshold to NPR 1,000,000.) This is a very large change. It means that a great many lower- and middle-income earners, whose total income falls at or below NPR 1,000,000, will now pay little or no income tax at all — and that everyone who earns more gets the benefit of having a larger bottom slice of their income shielded from meaningful tax.

The second change is to the **top marginal rate** — the rate on the highest slab of income. This budget *cuts* that top rate sharply, by ten percentage points, from 39 percent to 29 percent. (The 39 percent top rate had been reached through a combination of the highest ordinary slab and an additional surcharge applied to very high incomes; the new top rate of 29 percent represents a substantial reduction in the marginal rate faced by the highest earners. The exact structure of how the top rate is composed should be confirmed from the Finance Act and IRD circulars.) This means that the highest earners — those whose income reaches into the top slab — will now pay 29 percent rather than 39 percent on the topmost slice of their income.

Taken together, these two changes — doubling the exempt threshold and cutting the top rate by ten points — amount to what is plausibly the largest reset of personal income tax in Nepal in over a decade. The government presents this as broad-based relief: more money in the pockets of ordinary Nepalis, a lighter burden on the salaried middle class struggling with rising prices, and a more competitive tax regime that might encourage compliance and discourage the wealthy from finding ways to avoid tax. These are real arguments, and we will weigh them. But the headline "tax relief for all" conceals a more complicated distribution of who actually gains, and that distribution is where a careful citizen must look.

## Who actually benefits?

Here is the question the headline glosses over, and answering it requires exactly the marginal-versus-effective thinking we built above: when you doubled the exempt threshold and cut the top rate, *who gained the most*? The answer is more nuanced and more uneven than "everyone," and it has two parts that pull in different directions.

Consider first the **doubling of the exempt threshold**, from NPR 500,000 to NPR 1,000,000. Who does this help? On its face, it helps lower- and middle-income earners by shielding more of their income from tax. But here is the subtlety: in Nepal, as in many developing countries, a large share of the population already earned at or below the old threshold, or earned little above it, and therefore already paid little or no income tax. The personal income tax has historically been paid in meaningful amounts by a relatively small slice of the population — the formally employed and the better-off — while the majority, in agriculture, informal work, and lower-paid employment, fell at or below the threshold or escaped the formal tax net entirely. So doubling the threshold delivers its largest *proportional* benefit to those in the band between the old and new thresholds — roughly, middle earners who were paying some tax and now pay little — while delivering *no* benefit to the very large number who were already below the old threshold and paying nothing (you cannot relieve someone of a tax they did not pay), and delivering a *modest* benefit, in proportion to their income, to high earners (who get a larger bottom slice shielded, but for whom that shielded slice is a small fraction of their total income). The doubled threshold is genuine relief for the lower-middle and middle, but it does nothing for the poorest-taxed (who paid nothing already) and relatively little, proportionally, for the richest.

Now consider the **cut in the top rate**, from 39 to 29 percent. Who does *this* help? By definition, it helps *only* those whose income reaches into the top slab — that is, the *highest* earners, the wealthy. A person of modest or middle income never reaches the top slab, so a cut in the top rate does nothing whatever for them; their income is all taxed in the lower slabs, which this change did not touch. The benefit of the top-rate cut flows entirely to the high earners whose income extends into that topmost band, and the higher their income, the more of it sits in that band and the more they save from the ten-point cut. So while the doubled threshold spreads its benefit across the lower-middle and middle (and a little to everyone above), the top-rate cut delivers its benefit *exclusively* to the wealthy.

Put the two changes together and the honest distribution emerges. The poorest, who paid no income tax, gain nothing from either change. The lower-middle and middle earners gain real relief from the doubled threshold, but nothing from the top-rate cut. And the highest earners gain from *both* — a little from the doubled threshold and, more significantly, from the top-rate cut that is theirs alone — so that, in absolute rupee terms, the largest single beneficiaries of this package are the high earners, who receive the benefit of the threshold *and* the exclusive benefit of the top-rate cut. This is the reality behind "tax relief for all": the relief is real for the middle, absent for the poorest-taxed, and largest in absolute terms for the wealthiest. We are not saying this makes the policy wrong — there are arguments, which we will give, for cutting the top rate. We are saying that "relief for everyone" is a misleading description of a change whose largest absolute beneficiaries are the high earners, and that a citizen should see the actual distribution rather than the even-handed-sounding headline.

## The arguments on both sides

Having exposed the uneven distribution, fairness requires that we give the genuine arguments on both sides of these cuts, because reasonable people disagree and this series flatters no position, including the critical one.

The arguments *for* the cuts are real. The doubled threshold delivers meaningful relief to a salaried middle class that has been squeezed by years of rising prices, and putting money back in the pockets of ordinary earners is both popular and, arguably, a stimulus to consumption and to the economy. The top-rate cut, its defenders argue, makes Nepal's tax regime more competitive and less punitive at the top, which may *encourage compliance* — a lower rate gives high earners less incentive to evade or to shift income abroad, so that the government might collect a similar amount at a lower rate by capturing income that previously escaped — and may discourage the talented and the wealthy from leaving or from hiding their earnings. There is also a simplicity-and-incentive argument: lower, simpler rates can improve the efficiency of the tax system and the incentive to work, earn, and invest. And for a new government promising to energise the economy and reward enterprise, a visible tax cut is a statement of intent. None of these arguments is frivolous.

The arguments *against* are equally real. The most obvious is *cost*: tax cuts reduce revenue, and this budget is already borrowing nearly a third of what it spends; cutting income tax while running a large deficit means either that the gap must be filled by yet more borrowing, or that the revenue must be made up elsewhere — quite possibly through consumption taxes like VAT, which (as we will see in the next post) fall hardest on the poor. So an income-tax cut that benefits the middle and the wealthy may, in effect, be partly paid for by consumption taxes that burden everyone including the poor — a regressive shift hidden inside a "relief" package. The second argument is *equity*: cutting the *top* rate, in particular, directs benefit to those who need it least, widening the gap between the after-tax incomes of the rich and everyone else, at a time when inequality is itself a source of the discontent that helped bring this government to power. The third is *the optimism of the compliance argument*: the claim that a lower top rate will pay for itself by improving compliance is frequently overstated, and if it does not materialise, the cut simply loses revenue. And the fourth is *priorities*: in a country with enormous unmet needs in health, education, and infrastructure, every rupee of revenue forgone to a tax cut is a rupee not available for those needs, and one may reasonably ask whether cutting the top rate for high earners is the best use of scarce fiscal room.

The honest verdict, as so often in this series, is that this is a genuine trade-off with real considerations on both sides, and that the right judgement depends on values — on how one weighs relief for the middle against the cost in revenue, the incentive arguments against the equity concerns, the benefit to high earners against the unmet needs elsewhere. What a citizen should *not* do is accept the simple framing of "tax relief for all" without seeing the uneven distribution, the revenue cost in a deficit budget, the possibility that the cut is partly paid for by regressive consumption taxes, and the concentration of the top-rate benefit among the wealthy. See all of that, and you can form your own judgement; see only the headline, and you have been told a comforting half-truth.

## The capital gains change

This budget makes one further income-tax-related change worth noting briefly, because it affects a particular and growing group of Nepalis: the treatment of capital gains on listed securities. A *capital gain* is the profit you make when you sell something for more than you paid for it — in this case, shares listed on the stock exchange. This budget makes the tax on gains from selling listed securities *final* — meaning, broadly, that the tax withheld at the point of sale is treated as the final tax on that gain, rather than the gain having to be carried into a separate end-of-year tax settlement. The intended effect is simplification and certainty for the growing number of Nepalis who invest in the stock market: the tax is settled at the point of transaction, and the investor need not reconcile it separately later. As with the other changes, the precise mechanics and rates should be confirmed from the Finance Act 2083 and IRD circulars. The broad significance is that it reflects this government's general direction — simplifying taxes and easing the burden on investors and the better-off — and it is part of the same package whose distributional tilt we have just examined: the people who own significant amounts of listed shares are, on the whole, not the poorest Nepalis.

## A worked example: three taxpayers

To make the uneven distribution concrete, let us follow three different earners through these changes — directionally, since the exact savings depend on the slab table we have declined to invent. The three are deliberately chosen to sit at different points: one below the new threshold, one in the middle, and one high earner.

Consider first **Maya**, who earns about NPR 800,000 a year (around USD 5,333) — a respectable lower-middle income. Under the old system, with the threshold at NPR 500,000, the portion of Maya's income above NPR 500,000 fell into the taxable slabs and she paid some income tax on it. Under the new system, with the threshold doubled to NPR 1,000,000, Maya's entire income now falls at or below the threshold, so she pays little or no income tax at all. Maya is a clear winner from the doubled threshold: her income-tax burden falls substantially, perhaps to almost nothing. The top-rate cut, however, does nothing for her, because her income never came close to the top slab.

Consider next **Bibek**, who earns about NPR 2,000,000 a year (around USD 13,333) — a solidly upper-middle income, perhaps a senior professional. Bibek benefits from the doubled threshold, because a larger bottom slice of his income — now the first NPR 1,000,000 rather than the first NPR 500,000 — is shielded from meaningful tax, reducing his bill. But Bibek's income does not reach the very top slab either, so the top-rate cut likely does little or nothing for him. Bibek gains real relief, mainly from the threshold, in proportion smaller than Maya's relative to his income but larger in absolute rupees.

Consider finally **Anjali**, who earns about NPR 6,000,000 a year (around USD 40,000) — a high income, perhaps a successful business owner or top executive. Anjali benefits from *both* changes. Like everyone, she gets the larger shielded bottom slice from the doubled threshold. But uniquely among our three, a significant portion of her income reaches into the top slab — and so she benefits substantially from the cut in the top rate from 39 to 29 percent, a ten-point reduction on a large slice of her income that neither Maya nor Bibek receives at all. In absolute rupee terms, Anjali's tax saving is likely the largest of the three, because she captures the threshold benefit *and* the exclusive top-rate benefit on a substantial amount of income.

The three cases reveal the distribution precisely. Maya (lower-middle) gains from the threshold and is freed of tax almost entirely — large proportional relief. Bibek (upper-middle) gains moderately from the threshold. Anjali (high earner) gains from both and saves the most in absolute terms. And a fourth person we have not named — a subsistence farmer or informal worker earning well below NPR 500,000, who paid no income tax under either system — gains *nothing*, because there was no income tax to relieve. That is the true shape of "tax relief for all": real and welcome for the salaried middle like Maya and Bibek, largest in absolute terms for high earners like Anjali, and entirely absent for the poorest, who were outside the income-tax net to begin with. (The exact rupee savings for each would require the slab table from the Finance Act 2083; the *direction and relative magnitude* are what matter and what the example shows.)

## For the technically minded: computing the change, honestly

For readers who work with numbers, here is how one would *correctly* compute the effect of these changes — and, just as importantly, what one must *not* do, namely invent the slab rates.

```text
# CORRECT approach: load the ACTUAL slabs from the Finance Act 2083 / IRD circulars.
# Do NOT hardcode guessed rates -- the bands, the first-slab social-security treatment,
# and the single-vs-couple thresholds are specific and authoritative.

def income_tax(income, slabs):
    # slabs = ordered list of (lower_bound, upper_bound, rate) from the OFFICIAL source
    tax = 0
    for (low, high, rate) in slabs:
        if income > low:
            taxable_in_slab = min(income, high) - low   # only the portion within this slab
            tax += taxable_in_slab * rate                # each slab taxed at its OWN rate
    return tax

# The change this budget makes, in terms of the inputs:
#   - exemption threshold (bottom of the first taxable slab): 500,000 -> 1,000,000
#   - top marginal rate (rate of the highest slab):           39%     -> 29%

old_tax = income_tax(income, slabs_old)   # slabs_old from FY 2082/83 official tables
new_tax = income_tax(income, slabs_new)   # slabs_new from Finance Act 2083 official tables
saving  = old_tax - new_tax

# Effective rate (what is ACTUALLY paid as a share of total income) -- the quiet truth:
effective_rate_old = old_tax / income
effective_rate_new = new_tax / income
# Note: effective_rate is always well BELOW the marginal (top-slab) rate,
# because lower slabs were taxed at lower rates. Confusing the two is the deception below.
```

The code embodies two disciplines this post has stressed. First, *tax is computed slab by slab*, each portion at its own rate — which is why the effective rate (total tax divided by total income) is always lower than the marginal rate (the top slab's rate), and why anyone who multiplies someone's whole income by the top rate has badly overestimated the tax. Second, and crucially, *the slab rates must come from the official source*, not from memory or guesswork — the function takes the slabs as an input precisely so that it cannot be run on invented numbers. A developer or auditor who wants to know the real effect of these changes loads the genuine slab tables from the Finance Act 2083 and the IRD circulars and runs the calculation; anyone who hardcodes plausible-looking rates and presents the result as fact has committed exactly the kind of false precision this series refuses. The honest answer to "how much will I save?" is "load the real slabs and compute it" — and the honest writer says so rather than inventing a number.

## How budgets deceive: relief for everyone

The technique of deception for this post is one of the most common in all of tax politics, and it works precisely by exploiting the marginal-versus-effective confusion and the uneven distribution we have uncovered: **the "relief for everyone" framing** — presenting a tax cut whose benefits are concentrated as though they were evenly shared.

Here is the move. A tax cut is announced as broad-based relief — "putting money back in people's pockets," "easing the burden on hardworking citizens," "tax relief for all." The framing invites every listener to imagine themselves benefiting equally, and to feel gratitude accordingly. But, as we have seen, the benefits of a tax change are almost never evenly distributed; they depend entirely on *where in the income distribution each change bites*. A higher exempt threshold helps those in the band between the old and new thresholds, does nothing for those who were already below the old threshold, and gives only a proportionally small benefit to the very rich. A cut in the *top* rate helps *only* the high earners whose income reaches the top slab, and nothing for anyone below. So a package that doubles the threshold *and* cuts the top rate delivers its benefits very unevenly — real relief for the middle, nothing for the poorest-taxed, and the largest absolute gains for the wealthy — while being marketed as relief for all. The deception is not in any single false statement; it is in the *framing* that obscures the distribution, encouraging everyone to feel they have gained equally when in fact the gains are concentrated, and in particular when the most expensive component (the top-rate cut) benefits only the rich while being wrapped in the language of universal relief.

The defence is to *decompose every tax change by who it actually affects*, using the slab thinking this post has built. Whenever a tax cut is announced, ask of each component: *where does this bite, and therefore who benefits?* A change to the exempt threshold — who is in the affected band, and who is below it and unaffected? A change to the top rate — only the high earners reach the top slab, so the benefit is theirs alone. A new consumption tax to "pay for" the cut — that falls on everyone including the poor, so the net effect may be regressive even though the headline was "relief." Then ask the distributional questions directly: who gains the most in absolute rupees, who gains nothing, and is the cut partly paid for by taxes that fall on those who did not benefit? A citizen who decomposes a tax change this way — rather than accepting the warm, universal framing — can see its true shape: who really wins, who really pays, and whether "relief for everyone" is an accurate description or a comforting disguise for a transfer toward the better-off. The defence, in a phrase, is to remember that in a progressive, slab-based system, *no* significant tax change affects everyone equally, and that the word "everyone" in a tax-cut announcement is almost always doing concealing work. Find out where each change bites, and the concealment fails.

## Where we go from here

We have made income tax human. You now know what income tax is and how it differs from consumption taxes; how the slab system works, so that each portion of income is taxed at its own rate and the rates climb only as you move up; and the crucial difference between the marginal rate (on your topmost slice) and the effective rate (the share of your whole income you actually pay), which almost everyone confuses. You know exactly what this budget changed — doubling the exempt threshold from NPR 500,000 to NPR 1,000,000 and cutting the top rate from 39 to 29 percent — and, far more importantly, who actually benefits: real relief for the salaried middle, nothing for the poorest-taxed, and the largest absolute gains for high earners, with the top-rate cut benefiting the wealthy alone. You have heard the genuine arguments on both sides, and you have met the deception of "relief for everyone" and the discipline of decomposing every tax change by who it actually affects.

In Part 11 we turn to the taxes you pay without even noticing — value-added tax, customs, and excise — the consumption taxes that fund the largest share of the government's revenue and that, unlike income tax, fall on rich and poor alike when they spend. We will see how these taxes work, what this budget changed (simplifying customs, abolishing excise on many goods, adding new VAT measures and a new Green Tax), and why consumption taxes are regressive in a way that matters enormously for who really bears the cost of government — including, perhaps, the cost of the income-tax cut we have just examined. The first tax is understood. Now we follow the money you spend.
