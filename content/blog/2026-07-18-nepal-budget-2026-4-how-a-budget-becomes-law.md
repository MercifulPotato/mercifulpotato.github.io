There is a sentence that we have repeated at the foot of every post in this series, and in this post we finally earn it in full. The sentence is this: a figure that has been *announced* in a budget is not the same as one that has been *enacted* into law, which is not the same as money that has actually been *spent* and turned into something real. Most confusion about budgets, and a great deal of the disappointment citizens feel when promises evaporate, comes from collapsing those three stages into one — from hearing an announcement and believing the thing is done.

This post takes the budget out of the Finance Minister's mouth and follows it, step by step, through the machinery that turns a speech into binding law, and the further machinery that is supposed to turn that law into roads, salaries, and services. By the end you will understand exactly what happens between Jestha 15, when the budget is read aloud, and the moment — months or years later, if ever — when the money has actually done something in the world. And you will see why the gap between those moments is where so much of the truth about a budget hides.

## One speech, several documents

When people say "the budget," they usually mean the **budget speech** — the long statement the Finance Minister reads to Parliament on Jestha 15, laying out the government's plans, its priorities, its headline numbers, and its many announcements. The speech is the part that makes the news. It is eloquent, it is full of vision, and it is, in the strict legal sense, *not the law*. The speech is a *statement of intent*. It commits the government to nothing by itself. To turn its intentions into authority that officials can actually act on, the government must pass *legislation* — actual bills that become actual acts. And the budget is not one bill but a small family of them, each doing a different job. Understanding that family is the key that unlocks everything else.

The two most important members of the family are the Appropriation Bill and the Finance Bill. They are easy to confuse because they arrive together and both concern money, but they do opposite halves of the job, and keeping them distinct is essential.

The **Appropriation Bill** is about *spending*. The word "appropriation" means setting money aside for a specific purpose by legal authority. The government may not spend money out of the national treasury — the pool of public funds, formally the Consolidated Fund — unless Parliament has passed a law authorising that spending. The Appropriation Bill is that law. It is, in effect, the legal permission slip for every rupee the government intends to spend across all its ministries and programmes for the coming year. The detailed line-by-line breakdown of those spending authorisations — which ministry gets how much, for what — is published in a thick volume traditionally called the **Red Book** (its formal name is the Estimates of Expenditure). When you want to know what a budget *actually authorises* the government to spend, as opposed to what the speech *talks about*, the Red Book and the Appropriation Bill are where the binding numbers live.

The **Finance Bill** is about *raising* money — chiefly, about taxes. Every change to how the government taxes its citizens and businesses — the income tax thresholds, the customs duties, the value-added tax rules, the excise rates, the new levies — must be given legal force, and the Finance Bill is the vehicle that does it. For this budget it is called the **Finance Bill 2083**, after the Bikram Sambat year, and once it has passed Parliament and been authenticated it becomes the **Finance Act 2083**. This distinction between "Bill" and "Act" is not pedantry; it is the line between proposal and law. A Finance *Bill* is a draft the government wants Parliament to approve. A Finance *Act* is the approved, binding statute. When the budget speech announces, say, that the income tax exemption will double, that announcement has no legal force whatsoever until the Finance Bill containing it becomes the Finance Act. Until then it is a stated intention that Parliament could, in principle, amend or reject.

There are other members of the family too, less famous but real. Because the budget, as we saw, does not balance on revenue and grants alone — it relies on borrowing — there must also be legal authority *to borrow*. Separate legislation authorises the government to raise debt: to borrow domestically by issuing government bonds and bills, and to take on foreign loans and offer the guarantees that sometimes accompany them. These borrowing authorities are granted through their own acts, so that the government's power to put the country into debt, like its power to tax and to spend, rests on the consent of the people's representatives rather than on the executive's own say-so. The principle is consistent throughout: every way the government touches public money — taxing it, spending it, borrowing it — requires a law, and the budget is the moment all those laws are proposed at once.

## The journey through Parliament

So the Finance Minister has read the speech, and tabled the bills — the Appropriation Bill, the Finance Bill, and the rest. What happens next? The bills must travel through Parliament, and the journey has recognisable stages, each of which is a real opportunity for the plan to be examined, changed, or — at least in principle — stopped.

First comes *deliberation and debate*. The members of the House of Representatives discuss the budget: its overall direction, its priorities, its specific provisions. In a healthy parliament this is where the government is forced to defend its choices, where the opposition probes the weak points, and where the assumptions behind the numbers are dragged into the light. As we discussed in the previous post, the quality of this scrutiny depends heavily on the strength of the opposition — and with the opposition reduced to a fraction of the chamber after the March 2026 landslide, the scrutiny this budget faces inside Parliament is weaker than it would be in a more balanced house. This is precisely why scrutiny from outside Parliament — from journalists, analysts, professional bodies, and engaged citizens — matters more than usual this year.

Second comes *amendment*. Members may propose changes to the bills. In practice, a government with a secure majority can pass its budget largely in the shape it wants, accepting or rejecting proposed amendments as it sees fit; a government dependent on coalition partners must bargain, and the budget that emerges can differ meaningfully from the one that went in. Either way, the version that finally passes is the version that has legal force, and it may not be identical to the version announced in the speech. A careful analyst notes the announced figures but waits for the passed ones, because the gap between them is real.

Third comes *passage* — the formal votes by which the House of Representatives approves the bills. Here the special status of money bills, which we met in the previous post, matters. The core budget legislation is treated as money legislation, which is the constitutional province of the directly elected House of Representatives; the National Assembly may deliberate and offer suggestions within a defined period, but it cannot block the lower house on the public finances. So the decisive votes are those of the House of Representatives, and a government commanding a majority there — as this one emphatically does — can carry them.

Fourth and finally comes *authentication*. Once Parliament has passed the bills, they go to the **President**, the ceremonial head of state we met in the previous post, for formal authentication or certification. This is the President's constitutional role in lawmaking: not to govern, not to set policy, but to perform the formal act that turns a bill duly passed by Parliament into a binding law of the land. When the President authenticates the Finance Bill, it becomes the Finance Act; when the President authenticates the Appropriation Bill, the spending authority becomes legally live. Authentication is the moment the proposal finally, fully becomes the law. Before it, everything is intention and process; after it, the government has the legal authority it has been seeking since Jestha 15.

Notice what this whole journey means for our central distinction. The *announcement* happens in the speech, at the very start. The *enactment* happens only at the end of the parliamentary journey, with authentication, after debate and amendment and votes. Weeks can separate the two, and the enacted version can differ from the announced one. Anyone who treats the speech as if it were the law has skipped the entire journey we have just described — and skipped the possibility that the journey changed the destination.

## From law to action: the longer, harder road

You might think that once the budget is enacted into law, the story is essentially over — the money is authorised, so surely it now flows. This is the most important place where intuition fails, and where the third stage of our distinction, *implementation*, asserts itself. Enacting a budget grants the *authority* to tax, spend, and borrow. It does not, by itself, *cause* the taxing, spending, and building to happen. Between the law and the reality lies a second machinery, and it is in this machinery that a great many announcements quietly die.

Consider the tax side first. The Finance Act changes the rules — say, it alters an income tax threshold or a customs duty. But the people who actually collect taxes are officials in the tax administration, principally the **Inland Revenue Department**, which handles internal taxes like income tax and value-added tax, and the customs administration at the borders. For these officials to apply the new rules correctly and consistently, the high-level statutory change usually has to be translated into detailed operational instructions — **circulars** and directives that spell out exactly how the new provision works in practice, what forms to use, how to handle edge cases, from what date it applies. This is why, when the budget speech announces a tax change, the *precise* details — the exact bracket structure, the exact procedure — often cannot be stated with full confidence until the Inland Revenue Department issues its circulars. The announcement gives the headline; the circular gives the working rule. A change can be announced, and even enacted, and still not be fully operational until the administrative guidance lands. This is also why this series, when it reaches the tax posts, will repeatedly say that certain specifics must come from the Inland Revenue Department's circulars rather than from the speech — because that is genuinely where the operational truth is settled, and inventing those details from the headline would be exactly the kind of false precision we have warned against.

Now consider the spending side, which is where the gap is widest and most consequential. The Appropriation Act authorises a ministry to spend a certain sum on a certain programme. But authorisation is not action. Before money becomes a built road or a staffed clinic, a long chain of steps must occur: the funds must actually be released to the spending unit through the government's financial management system; the project must be designed and approved in detail; the work must be advertised and put out to competitive tender; contractors must bid and a contract must be awarded; any land required must be acquired; and only then can building begin — and, as we saw in the post on the calendar, building in Nepal must contend with the monsoon and with chronically slow procedures. The body that manages the government's accounts and the release and recording of funds is the **Financial Comptroller General Office**, which sits within the Ministry of Finance and keeps the running record of what has actually been spent, as opposed to what was merely authorised. It is the Financial Comptroller General Office's actual-expenditure data, not the budget's authorised figures, that tells you whether the money truly moved. And year after year, as we will document in detail later in the series, that data reveals the same pattern: a large share of the authorised capital budget — the money for building things — going unspent, the authority lapsing unused at the year's end. The law gave permission to build. The machinery failed to build. Both things are true at once, and only the second one leaves a road behind.

## The watchdogs: who checks that the money was used properly

A budget cycle does not end when the money is spent. There is a final stage, often invisible to the public, in which independent bodies examine whether the money was spent legally, properly, and on what it was meant for. This is *oversight*, and it is the part of the system that, in principle, keeps the whole thing honest. Three institutions matter most, and you should know their names.

The first is the **Office of the Auditor General**. This is a constitutional body — meaning its existence and independence are guaranteed by the constitution itself, so that it cannot easily be neutered by a government it is supposed to be checking — whose job is to audit the accounts of the government: to examine, after the fact, whether public money was spent in accordance with the law and the authorisations, to identify irregularities, waste, and amounts spent without proper documentation or authority, and to report all of this publicly. The Auditor General's annual report is one of the most important and least read documents in Nepali public life. It is where you discover not what the government *said* it would do with the money, but what an independent examiner found it *actually* did — including, very often, large sums flagged as irregular. We will draw on the kinds of findings such audits produce when we examine the budget's credibility in a later post.

The second is the **Financial Comptroller General Office**, which we have already met as the keeper of the government's accounts and the releaser and recorder of funds. While its primary role is operational rather than investigative, it is the source of the authoritative record of actual expenditure against authorisation — the data that lets anyone, including auditors, citizens, and series like this one, compare what was budgeted with what was spent. When we say a particular capital budget went substantially unspent, it is the Financial Comptroller General Office's records that make that statement checkable rather than rhetorical.

The third is the **Public Accounts Committee** of Parliament. A parliamentary committee is a smaller group of members charged with examining a particular area in detail, away from the full chamber. The Public Accounts Committee is the committee that scrutinises public spending and, crucially, examines the Auditor General's findings, summoning officials to explain irregularities and pressing for accountability and recovery. It is the parliamentary mechanism through which the auditors' findings are supposed to translate into consequences. How vigorously it does so depends, once again, on the political will and composition of the Parliament of the day — and a Parliament dominated by a single party may scrutinise its own government's spending less fiercely than a more balanced one would. The institution exists; its bite varies.

Together these bodies are meant to close the loop: the budget authorises, the government spends, the Financial Comptroller General Office records, the Auditor General examines, and the Public Accounts Committee holds to account, after which the next year's budget is written with — in theory — the lessons of the last one in mind. In practice the loop is leaky at several points, and much of what the auditors flag never results in real consequences. But knowing that the loop exists, and where it leaks, is what lets you ask the right questions. A citizen who knows to look for the Auditor General's report on how last year's money was actually used is a citizen who cannot be satisfied with this year's speech about how next year's money will be used.

## Where to find the real documents

A recurring theme of this series is that you do not have to take anyone's word, including ours: the primary documents exist and are, increasingly, public. It is worth knowing where they live, because the ability to go to the source is the ultimate defence against being misled.

The budget speech, the Red Book of detailed expenditure estimates, and the supporting budget documents are published by the **Ministry of Finance**, accessible through its website at mof.gov.np, and Nepal has in recent years developed online budget portals intended to make this information more navigable. Enacted laws, including the Finance Act and the Appropriation Act once authenticated, are published officially — historically in the Nepal Gazette, the government's official record of laws. The Inland Revenue Department publishes its tax circulars and directives, which contain the operational detail of tax changes, through its own channels. The actual expenditure data — what was really spent against what was authorised — flows from the Financial Comptroller General Office. And the **Office of the Auditor General** publishes its annual audit reports, which are the closest thing to an independent verdict on how public money was truly handled. None of these documents is light reading, and some are more accessible than others, but they are the bedrock. Everything in this series is meant to be checkable against them, and you are not only permitted but encouraged to check.

## A worked example: the announcement that lapsed

Let us make the announced-enacted-implemented distinction vivid with a composite example of a kind that recurs in every budget cycle.

Suppose the budget speech announces, to applause, a popular new programme — say, the construction of a set of new local hospitals in underserved districts, with a specific sum attached. The announcement is genuine; the government means it; the sum appears in the Red Book; the Appropriation Act, once passed, makes the spending legally authorised. By every measure of *announcement* and *enactment*, the programme is real. A citizen reading the news, or hearing a minister speak, would reasonably conclude that the hospitals are coming.

Now follow the money into the machinery of *implementation*, and watch where it can fail. Perhaps the detailed designs for the hospitals are not ready, so months pass before anything can be tendered. Perhaps the land for several sites has not been acquired, and acquiring land in Nepal can become tangled in disputes and delays that consume an entire fiscal year. Perhaps the procurement process — advertising, bidding, awarding — stalls or is challenged. Perhaps the funds, though authorised, are released late through the financial system. Perhaps the monsoon swallows the building season. And so the fiscal year ends with the hospitals unbuilt, much of the authorised money unspent, and the authority lapsing. The announcement was real. The law was real. The hospitals are not. Next year, the very same hospitals may be announced again, in a new speech, with a new sum, beginning the cycle afresh — which is why a careful observer learns to feel a particular suspicion when a "new" announcement closely resembles last year's.

Notice that at no single point did anyone necessarily lie. The government genuinely intended the hospitals; Parliament genuinely authorised them; the failure lived in the slow, unglamorous machinery between authorisation and action. This is the ordinary way that ambitious budgets become disappointing realities — not through a dramatic act of deception, but through the quiet, cumulative friction of implementation. And it is exactly why the three-stage distinction is the single most useful idea in this entire series. An announcement tells you what a government hopes. An enactment tells you what it has legal permission to do. Only the actual-expenditure record, examined by the auditors, tells you what it did. When you keep those three apart, you stop being the audience for a speech and start being a judge of a record.

## For the technically minded: the budget as a state machine

For readers who think in terms of systems and processes, the life of a budget provision is usefully modelled as a *state machine* — an object that moves through a defined sequence of states, where reaching the final state is what actually matters and where it can stall or fail at any transition. Almost every confusion about budgets comes from treating an item in an early state as though it had reached the final one. Here is the model, expressed as plain pseudo-logic.

```text
states = [ANNOUNCED, TABLED, AMENDED, PASSED, AUTHENTICATED, FUNDS_RELEASED, IMPLEMENTED, AUDITED]

transition(provision):
    ANNOUNCED      -> TABLED          # appears as a bill before Parliament
    TABLED         -> AMENDED         # debated; may be changed or removed
    AMENDED        -> PASSED          # voted through by the House of Representatives
    PASSED         -> AUTHENTICATED   # certified by the President -> now it is LAW
    AUTHENTICATED  -> FUNDS_RELEASED  # money actually released through the financial system
    FUNDS_RELEASED -> IMPLEMENTED     # tendered, contracted, built, delivered
    IMPLEMENTED    -> AUDITED         # examined after the fact by the Auditor General

    # A provision can STALL or FAIL at any transition:
    #   - rejected or dropped during AMENDED
    #   - funds never released, or released too late to use
    #   - procurement, land acquisition, or monsoon block IMPLEMENTED
    # An item stuck before IMPLEMENTED has changed nothing in the real world,
    # no matter how prominently it featured in the ANNOUNCED state.

verdict(provision):
    if provision.state == ANNOUNCED:      return "a hope"
    if provision.state == AUTHENTICATED:  return "a legal authority, not yet an outcome"
    if provision.state == IMPLEMENTED:    return "a real thing that happened"
    if provision.state == AUDITED:        return "a real thing, now checkable for how well it was done"
```

The discipline this model teaches is simple and powerful: before you credit a government with anything, ask *what state is this provision actually in?* The budget speech presents a long list of items in the `ANNOUNCED` state and invites you to feel as though they were `IMPLEMENTED`. The whole art of reading a budget honestly is refusing that invitation — tracking each provision to the state it has genuinely reached, and reserving real credit for the things that reached the end of the chain. A government should be judged by how many of its provisions reach `IMPLEMENTED` and survive `AUDITED`, not by how many it can place in `ANNOUNCED`. Placing things in `ANNOUNCED` is the easiest thing a government does. It is also, not coincidentally, the part that makes the news.

## How budgets deceive: announcement as achievement

The technique of deception for this post is the natural companion of everything above, and it is perhaps the most pervasive in all of politics: **announcement as achievement** — the presentation of a promise as though it were a delivery, a plan as though it were a result.

The move works because of a quirk of how we process news and speeches. When a government announces a programme with confidence and detail — a sum, a target, a name — our minds tend to file it as a thing that is *happening*, even though, in the state-machine terms above, it has merely entered the `ANNOUNCED` state and may never leave it. The announcement is concrete and vivid and now; the long, dull, failure-prone road to actual implementation is abstract and distant and easy to forget. So a government can accumulate political credit simply by announcing, and the credit is banked long before the difficult work of delivery is even attempted — and often the public's attention has moved on by the time the delivery fails to materialise. A government that is good at announcing and bad at delivering can, for a surprisingly long time, be mistaken for a government that is good at governing, because the gap between announcement and delivery plays out over years while the announcements arrive every season.

The defence is the three-stage distinction made into a habit. Whenever you encounter a government claim about what it is "doing" for the country, ask which stage it has actually reached. Is this an *announcement* — a stated intention, words in a speech? Is it *enacted* — actually passed into law, with the authority to proceed? Is it *implemented* — money actually spent, the thing actually built or the service actually delivered, verifiable in the expenditure record and the world? And for things claimed to be done, ask the further question the auditors ask: was it done *properly*, or would an independent examination find irregularities? Most political communication wants you to stop at the first stage and feel the satisfaction appropriate to the third. The disciplined citizen keeps the stages apart and assigns credit accordingly — generous credit for things genuinely implemented and well-executed, provisional interest in things merely enacted, and nothing more than polite attention for things merely announced. Apply this consistently and you become very hard to impress with a speech, which is exactly the right way to be hard to fool.

## Where we go from here

We have followed the budget from the Finance Minister's mouth all the way to the auditor's report. You now understand that "the budget" is not one document but a family — the speech that states intent, the Appropriation Bill that authorises spending and whose details live in the Red Book, the Finance Bill that becomes the Finance Act and gives legal force to tax changes, and the separate authorities to borrow. You understand the parliamentary journey of debate, amendment, passage, and Presidential authentication that turns a bill into law, and the special weight of the House of Representatives over money. You understand the second, harder machinery of implementation — fund release, circulars, procurement, construction — in which authority so often fails to become action. You know the watchdogs: the Auditor General, the Financial Comptroller General Office, the Public Accounts Committee. And you have, now fully earned, the central distinction of this entire series — announced, enacted, implemented — together with the state-machine discipline and the announcement-as-achievement defence that make it usable.

We have now spent four posts building foundations: what a budget is, when and why it arrives, who writes it, and how it becomes law and action. The scaffolding is complete. In Part 5 we climb onto it and begin the real work of analysis, starting with the number that towers over everything else — the headline total of NPR 2,124.34 billion, about USD 14.16 billion. We will dissect what "the largest budget in history" actually means in real terms, why a record budget can be a hollow one, and how to size up any giant national number against the things that give it meaning: inflation, the previous year's true figures, the population, and the size of the economy itself. The foundations are laid. Now we build.

## A Note on Numbers and Assumptions

The recurring monetary figures in this post — the total budget of NPR 2,124.34 billion (about USD 14.16 billion) — use our single assumed exchange rate of **1 US dollar = 150 Nepalese rupees**, an approximation close to the Nepal Rastra Bank reference rate in mid-2026. The Nepalese rupee is pegged to the Indian rupee at 1.60 and drifts daily, so treat dollar figures as intuition rather than accounting. All rupee figures are nominal unless stated otherwise.

This post describes the general structure of Nepal's budget legislation and oversight as established by the constitution and standard practice; the exact sequence and timing of parliamentary stages can vary, and the names and web addresses of institutions should be confirmed against their official sources. Where this series later relies on the operational detail of tax changes, those details are drawn from the Inland Revenue Department's circulars rather than the budget speech, because that is where they are authoritatively settled.

And the assumption this entire post exists to establish, now stated for the last time as a discovery rather than a warning: announced is not enacted, and enacted is not implemented. A speech states a hope; a law grants an authority; only the spending record, examined by the auditors, shows a result. Keep those three apart, and the rest of this series — and the rest of the budgets you will read for the rest of your life — will be far harder to spin past you.
